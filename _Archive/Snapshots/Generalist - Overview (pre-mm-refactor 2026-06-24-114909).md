- **The analysis in this vault is intended to capitalise on the perverse incentives and organisational deficiencies of traditional asset management firms**
	- **Investment process that has an overreliance on DCFs and quantitative tools**. 
		- Investors tend to rely on excel spreadsheets, comparable companies and traditional DCFs to anchor their thinking, and analysis. 
		- This is often due to the fact that in an institutional environment, communication of ideas need to go through a 'quantitative' translation layer to provide a foundation for discussion. Emphasis on qualitative inputs (which is what should be done) is considered too 'fluffy' and difficult to align IC members because each have their idiosyncratic priors.
		- The disciplined inversion is endgame / reverse DCF — rather than forecast forward, ask what the business must look like in 10 years to justify today's price, then evaluate whether that endgame is plausible. 
	- **Antimatching cyclicality for investment opportunity and capital inflows**.
		- Creates incentive distortion to be aggressive when markets are expensive and conservative when markets are cheap. 
		- At scale this dynamic is a major variable for the pervasiveness of the capital cycles.
	- **Information and decision preferences flows hierarchically and not on the basis of full transparency or first principles reasoning merit.**
		- Single CIO needing to make decisions on the recommendations and positions of multiple PMs / sector heads. In an environment dominated by rapid hiring / firing, bonus tied to book performance, and desire to placate idiosyncrasies of the CIO creates perverse incentive to distort information presented to CIO. CIO no longer relying on pure information but rather seeing through tinted lens.
		- Work at at he sector head / PM level often dominated by playing status games while only junior analysts actually has access to front line information whilst rest of the org structure receive information from junior analysts as second source information.
- **A first principles redesign of hedge fund structure would look something like this:**
	- At the source you would need to eliminate the perverse incentive of separation of management of the self's assets (skin in the game) and client assets (no skin in the game). 
	- The proposed structure would have at its head is a council of analyst x partners that co-owns the equity in the business and each run their own 'book' while a voting process maintains a 'public/client' book which is made up of individual components of each analyst x partners books. 
	- Partners get remunerated on the axis of fund inflows and public/client book performance which together make up firm profits. Some can focus on more sales role in bringing money, others can focus their time on analysis in generating returns. 
	- Each analyst x partner can bring their own junior resources (associates) as research assistants and pay them out of their own share of compensation. Under this model all partners are expected to be analysts in some way so the number of associates to analyst x partner ratio is low.
	- An IC for the public/client book would look like a 'pitch' from a analyst x partner to include a stock from his personal book into the public/client book (only if its a major weight and there is substantial research on it to a rotating 'portfolio manager / CIO' (selected on the basis of combination of best personal book returns heavily discounted by entire partner's selection of risk taken to achieve those returns), this selection is done annually on a rolling basis. Each partner has 'votes' ranked based on past performance of his personal book, to allocate in a IC vote as to what gets included. 
	- Partners that consistently generate last quartile performance lose their position in the business regardless of his FUM inflow potential. While partners that generate top quartile performance are promoted as PM and earn an extra incentive compensation based on the portfolio and his individual selection's performance subject to clawback.

**The case for a long term investment framework is more relevant than ever.** 
	- Quant and systematic trading strategies have wrung out all alpha in ultra short term (<1 months) and short to medium term (1-6 months) remains the competitive sweet spot for traditional L/S hedge funds. I do not believe it to be possible to consistently 'make money' in the traditional sense in these markets unless you're a successful multibillion dollar fund - they're where things have gotten the most competitive. 
	- Given concentration of trading volume in shorter term strategies, the markets capacity to assign value to longer term (3-5+ year) datapoints is weaker than ever. Alpha generation for the discerning value investor without access to expensive expert network or market data is in predicting long term outcomes, this actually has never been easier with the proliferation of mass market information about companies, traditional proprietary newsflow (in person conferences for example) has been opened up to the masses (available on AI summary or YouTube). 

- **The economy and markets is a complex dynamical system undergoing constant mutation with subsystems leading to emergent supersystems.** Dynamical systems converge and dissolve on unpredictable time horizons based on many economic schelling points which are then compounded by investor heuristics driving extreme flows in valuation despite little change in fundamental economic flows at the revenue / GMV level.
	- The hardest meta-question in every position is whether mean reversion or trend continuation applies — misapplying mean reversion to compounders is the single most expensive equity research mistake, misapplying trend continuation to cyclicals is the second. The lack of visibility in predicting industry schelling points leads to short term thinking and heavily discounting long term outlooks, leading to mispricing of duration where it genuinely exists or otherwise.
	- Reflexivity compounds the mispricing in high-growth equities: rising stock prices lower cost of capital, fund M&A, and attract talent via stock comp, so price feeds back into the fundamentals it is meant to discount, making traditional DCFs most wrong precisely when most needed.

- **Carlota Perez and theories of cycles of financial and production capital booms and busts.**
	- Perez's core duality, the part a pure S-curve read misses: financial capital (mobile, return-agnostic, funds the risky build-out) and production capital (tied to real output, harvests the productivity gains) lead in alternating phases of a ~50-60 year 'great surge'. Each surge runs four phases across two periods. Installation, where financial capital dominates: *irruption* (the new technology erupts while the old paradigm still rules), then *frenzy* (the speculative bubble — finance decouples from production and over-funds the new infrastructure). Then a crash and *turning point*: the bubble bursts and institutions and regulation recompose to channel the technology's potential. Deployment, where production capital dominates: *synergy* (the golden age of broad, productivity-led growth), then *maturity* (the paradigm exhausts and capital starts hunting the next revolution).
	- The underlying physical adoption curve that Perez bubbles oscillate around: invisible R&D, early adopters, chasm, mainstream, saturation. Investors consistently misread where on the curve they sit — extrapolating early-adopter growth as if it represented saturation behavior, or declaring saturation when an enabling technology has actually shifted the asymptote upward. The skill is dating where on the curve you are: pre-chasm (binary outcome distribution, high failure risk), mid-chasm (S-curve secured but timing uncertain — the highest-edge zone for long-term capital), and late mainstream (peak earnings, falling growth, mean-reversion regime).
	- The bubble is functional, not merely mania: the frenzy's over-investment is what funds the expensive, initially-unprofitable infrastructure — canal networks, railway gauges, electricity grids, fiber-optic backbones — that the golden age then exploits at near-zero marginal cost. The over-build and the crash are the toll for installing a new platform. This reframes 'bubble' from a pure short signal into a positioning question: the capacity laid down in the frenzy (dark fiber after 2000, hyperscale GPU and datacenter build now) becomes the cheap substrate for the next decade's application-layer winners — who are usually not the infrastructure builders that went bust funding it.
	- Five surges since 1771: the Industrial Revolution (1771), Steam and Railways (1829), Steel, Electricity and Heavy Engineering (1875), Oil, Autos and Mass Production (1908), and Information and Telecommunications (1971, dated to the Intel microprocessor). The live question for today's book: is AI a sixth surge in its irruption/frenzy phase — early, bubble-prone, over-building — or the late-deployment synergy of the same ICT surge that began in 1971? Perez herself argues the ICT golden age was stunted — the post-2000 recomposition never fully reined in financial capital, so the broad productivity boom was partly skipped and the turning point dragged across both the 2000 and 2008 crashes. Which reading is right dictates whether AI capex is early-cycle infrastructure to underwrite or late-cycle over-extension to fade. 

- **Underappreciation for corporate quality, culture, biocybernetic nature of corporate decision making.** 
	- The proliferation of the 'comparable companies' framework, although much more valid historically where each industry and subindustry had a multitude of companies, creates a perception that all corporations are can be reflected in financial metrics, and anything qualitative that cannot be measured doesnt actually exist.
	- Corporations are biocybernetic lifeforms where internal power structure, communication flows patterns and incentive design with respect to rewarding upwards mobility and compensation lead to decision making styles and competitive/customer information processing competencies that differ substantially from firm to firm.
	- A firm with high quality culture tend to be where decision making is meritocratic, beureucracy and headcount needed to maintain internal coherence is low, the degree of conflict within the organisational power structure is limited, and the prevalence of corporate cliques is reduced.
	- A firm with good corporate governance is where the executive team understands capital allocation and understand the tradeoff between buybacks, internal investment and external investment hurdle rates. It is also where waste is reduced and interal organisational fraud due to power accumulation is kept at a minimum. 
	- A firm with good executive management is where the C-suite understands its primary purpose is enabling long term strategy, in growing new markets, and outmaneuvering competition. It is where everyday management is relegate do subordinates and the firm can take a long term perspective on decision making.

- **Pricing power, competition and software-like monopoly businesses.** 
	- Low incremental cost to serve, capex (software development + sales) determines investment, extremely high switching cost, often a new product category so there are no historical sets of incumbents that lead to a market having multiple competitors. 
	- Extremely high barriers to entry both in customer acquisition and software development (less so nowadays). Leads to often monopoly or oligopoly dynamics where there is effectively close to infinite pricing power and ROIC. Investment profile generally leads to winner take all dynamics. 
	- The defensibility itself comes from distinguishable network effect types, each with different durability: direct (each user makes the network more valuable to other users — social, comms), indirect / two-sided (marketplaces, OS — more buyers attract more sellers), data network effects (utility genuinely increases in data scale — search, ad targeting), scale economies (often mislabeled as networks), and tribal / social-capital effects. Direct networks succumb to bundling (Slack → Teams). 
	- Two-sided marketplaces succumb to disintermediation (Airbnb host loyalty). Most claimed 'data network effects' do not actually qualify — the data utility must be genuinely increasing in scale

- **Return on Incremental Invested Capital (ROIIC) x runway of capital deployment opportunities is the most important important variable in investing** 
	- How to estimate it on a going forward basis -> Return on incremental invested capital vs. incremental capital deployed. Measurement tells you what happened; capital allocation analysis tells you what will happen. 
	- The mining framework for corporations, existing mine + pipeline of new mines (capex of new mines x return profile x certainty). Unlike a mining company the pipeline of new mines are internal and company specific (vs. mining companies where its all you can bid). The key delta for long term investor is investing in companies with a good pipeline of new mines when they are not being priced, or priced at negative value.

- **Most important job of senior management is capital allocation, and most 'operators' have a perverse incentive to invest poorly**
	- The CEO's real job — and the one most CEOs are untrained for, having been promoted from operating roles — is choosing the mix across five levers: organic reinvestment, M&A, dividends, buybacks, debt paydown. The optimal mix is price-dependent (buy back below intrinsic value, issue above). 
	- Identifying outlier capital allocators (Singleton, Malone, Leonard) is one of the few durable sources of alpha because they compound differently from the index. 

- **Losses take more % gain to recover the deeper it is.** 
	- Also the marginal utility of an incremental extra dollar massively underweights the marginal cost of a decremental dollar lost, especially for big losses. This is the main motivation for wanting to reduce portfolio volatility. The utility function of an extra volatility unit reduced is positive initially (as this is a requirement for earning gains above RFR) until perhaps 10-15%, then trends exponentially from 25% onwards. This tells you the optimal point of portfolio volatility. Within that volatility budget, public market returns are power-law / lognormal distributed rather than normal — over a decade, 2-3 names in a 30-stock book typically drive >70% of returns — so Markowitz-style mean-variance optimization systematically underweights the asymmetric upside cases. Kelly criterion (adjusted for uncertainty about edge magnitude) argues for larger concentration on highest-conviction names than typical institutional practice. The barbell resolves the apparent tension between vol reduction and concentration: 80-90% of book in highly resilient compounders, 10-20% in downside-bounded convex bets where the loss is capped at position size but the upside is multiples (Taleb's antifragility).

- **Base rates and the outside view beat the inside-view narrative — anchor every forecast to the reference class before adjusting for company-specific edge.**
	- Mauboussin's *Base Rate Book* maps the empirical distributions of corporate outcomes — revenue growth, gross-margin persistence, ROIC fade — across thousands of firms over decades. Two findings reset most models: sustained high growth is far rarer than analyst extrapolation assumes (few firms hold >20% revenue CAGR for a decade, and the rate collapses as the revenue base grows), and abnormal ROIC fades toward the cost of capital on a measurable schedule. The discipline inverts the default process — start from what comparable firms at this size, growth and margin actually did, then adjust for genuine edge, never the reverse. This is the empirical backbone the mean-reversion-vs-trend meta-question needs: extrapolating early-adopter growth as if it were saturation behavior is the exact inside-view error base rates correct, and a genuine compounder earns its premium by being a justified outlier to the base rate, not by ignoring it.

- **Intangible investment is expensed, not capitalised — so reported earnings, invested capital and ROIC understate the economics of asset-light businesses.**
	- R&D, brand-building, software development and customer-acquisition spend run through the P&L the year they occur, even though they build multi-year assets. The consequence: a firm compounding intangible capital fastest looks least profitable and most expensive (depressed earnings, understated book equity), so P/E, P/B and EV/EBITDA become actively misleading screens and the book-value-based value/growth distinction breaks down. Mauboussin's correction is to re-capitalise the investment-like portion of SG&A and R&D onto a notional balance sheet, amortise it over its useful life, and recompute economic earnings and invested capital. Without this adjustment the ROIIC framework above is mismeasured for every software, consumer-brand and platform name in the vault — the real investment never enters the denominator.

- **For customer-driven businesses, value is built bottom-up from cohort economics — CLV, retention-curve shape and CAC — not top-down from aggregate financials.**
	- Aggregate revenue growth blends two forces with opposite value implications: new-customer acquisition and existing-customer retention/expansion. Customer-based corporate valuation (Mauboussin, building on Fader and McCarthy) decomposes the business into cohorts and models four drivers — acquisition (count and CAC), retention-curve shape (the most value-determinative variable; a flattening tail is worth multiples), monetisation per retained customer (expansion / net revenue retention), and cost to serve. A business growing 30% by pouring rising CAC into a leaky retention curve is worth a fraction of one growing 30% on a flattening curve with negative churn, yet the two look identical at the aggregate P&L. This is the customer-unit version of the mining framework above: each retained cohort is an existing mine with a predictable decline rate, and CAC-funded acquisition is new-mine capex whose return profile (LTV/CAC, payback) is the true ROIIC of a subscription or marketplace business.

- **Read the expectations the price already embeds, then isolate the single operating variable the market is mispricing — don't forecast in a vacuum.**
	- Rappaport and Mauboussin's *Expectations Investing* inverts the standard process: rather than build a DCF to a target price, reverse-engineer the price-implied expectations the current price discounts, then judge whether they are too high or too low. The contribution beyond a generic reverse-DCF is the expectations infrastructure — a structured chain from value triggers (sales, operating costs, investment) through value factors (volume, price/mix, operating leverage, scale economies, cost efficiencies) to the three value drivers (sales growth, operating margin, incremental investment). Working the chain pinpoints which operating lever a re-rating must come from, sharpening the research question from "is this cheap?" to "the price implies 8% revenue CAGR and 200bps of margin expansion — which is wrong, and what datapoint confirms it first?". This is the operating-variable scaffolding that turns the endgame / reverse-DCF discipline at the top of this note into a falsifiable, monitorable thesis.
