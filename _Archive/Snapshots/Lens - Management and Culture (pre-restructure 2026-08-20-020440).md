
**The capital markets rationale underlying importance of culture**

- Companies are a function of an existing business which generally requires little innovation or first principles thinking to maintain or slightly grow its cashflows, and a pipeline of  inventory of new optionalities that constantly renew across time. These optionalities are where the market presents an opportunity for management teams to design new products to capturing new markets that is adjacent to its competitive advantage, reorient business model to gain a competitive advantage, or simply to wait and return cash to shareholders when there is not an opportune time to deploy capitak. 
- Traditional investment analysis almost solely focuses on the existing business as is and not the optionality embedded in the organisation's presence in certain economic chokeholds or intersection of technological crossroad.
- The framework to view how these optionalities are assesed are thus:
	- a) companies that exist in an industry with high capacity for change are able to receive a feed of underpriced and overpriced optionalities with high frequency 
	- b) those that can recognise when there is a positive NPV opportunity require astute and strategic management with long term thinking that are experts in their field and most importantly 
	- c) most importantly, require a ready workforce that is sufficiently adaptable, hardworking and coordinated enough to execute these optionalities in a timely manner
- Companies possessing all three above characteristics are generally able to be valued on the basis of its existing business PLUS a optionality capture premium. 
- Examples of these types of businesses in that past are abundant in the capital markets (todays vanguard of this theme include companies like SpaceX, Stripe, Cloudflare etc.) and are generally structurally undervalued because the market often prices new optionalities at zero, and in a majority of instances, at a negative value due to the perception of capital destruction in pursuing new ventures. We exclusively focus on investing in companies that suffer from high degrees of capital markets psychosis with respect to this framework.

**Discussion on the characteristics of organisation with strong management & culture**

- Management team incentives around long term orientation is often a critical factor for the innovation speed and adaptability threshold and key reason why founder controlled companies tend to do better over time. 
	- Executives with compensation packages aligned to short term and those without ascribing their own 'soul' to the organisation seek to maximise short term stock price through market messaging, propensity to buy back shares at unattractive prices, engage in corporate expansionism, and most importantly benchmarking strategic decision competitive peers consensus and hyped market trends instead of on a first principles basis. 
	- This occurs because  career risk from standing out outweighs any benefit from first principles innovation and boosting short term stock prices is inherent in their incentive structure of positive performance rather than any fundamental gauge of corporate performance. We see to actively reward and look for companies with high levels of executive and employee stock ownership (and treat executive buys as catalysts) as well as incentive structures tied around concepts like EPS growth, ROIC or product level volumes for new business lines.
- Management teams capacity to understand and digest information about what is happening on the ground is also a critical variable, the number of hops before a piece of critical customer or operational information that it takes between management layers until it gets to a key decision maker or CEO is an important factor in assessing adaptability and resiliency to market versicitudes. 
	- A low level account exectutive who notices their companies products are failing to adapt to changing market conditions but is not incentivised, or unable to reach decision makers is a missed opportunity for the organisation to get ahead of competitive headwinds and suffers against an organisation that rewards and incentivises a high flow of strategically sensitive information to management. Most executives at stale organisations only see product level issues after a few years of poor performance in a tableu table of declining growth, the best organisations employees are able to directly message senior leaders to address emerging concerns. 
	- Operational staff's capacity to want to report issues and opportunities to middle and evenutally senior management is a function of either expected personal reward or the mission alignment score of an organisation. Organisations with a high mission alignment score can do away with strict individual tracking of performance and merit, however those without it like most mature organisations need to ensure that it is a cultural imperitive to be meritocratic and that sub-ordinates are recognised for project level success rather than middle management. 
- Capturing new optionalities requires the workforce to constantly thrive in a state of being on edge, this is not common for most organisations. A firm's capacity to be adaptable and innovative in the context of a changing competitive environment or customer demands and operate outside of their comfort zone is a function of their capacity to feel rewarded and recognised for their contribution. This is determined more by the 'cultural status quo' at organisations. 
	- Companies that have a strong mission (not just fluff around accountability, integrity, customer focus etc.) that is typically related to a unique product or service that it provides for the economy that is in flux or increasing demand provide staff with a sense of achievement with the company itself is doing well and taps to the 'pro-social' aspect of the mind of the employee where work achievements feel like charitable outcomes. These scenarios percolate a workforce that is dedicated and have incentives aligned with the organisation such that excessive employee monitoring around productivity, processes around seeking accountability is kept at a minimum and the capacity for employees to explore is maximised.
	- Good organisations have high capacity and demand for rate of learning, and thus are able to adapt to new situations and propose solutions and thus 'thinking' and 'deciding' can be done closer to where the work gets done and thus organisation become more nimble. But this requires both a growing organisation and also a meritocratic anti-bureucratic organisation which typically means younger firms or as firms naturally accumulate 'deadweight' staff over time that simply want to work for status and compete for increasing salary on a negative-sum basis with their colleagues, this is the gravitational attractor base rate for all organistations and it takes exemtary leadership for certain organisations to fight it.
	- Organisations that have growth momentum, are constantly chasing new adjacencies, naturally attract problem solvers and higher calibre employees, they do not need to pay these employees as much as their market rate because these employees feel they are paid in 'learning opportunities' and 'experience'. Thus these firms have a higher 'operating margin threshold' to tackle low margin opportunities that incumbents find it difficult to address
- Negative indicators are workplaces that reward:
	- sycophantic behaviour where the incentives for the workforce are not to deliver on the project but to make your superior look credible or well respected. This is often due to a 'waterflow' structure where bonuses and promotional opportunites are rewarded down a reporting line rather than assessed on a cross team and cross departmental basis
	- limited measurement and attention by higher executives of lower hierarchical incentives and contribution to projects. Limited opportunity for originators of high performing projects to be recognised and rewarded outside of his direct reporting line
	- excessive meetings where primary transmission of basic information that requires decision making is done through meetings rather than through written forms, meetings should be required to discuss complexity and not as a input/output mechanism
	- fiefdoms and limited internal mobility, subordinates are 'attached or owned' by their hiring managers and compensation is structured around 'share of overall pool' by senior leaders and different teams are benchmarked against eachother internally via strictly financial outcomes 
- Positive indicators (not overlapping with the above indicators)
	- Organisations with clear documented processes or knowledge wikis that evolve over time provide clarity to employees increase the rate of onboarding and can tolerate higher employee 'burn and churn'
	- Strong written communication culture that are cross departmental, cross hierachical. Entire organisation has fluid movement of staff and ideas and new projects are often not seeded from existing team structure but rather cross departmentally. It is often that collaboration between different parts of the organisation that new product opportunities can be forged.

**Product vs. Matrix Orgs**
- There are generally two polarity styles for organising people within large companies, one sorting by product for multi-product companies (or function for single product companies) and another is relying on a matrix style management design. These archtyoes are useful in different environments and recognising when and where there is alignment or misalignment has meaningful alpha to gauge execution efficacy.
- Product organisations (e.g. Apple or most Japanese industrials) have a very basic foundational cross segmental layer of 'corporate functions' that include HR, legal and finance but the rest of the staff, most importantly R&D, sales, and operational executives support the various products formed. This create meaningful duplication and cost burdens but it means that agility is maintained for serving the respective end markets. 
- Matrix organistions (e.g. early Spotify, current Cloudflare) are typically smaller, and function in the <5,000 headcount range, and is generally structurally more adaptable and effective when executed well. In these organisations, employees do not have a fixed reporting hierachy or role, they sometimes may have multiple managers, or sometimes are directly managed by a senior executive. Matrix style organisations tend to have lower hierachy, and are capturing faster growth markets and tend to have employees structured on a project by project basis where employees are assessed at the end of a project and then reassigned to where their interest lie, meaning they are free to explore in the 'menu' of available optionalities.
- Successful 'optionality capturing' fast growth companies tend to be structurally more similar to a matrix organisations, this is the very essence and embodiment of the 'founder led' firm with 'start-up' energy. However matrix organisations do not scale beyond a high enough headcount where individual accountability becomes easier to hide and incentive burden overhead necessitates an occification of processes and roles into more managable forms. 
- There are two high signal indicators in this applying this framework to assessing capital markets performance:
	- Fast growth startup pivots to a product level organisation generally indicates that the period of hyper innovation is over and that the organistional management overhead has outweighed the relative agility
	- Firms that stick to a matrix structure beyond its useful life may experience an implosion and decline in productivity and become outmaneauvred by a more mature rival that management design new first principles organistional structure and processe and pivot to a traditional organisation structure.
	- Decaying firms where a prodigal founder or high calibre management team returns to rejig the org structure often find themselves move towrads the matrix structure pole (although still largely a traditional product of functional org). The degree with which this is calibrated (e.g. key blocking stakeholders removed, high performing subordinates promoted, processes re-examined) determine the relative sucess of the turnaround. This is the standard private equity turnaround playbook. 

**Closing thought**

- There is a generally a natural momentum inherent in firms that do execute well and are in favourable markets, and the capital markets often do not ascribe the correct valuation to these opportunities. Firms that lose this momentum may survive and still thrive for a long time on the basis of existing market growth, however are generally fairly valued and do not deliver outsized returns. On the other hand, stale organisations that suddenly face new competitive disruption are most often melting ice cubes, and the market may still price them at reasonable multiples right before the vulture are feeding on their corpse.
