# AI-Grade MLCC Supply Chain: Upstream Pricing Power — Equity Research Deep-Dive (June 2026)

## TL;DR
- The single most acute, durable bottleneck in the AI-grade MLCC supply chain is **specialty PET release film for ultra-thin (<1 μm) dielectric green sheets**, where Toray Industries (TSE: 3402) is the global leader of a 5-firm oligopoly that QY Research estimates accounts for "nearly 87% of market share." The second-most acute bottleneck is **high-purity, sub-100 nm hydrothermal barium titanate**, where Sakai Chemical (TSE: 4078), Nippon Chemical Industrial (TSE: 4092), and the Murata-affiliated MF Material JV (accessible via Ishihara Sangyo, TSE: 4028) sit at the top of a three-firm Japanese oligopoly. The third tier is **ultra-fine CVD/wet-route nickel powder**, dominated by Toho Titanium (TSE: 5727 — in the process of being delisted by JX Advanced Metals), Sumitomo Metal Mining (TSE: 5713), and privately held Shoei Chemical.
- The cleanest publicly-listed pure-plays on the theme are **Sakai Chemical (4078)** and **Nippon Chemical Industrial (4092)**. The cleanest large-cap diversified vehicles with genuine MLCC exposure are **Toray Industries (3402)** for release film and **Disco Corporation (6146)** for MLCC singulation equipment. Sumitomo Metal Mining (5713), Ishihara Sangyo (4028) and SKC (KOSPI: 011790) are partial-exposure plays.
- Critical caveats: (1) **Shoei Chemical**, the single most-cited MLCC paste/nickel-powder leader, is **private** (Bloomberg ID 7867113Z:JP) and uninvestable. (2) **Toho Titanium (5727) is being taken private** by parent JX Advanced Metals in a simplified share exchange announced February 25, 2026 — investors must rotate into JX Advanced Metals to retain exposure. (3) The pure-play names (Sakai ¥76 bn; NCI ¥48 bn market cap) are illiquid by institutional standards.

## Key Findings

### End-market context: why upstream pricing power is structural, not cyclical
The MLCC industry is in the early innings of a multi-year structural cycle driven by AI infrastructure. Per TradingKey (May 2026): "a standard enterprise-grade server requires about 1,000 units, an Nvidia GB200 NVL72 rack requires approximately 440,000—a quantity 30 times that of a smartphone." At Murata's IR Day 2025 (Dec 1, 2025), as cited by TrendForce via Kyodo News: "Murata Manufacturing said demand for MLCCs used in AI servers is projected to be 3.3 times higher in fiscal 2030 than in fiscal 2025, with an average annual growth rate of approximately 30% from fiscal year 2025 to fiscal year 2030."

Pricing actions are now confirming the tightness. On March 17, 2026, Murata announced a 15–35% price increase effective April 1, 2026 (per TrendForce), "applying to AI server high-capacitance MLCCs, high-end automotive-grade MLCCs, and RF/microwave MLCCs." Per SemiMedia's customer-notice report (Apr 15, 2026), "Taiyo Yuden has notified customers it will raise prices on a range of passive components, with changes set to take effect from May 1," with TrendForce confirming increases "ranging from approximately 6% to 13%." Samsung Electro-Mechanics is reportedly evaluating up to 10%. In a March 18, 2026 note (per Investing.com), Goldman Sachs analysts wrote that "the reported price hikes for MLCCs in particular, if accurate, would likely have positive implications for Murata Mfg., Taiyo Yuden, and the industry as a whole."

Lead times for premium high-capacitance, low-ESL parts have stretched from 8 weeks to 24–40 weeks. **Auto-grade MLCC capacity cannot flex into AI-server-grade output without an 18–24 month requalification cycle**, and greenfield MLCC capacity lead time is 18–24 months — meaning shortages translate into pricing power at the materials-supplier level faster than capacity arrives.

The premium AI-grade segment imposes three constraints simultaneously: (1) sub-100 nm dielectric particle size with tight distribution; (2) sub-200 nm ultrafine nickel powder; (3) sub-25 μm release film with sub-0.1 μm defect control. Each constraint narrows the merchant supplier set from "many" to "two or three." That is the structural setup we are underwriting.

## Detailed Bottleneck Analysis (Ranked by Acuteness of Pricing Power)

### Bottleneck #1 — Specialty PET Release Film (HIGHEST CONVICTION)

**The choke point.** The release film is the polymer "carrier" on which the ceramic slurry is cast and dried to form the green sheet that becomes a single dielectric layer in the MLCC. For premium AI-grade MLCCs with dielectric layers below 1 μm and stacks of 500–1,000+ layers, the film's surface roughness (sub-0.1 μm), thickness uniformity (±1%), defect density (<0.05%), and release-coating uniformity become the rate-limiting variable for yield. A pinhole or particle defect on a 25 μm-thick film translates directly into a short between adjacent electrode layers in the finished MLCC — a fatal defect.

**Industry structure and quantified concentration.** Per QY Research (Global MLCC Release Film Sales Market Report 2026–2032), "Global top five companies account for nearly 87% of market share" and "the top seven companies – Toyobo, Mitsui Chemicals, LINTEC, SKC, Toray Industries, Mitsubishi Chemical Corporation, and Nan Ya Plastics Corp. (NPC) – accounting for an estimated 70% of the market" (Marketreportanalytics). Toray is universally cited as the #1 player; the company's own October 2022 press release states verbatim: "Toray leads the global MLCC mold release film market, producing these offerings at the Mishima and Gifu plants in Japan, Penfibre Sdn. Berhad in Malaysia, and Toray Advanced Materials Korea Inc." Toray is investing ¥8 billion to lift Lumirror release-film capacity by 60% at the Gifu Plant, online in 2025.

**Why pricing power is durable.** Release film for sub-1 μm dielectric is one of the most qualification-intensive materials in the MLCC bill of materials. MLCC makers typically dual-source but only after multi-year qualification including statistical yield validation across thousands of layer stack-ups. The film is "cheap" as a share of MLCC bill-of-materials (low single-digit %) but binary-critical — a single defect destroys a multi-layer MLCC — giving the classic "small cost, high criticality" pricing-power profile. New entrants face: (a) decades of biaxial-orientation polymer film tacit knowledge; (b) proprietary release-coating chemistry; (c) capex intensity; (d) multi-year qualification runs at each MLCC maker.

**Investable names:**
- **Toray Industries (TSE: 3402)** — Market cap ¥1.57–1.96 trillion (~$10–13 bn) as of mid-2026; FY2026 (Mar-26) revenue ¥2,585.1 bn (+0.9% YoY), net income ¥79.5 bn. Toray does **not** separately disclose MLCC release film revenue (it sits in the Functional Chemicals segment, which contains resins, foams, films, fine chemicals, and electronic materials — roughly 20–25% of group revenue). The MLCC release film business itself is estimated at low-single-digit % of Toray's enterprise value, so this is **NOT a pure-play** — Toray is a diversified materials conglomerate where MLCC film is a high-quality but small revenue contributor. The thesis works best when bought as one of multiple AI-materials exposures within Toray (also includes carbon fiber, ICT films, semiconductor films).
- **SKC (KOSPI: 011790)** — Market cap ~₩4.5–5.5 trillion (~$4.4 bn). Top-5 global MLCC release film supplier via SKC Hi-Tech & Marketing / Industrial Materials sub. Caveat: SKC has reported significant net losses in 2024–2025 from copper foil (SK Nexilis) and semiconductor materials (Absolics) units; mid-restructuring and the release film exposure is a small share of consolidated revenue. Useful as a Korean-listed proxy but not a clean play.
- **Lintec (TSE: 7966), Mitsubishi Chemical (TSE: 4188), Mitsui Chemicals (TSE: 4183), Toyobo (TSE: 3101), Nan Ya Plastics (TWSE: 1303)** — All have meaningful MLCC release film exposure but it is small relative to enterprise value for each.

**Bottom line:** Toray is the highest-conviction listed name in release film. Acknowledge it is a diversified conglomerate where MLCC release film may be approximately 5% of revenue (analyst estimate, not disclosed), so position sizing must reflect dilution of the pure pricing-power signal.

### Bottleneck #2 — High-Purity, Sub-100 nm Hydrothermal Barium Titanate (BaTiO3)

**The choke point.** Barium titanate is the dielectric ceramic that gives the MLCC its capacitance. For premium AI-grade high-capacitance MLCCs (e.g., 10 μF in 0402 case, 100 μF in 0603), dielectric layers must be sub-1 μm thick and the BaTiO3 grains must be sub-100 nm with tight particle-size distribution, high crystallinity, and impurity levels below ppm thresholds for iron, silicon, sodium. This requires hydrothermal or co-precipitation synthesis (not the cheaper solid-state route), which involves significant tacit IP.

**Industry structure.** Ceramic powders are 20–25% of cost for low-capacity MLCCs and 35–45% for high-capacity MLCCs. The merchant market is dominated by three Japanese names — Sakai Chemical, Nippon Chemical Industrial, and Fuji Titanium (now mostly captive via the MF Material JV with Murata) — plus captive segments at Murata and Samsung Electro-Mechanics. Chemical Research Insight states: "Sakai Chemical dominates the global MLCC dielectric powder market with approximately 25% share." Dataintelo notes: "Sakai Chemical Industry is widely regarded as the global leader in merchant-market high-purity BaTiO3 powder, with decades of expertise in co-precipitation and hydrothermal synthesis that enables consistent sub-100 nm particle production." Fuji Titanium is now 55%-owned within the joint venture **MF Material Co., Ltd.** with Murata 35% and Ishihara Sangyo 10% — capacity being expanded at the Nobeoka, Miyazaki plant for 2027 commissioning.

**Why pricing power is durable.** Three reasons. (1) **Captive-merchant split** — Murata and SEMCO produce some powder in-house, but high-spec AI-grade powder, especially sub-100 nm and rare-earth-doped formulations, is increasingly sourced from the Japanese merchant players. (2) **Sub-100 nm grade commands a 35–60% price premium** over standard high-purity grades (Dataintelo), and that premium is widening as MLCC layer thickness shrinks. (3) **TDK's April 1, 2026 establishment of TDK-NCI Advanced Materials** (51% TDK, 49% Nippon Chemical Industrial) for MLCC ceramic materials shows that even the largest captive-capable MLCC makers are *increasing* — not decreasing — their reliance on Japanese merchant powder chemistry expertise. This is a structural validation of the moat.

**Investable names:**
- **Sakai Chemical Industry (TSE: 4078)** — Market cap ¥76 bn (~$500 M) at recent price ¥5,640. PE 15.9x, dividend yield 2.94%. FY2026 (Mar-26) revenue ¥81.4 bn, net income ¥2.75 bn. The company reports 11 segments; Electronic Materials (high-purity dielectric powders + spherical silica + secondary battery materials) is one of three "growth businesses" highlighted for capex, but Sakai does not headline a single Electronic Materials %; analyst consensus places it at roughly 15–20% of group sales. The stock has performed strongly (+18% vs. Nikkei 225 over the past 6 months; +50% above 200-day MA) on the AI MLCC narrative. **This is the closest thing to a small-cap pure-play on high-purity BaTiO3 in public markets.**
- **Nippon Chemical Industrial (TSE: 4092)** — Market cap ¥48.5 bn (~$320 M), recent price ¥5,560, PE on ¥331 EPS gives ~17x, dividend yield 2.55%. The April 2026 establishment of TDK-NCI Advanced Materials JV (TDK 51% / NCI 49%) is a major event — it locks in NCI as TDK's strategic ceramic powder partner. NCI also produces phosphorus, chromium, and barium compounds; barium-titanate/MLCC dielectric ceramics is one segment within Specialty Chemicals. Smaller and less diversified than Sakai, but the TDK JV makes it a higher-conviction direct AI-MLCC exposure on a forward basis.
- **Ishihara Sangyo (TSE: 4028)** — Market cap ¥94.6 bn (~$620 M). Owns 55% of MF Material via Fuji Titanium (with Murata 35%, Ishihara direct 10%). However, Ishihara Sangyo is dominantly a titanium dioxide pigment / agrochemicals company — MF Material / barium titanate is a small share of the parent. Use as a "Murata's captive supply chain" tracker, not a pure-play.

**Bottom line:** Sakai Chemical (4078) and Nippon Chemical Industrial (4092) are the cleanest pure-play public-market names on the high-purity BaTiO3 thesis. Both are small-caps with limited liquidity and have re-rated significantly on the AI MLCC narrative.

### Bottleneck #3 — Ultra-Fine Nickel Powder for Internal Electrodes

**The choke point.** Each MLCC contains hundreds to thousands of nickel internal-electrode layers stacked between dielectric layers. For premium AI-grade MLCCs with sub-1 μm dielectric layers, the nickel powder must be sub-200 nm (and increasingly sub-150 nm) with tight particle distribution, low oxygen content (0.3–3.0 wt%), controlled crystallinity, and minimized sintering shrinkage mismatch with the BaTiO3. This is achieved through CVD (chemical vapor decomposition / gas-phase reduction) or specialized wet-chemical (reactive crystallization) routes that only four companies have industrialized at MLCC-grade scale: JFE Mineral, Sumitomo Metal Mining, Toho Titanium, and Shoei Chemical.

**Industry structure.** Per Dataintelo, Sumitomo Metal Mining holds approximately 18.2–18.3% of MLCC-grade nickel powder share with ~68,000 metric tons of capacity; JFE Mineral holds ~14.2–14.6% with ~52,000 metric tons; the next tier (Toho Titanium, Shoei Chemical) and Chinese entrants (Jiangsu Boqian, CNPC Powder) fill out the balance. Toho Titanium completed its No. 4 nickel powder plant at the Wakamatsu Plant with a ¥7.5 bn investment; the company's own 2024 CSR report states: "In the medium to long term, further growth in MLCC demand is expected owing to the expansion of the automotive electronics and AI server markets." Per Toho Titanium's product page: "Our nickel powder is used as the ultra-thin electrode materials for multi-layer ceramic capacitors (MLCCs). Taking advantage of the small particle size distribution width that distinguishes our nickel powder, it is used in the electrodes of high-end MLCCs."

**Why pricing power is durable.** Particle-size and oxygen-content tolerances tighten with each MLCC generation, narrowing the qualified supplier set. Nickel powder is a small share of MLCC bill-of-materials but a binary-critical electrode input. Switching costs are high — a new nickel powder grade typically requires 12–18 months of qualification work at the MLCC maker. The Japanese leaders have a multi-decade lead in process IP and customer co-development.

**Investable names — IMPORTANT INVESTABILITY ISSUES:**
- **Toho Titanium (TSE: 5727)** — Market cap ¥188 bn (~$1.2 bn) recently; **JX Advanced Metals (parent) announced February 25, 2026 a simplified share exchange to take Toho Titanium private as a wholly-owned subsidiary.** The listed equity will disappear. This is the cleanest Japanese-listed pure-play on MLCC nickel powder, but **investability is closing**. Investors wishing to maintain the exposure must rotate into the parent **JX Advanced Metals** (the new spinout of the metals business of ENEOS Holdings, listed in March 2025).
- **Sumitomo Metal Mining (TSE: 5713)** — Market cap ¥2.6 trillion (~$16.3 bn). Major MLCC nickel powder supplier (estimated 18% global share) within its Materials segment, which itself is roughly 15–20% of consolidated revenue. The remainder is nickel mining/smelting and copper smelting — so this is a **diversified base-metals company** where MLCC powder is a fraction of revenue and even smaller fraction of operating profit (battery materials impairments in FY2025 hurt segment economics). Real exposure but heavily diluted; primarily a nickel-price play.
- **Shoei Chemical** — The most-cited MLCC paste/nickel powder pure-play in the literature — but **the company is privately held** (Bloomberg ID 7867113Z:JP), founded 1956, Asada-family controlled, ~$350–460 M revenue. **Not investable in public markets.** Often confused in retail sources with the helmet maker Shoei Co Ltd (TSE: 7839) and the Osaka chemical trader Shoei Yakuhin (TSE: 3537) — both of which have **no MLCC exposure**. **Investors should not buy 7839 or 3537 thinking they are getting MLCC paste exposure.**
- **JFE Holdings (TSE: 5411)** — Holding company of JFE Mineral, the second-largest MLCC nickel powder supplier. But JFE Holdings is dominantly a steel company (FY2025 consolidated revenue ¥4.86 trillion); MLCC nickel powder is rounding-error — **not a viable single-thesis investment**.

**Bottom line:** Public-market access to MLCC nickel powder is structurally compromised. The pure-play (Shoei) is private, the cleanest listed name (Toho Titanium) is being delisted, and the remaining listed names (SMM, JFE) are dominantly metals companies with rounding-error MLCC exposure. This is a real bottleneck with no clean public-market vehicle — a notable conclusion in itself.

### Bottleneck #4 — Conductive / Electrode / Termination Pastes

**The choke point.** The nickel/copper inner-electrode paste and external termination paste are formulated by combining ultra-fine metal powders, glass frits, organic binders, and rheology modifiers. The paste must screen-print at sub-1 μm thickness with high uniformity and survive co-firing with the ceramic at 1,000–1,300°C. The leading paste formulators are Shoei Chemical (Japan), Sumitomo Metal Mining, Noritake, Daiken Chemical, Ferro/Vibrantz, Heraeus, DuPont, Tanaka, plus Chinese entrants Shandong Sinocera and Fenghua Advanced.

**Industry structure.** Per Archive Market Research: "Ferro, Shoei Chemical, Sumitomo Metal Mining, and Noritake are key players, collectively accounting for an estimated 60% of the global market value" of base-metal-electrode paste for MLCC. Shoei is widely cited as #1 in Ni inner-electrode paste; Sumitomo Metal Mining is the integrated supplier (powder → paste) for the Japanese MLCC base.

**Why pricing power is meaningful but slightly less than #1–3.** Paste formulation IP is significant and switching costs are real, but there are more qualified suppliers globally (8+ credible firms) compared to release film (5) or BaTiO3 (3). Chinese players are gaining share in mid-grade applications.

**Investable names:** Shoei (private). Sumitomo Metal Mining (5713) — same diversification caveat as above. Ferro is now part of privately-held Vibrantz (Apollo). Heraeus is private. DuPont is diversified — MLCC paste is rounding error. Tanaka Holdings is private. Noritake (TSE: 5331) — listed but tableware/grinding-wheel dominant. **There is no clean publicly-listed pure-play here.**

### Bottleneck #5 — Precision Dicing / Singulation Equipment

**The choke point.** After firing, MLCC bar stacks must be precision-cut into individual chips (typically 0402, 0201, or 008004 case size, where 008004 = 0.25 mm × 0.125 mm). The dicing blade and machine must hold sub-micron tolerances on extremely small parts at high throughput. Disco Corporation's DAD/DFD-series dicers are the de-facto industry standard at Murata, TDK, Taiyo Yuden, and Samsung Electro-Mechanics.

**Industry structure.** Per Morningstar equity coverage of Disco Corp (TSE:6146, 2025): Disco is "the world's largest manufacturer of semiconductor-grade saws and grinders, with an estimated 60%-70% market share in the semiconductor dicing and grinding industry as of 2025." Mordor Intelligence notes "DISCO and Tokyo Seimitsu anchor the blade segment with more than 60% of shipments." For MLCC singulation specifically, anecdotal industry estimates put Disco's share **above 80%**, though Disco does not separately disclose this in IR. Tokyo Seimitsu (TSE: 7729) is the #2 in semiconductor dicing but has marginal presence in MLCC chip singulation.

**Why pricing power is durable.** Disco is a near-monopoly. Switching is uneconomic for incumbent MLCC makers. ~20–25% of Disco's sales are consumables (dicing blades, grinding wheels), creating a razor-and-blade revenue model that compounds with installed-base growth.

**Investable name:**
- **Disco Corporation (TSE: 6146)** — Market cap ¥7.06–7.59 trillion (~$43–48 bn). FY2026 revenue ¥436.9 bn (+11% YoY), net income ¥135.5 bn, gross margins consistently above 65%, operating margins >40%. PE ~52x reflects the AI-driven structural growth narrative. **Caveat:** Disco's primary growth driver is *semiconductor* wafer dicing/grinding for AI logic, HBM, and SiC power devices — MLCC dicing is a smaller share of revenue (estimated low-double-digit %, not disclosed). So Disco is a **high-quality near-monopoly equipment supplier where MLCC is a beneficial but not primary exposure**. Best understood as an AI-broad play that includes MLCC, not as an MLCC pure-play.

### What we are NOT recommending (and why)

- **Murata, Samsung Electro-Mechanics, TDK, Taiyo Yuden, Yageo, Walsin** — These are the MLCC makers themselves. They have pricing power, but they are the customers our upstream thesis is positioned against. In tight supply, upstream BOM suppliers with single-source positions often extract more rent than the MLCC makers, who must still pass through to the GPU/OEM customer.
- **Chinese ceramic powder / paste makers** (Shandong Sinocera, Fenghua, Three-Circle/CCTC) — Real businesses but face quality gaps versus Japanese incumbents in the high-end AI-grade segment, RoHS/REACH compliance friction in Western supply chains, and significant local listing/governance risk.
- **Holy Stone, Walsin Technology, Yageo** — Taiwanese MLCC makers, not upstream materials suppliers.
- **Stocks named "Shoei"** other than the private Shoei Chemical Inc. (i.e., Shoei Co 7839 and Shoei Yakuhin 3537) — These have **no MLCC paste exposure**. Common identifier confusion.

## Caveats

1. **Shoei Chemical is private and not investable.** Sources frequently cite Shoei as the dominant MLCC nickel-paste leader; investors should not interpret this as a buyable thesis. Other "Shoei"-named listed entities (TSE: 7839 helmets, TSE: 3537 chemical trader) are unrelated.
2. **Toho Titanium (5727) is being delisted.** JX Advanced Metals announced February 25, 2026 a simplified share exchange to take Toho Titanium private. The listed equity will disappear. Pricing-power exposure must be replicated via JX Advanced Metals' shares post-transaction.
3. **Disclosed segment data is opaque.** Most diversified names (Toray, SMM, JFE, Ishihara) do not break out MLCC-materials revenue separately. Estimates of "X% of revenue is MLCC" are analyst estimates, not company-disclosed.
4. **Small-cap liquidity.** Sakai (¥76 bn) and Nippon Chemical Industrial (¥48 bn) are illiquid by institutional standards; meaningful position sizes can move the market.
5. **Speculative pricing-power risk.** Market-research reports cited (Dataintelo, Valuates, QY Research, intelmarketresearch, Chemical Research Insight) are syndicated aggregators of uneven quality. Where possible, conclusions are anchored to (a) company IR documents (Toray Lumirror release, Toho Titanium CSR report, Murata MF Material JV disclosure, TDK-NCI JV announcement, Murata IR Day 2025), (b) trade press (TrendForce, DigiTimes, EE Times Europe, Passive Components Blog, SemiMedia), and (c) Bloomberg/StockAnalysis/Investing.com for market data. Treat third-party market share estimates as directional.
6. **Captive vs. merchant distinction is critical.** Murata produces some of its own dielectric powder, nickel powder, and paste in-house. The merchant suppliers we identify therefore sell to the *gap* between captive supply and total demand. As Murata builds more in-house (via the MF Material JV and other moves), merchant suppliers may be increasingly displaced for Murata-specific volume — though SEMCO, TDK, Taiyo Yuden, and Chinese MLCC maker demand continues to grow and creates offsetting opportunity. The structural thesis assumes premium AI-grade demand growth >> Murata captive capacity growth.
7. **The most acute bottleneck — release film — is also the one where the leading player (Toray) has the *least* concentrated equity exposure** (most diluted by other businesses). Conversely, the pure-plays (Sakai, NCI) are in BaTiO3, where the bottleneck is real but the merchant market is already partially captive-served. This is the central trade-off facing the thesis.
8. **Equipment exposure (Disco) is high-quality but valuation-extended.** PE ~52x reflects the AI semiconductor narrative more than MLCC; investors should not double-count Disco as both an AI-semis and AI-MLCC play.
