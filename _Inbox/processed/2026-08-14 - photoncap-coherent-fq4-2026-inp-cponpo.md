# Coherent FQ4 2026: InP Capacity, CPO/NPO Content, and CapEx Return

TL;DR

Coherent’s FQ4 2026 revenue, released after the close on August 12, came in at $2.05B. That lands almost exactly at the top of the guidance range given three months ago, and non-GAAP EPS of $1.74 went past the top end ($1.72) [1].

Guidance for the next quarter is $2.2B to $2.4B in revenue, roughly +12% QoQ at the midpoint. And yet the stock, up nearly 9% in the session right before the release on the 12th, gave the entire rally back with an 8% drop on the 13th, the first trading day after [1][3].

My read is this. The real news from this call is between the lines of the transcript. Where the bottleneck has moved, what six-inch does to margins, and what the company is worried about instead of demand. The direction is in the body.

## 1. August 12: A Top-of-the-Guide Quarter and a Post-Earnings Reversal

Results touched the top of the guide, EPS went through it, and the stock still fell 8% the next day. That is this Coherent quarter in one line.

Coherent reported FQ4 2026 results (quarter ended June 30) after the close on August 12. Revenue came in at $2.05B, up 34% year over year on a reported basis [1]. The guidance range the company had given three months earlier was $1.91B to $2.05B, so this lands almost exactly at the top end. I go through the item-by-item guidance comparison in section 9.

Profitability came along with it. Non-GAAP gross margin of 40.2%, EPS of $1.74 up 74% year over year, and for the full year, FY2026 revenue of $7.12B at +22.5% [1]. The segment mix tells you where this growth comes from: Datacenter & Communications did $1.6B-plus in Q4, up close to 60% year over year and now around 80% of the total, while Industrial slipped backward to the $430M level [1]. The AI data center side is carrying the whole company.

Guidance is strong too. FQ1 2027 revenue of $2.2B to $2.4B, non-GAAP gross margin of 39.5-41.5%, EPS of $1.85-2.05 [1]. At the midpoint, that is another quarter of 12%-plus sequential growth.

And yet the stock moved in an interesting way. Shares rose nearly 9% to around $356 in the regular session on August 12, right before the release, then closed the first post-earnings session on the 13th down 8% at $327.23, giving the entire rally back (as of the 2026-08-13 close) [3]. After touching the top of the guide and calling for another quarter of double-digit growth, why did it fall? There are about three candidates. Expectations already loaded into a stock trading at close to four times its 52-week low [3], the cash burden of an aggressive buildout, or simply a bar that had formed above the guidance. The second candidate is one you can check directly against the CapEx and cash-flow numbers later in this piece.

The public takeaway was just one thing, the InP bottleneck. But reading the transcript from start to finish, this call carries a few bigger changes underneath. And the gap between the headline numbers and the post-earnings reaction is filled by exactly these lines between the lines. So here they are.

## 2. The constraint has already moved upstream from the module

On this call, Coherent stated flatly that transceiver assembly and test capacity are not constraints today. The biggest constraint is InP production [2].

Honestly, this is where I paused. It was hard to agree with, and it made me think twice. I have long viewed test as the single biggest bottleneck in optical devices. I have published quite a few articles on test, and I hold quite a few of the related stocks too. Why PIC test is harder than EIC test, and how large its share is at the package level, is something I also touched on in [PC101] Lecture 4. So why did Coherent point at InP, and specifically production, instead of test?

I still think InP is critical and close to irreplaceable. But here is how I read that line: the InP bottleneck right now is simply that severe.

The numbers back it up. June-quarter InP laser output grew about 80% year over year, and the company’s own 800G and 1.6T transceiver demand is effectively absorbing all of it [2]. Everything made goes straight into its own modules, which is why the optical supply chain’s scarcity is getting harder to understand by counting transceiver units. You have to look past module assembly to EMLs, CW lasers, and below them, InP wafer and substrate capacity.

And this is not just Coherent’s story. On the Lumentum call the day before Coherent’s report, the same direction showed up: EMLs are still shipping behind demand, and on high-power lasers supply has fallen even further behind as demand accelerated [7]. Two reports one day apart are pointing at the same upstream layer. I put the Lumentum side together separately in Lumentum’s First $1B Quarter: FQ4 2026 Earnings Analysis.

I went deeper into that upstream bottleneck recently in AXT and Lumentum’s Prepayment Deal: The InP Substrate LTA Chain. Expanding fab capacity and securing InP substrate you can actually use are two different problems.

## 3. Six-inch InP is a capacity story and a margin story at the same time

Coherent’s move to six-inch is not only about a bigger wafer adding production capacity. The CFO’s phrasing was clear.

“Four times, but it’s at half the cost.” [2]

Spelled out, a six-inch wafer yields about four times as many devices as three-inch, at around half the cost per device [2]. The more interesting part is that yields for EMLs, CW lasers, and photodiodes are all already higher on six-inch than on three-inch [2]. Normally, when you move to a larger wafer diameter you lose yield first before you gain anything, and this line says they are already past that stage. So as InP capacity grows, transceiver shipments can rise while the cost of the most critical component inside them comes down at the same time.

I had already flagged this as a core monitoring point in the previous-quarter piece, $20B+ New SAM on Top of +27% Pro Forma Revenue: The Quarter Coherent Crossed from Transceiver Player to Platform, and this call made the six-inch margin thesis considerably sharper than back then. In other words, in this cycle, volume growth and margin expansion do not necessarily have to move in opposite directions.

That is the supply side of the transcript. What stayed with me longer, though, were the demand-side lines: the company’s answer to the market’s CPO-delay worry, and the CEO’s description of the FY27 order book. If the next day’s 8% drop missed something, it is probably here.

## 4. What kind of debate is CPO versus NPO for Coherent?

The market has lately had a lot to say about CPO delays and whether NPO gets adopted first. Coherent’s answer was unusually strong.

“We have seen absolutely no push-out of CPO demand.” [2]

Demand has instead increased, with some customer requests even pulled in, they said. At the same time, NPO engagement has also grown sharply over the past three to six months [2]. And the sentence I read as the most important in this section is this one.

“The amount of content that we would have in an NPO application versus a CPO is very comparable, very similar.” [2]

Why does this sentence matter? Because for Coherent, NPO and CPO are less two separate investment theses than two different form factors. Coherent is not a company that only supplies the laser. It can take the laser, the external laser module, isolators, polarization-maintaining fiber, fiber attachment, all the way to the SiPh PIC, and PhotonLink is the strategy of bundling those into one integrated optics platform [2]. Whether the optical attach point sits next to the package or on the board, the optical content going in is similar, and the company said so itself.

Why I view CPO as a staged optical transition rather than a single architecture is laid out in DSP, LPO, NPO, CPO: The Four Optical Architectures and the Light Source Beneath Them All. Even if CPO takes longer, LPO and NPO can fill the gap in between. So the more important question for Coherent may be less whether CPO beats NPO, and more how much of scale-up converts from copper to optics. As long as copper-to-optics conversion continues, Coherent’s content opportunity keeps growing.

## 5. How far up the network does OCS go?

OCS was more interesting than expected too. Coherent puts the OCS opportunity at more than $4B, and this is not a new number from this call: it is the scale the company has been laying out through this year, and it held [2]. What is widening instead is the application area, from the existing scale-out domain toward DCI and other network domains [2].

Here it overlaps with Lumentum again. Lumentum also doubled OCS shipments quarter over quarter and guided to its first $100M-plus OCS quarter [7]. Coherent’s number is an opportunity estimate and Lumentum’s are actual shipments, so they are different kinds of figures, but the two companies pointing at the same layer seems clear enough.

What I read here is less simple OCS unit growth and more that optical switching is spreading across multiple layers of the AI network hierarchy. If CPO/NPO is the process of turning electrical links into optical links, OCS is the question of how to reconfigure the optical fabric those links create. Instead of passing through one more electronic switch, it reconfigures the optical path itself, which makes it a different kind of thing from the usual networking box. So CPO/NPO and OCS are less separate themes than the same direction in the end. More network functions are moving into the optical domain.

The mechanics of OCS themselves are something I laid out at length a while back in Mapping the AI Landscape with Light: How OCS Transforms the Data Center Physical Layer, and enough updates have piled up since then that I am planning to cover it once more in a separate piece before long.

## 6. What does Coherent sell into scale-across?

Coherent’s Communications revenue grew 56% year over year in Q4 [2]. The products management singled out were ZR/ZR+ transceivers, pump lasers, and high-end optical subsystems, and Multi-Rail has sampled to multiple customers with a revenue ramp expected in the first half of 2027 [2]. And in describing Multi-Rail, the company explicitly connected it to the “scale-across AI networking” opportunity [2].

What matters here is that Coherent does not monetize scale-across through a single component. It sells transceivers, sells pump lasers, sells amplifiers and subsystems, and can climb up to higher-level transport solutions. That theme got its own piece in One Layer Below SemiAnalysis’s Meta Map: Scale-Across Is Coherent, Not CPO. If scale-up inside the rack is the CPO/NPO fight, then between data centers and campuses an independent optical cycle called coherent DCI opens up.

And interestingly, this lines up with what came out of Lumentum’s report too. On Lumentum’s call there was even an anecdote that one large hyperscaler’s network capacity connecting two AI data centers is twice the entire global backbone that company deployed over the past decade [7].

Once AI clusters stop growing inside a single building and spread across multiple buildings and campuses, optical content becomes hard to calculate from transceiver counts alone. Pump lasers, amplifiers, coherent DSPs, transceivers, subsystems, and fiber all come with it. Personally, this scale-across layer is the one I have been watching with real interest lately.

The related players line up like this.

- $CIEN Ciena: coherent DSP, NPO/CPO, FST, scale-across, DCI
- $CSCO Cisco / Acacia: coherent pluggables, 800G DSP, coherent transmission
- $NOK Nokia: 1.6T coherent, Coherent-Lite, InP/SiPh PIC, scale-across transport
- $MRVL Marvell: 1.6T coherent DSP, 2nm DSP, optical connectivity silicon
- $AVGO Broadcom: 400G/lane DD-EML, InP laser, DSP, CPO, switch ASIC
- $COHR Coherent: InP EML/PD, 400G/lane optics, SiPh, coherent components
- $TSEM Tower Semiconductor: silicon photonics foundry, PH18 SiPho, 400G SiPh MZM
- 6503.T Mitsubishi Electric: high-speed InP EML, 400G/lane device technology

FST (Full-Spectrum Transponder) on the Ciena line may be unfamiliar, so one line on it: instead of plugging coherent pluggables into a router one by one, it processes a wide swath of C/L-band optical spectrum all at once in a single standalone transport platform. Ciena proposed the concept at OFC, and the picture was 16 lanes of 1.6T bundled at 300GHz channel spacing, pushing 25Tb/s out of a single line port. If CPO/NPO is the technology that pulls client optics in next to the ASIC, FST is the transport box that takes the data center-to-data center coherent traffic pushed out of the router and handles it in bulk. In effect it is a coherent transport architecture for scale-across.

Of these, the June correction in CIEN, NOK, and CSCO was covered in Down 40%, 40%, and 14% in Six Weeks: What the Ciena, Nokia, and Cisco Correction Priced In, and how the point where NVIDIA and AMD cut the rack reshapes the optical landscape is laid out in Same Split, Opposite Directions: Where NVIDIA and AMD Cut the Rack Decides Where Optics Gets Paid.

## 7. What the FY27 order book says

If I had to pick the single strongest line on this call, it is this one.

“Fiscal 2027 is basically completely booked out.” [2]

And what came right after matters more.

“What we are seeing now is customers now booking into calendar 2028.” [2]

This was not vague forecasting: the explanation was that purchase orders for specific products are already coming in for 2028 [2], and multiple LTAs run to the end of the decade [2]. The contracts also go beyond simple volume forecasts.

“They have pricing-related commitments, and then they also have sort of minimum demand guarantees from our customers, or sometimes you refer to those as take-or-pay agreements.” [2]

All of this is management’s description, to be clear. Cancellation terms on individual POs and the minimum-purchase levels in each LTA are not disclosed. Still, the direction reads through. The problem Coherent has to solve now is less guessing whether demand shows up, and more how fast it can turn demand it can already see into capacity.

NVIDIA’s investment in Coherent reads in the same context. It is less a simple equity bet than something that carries a multi-year commercial relationship and optical capacity lock-in at the same time [5]. So the optical cycle is increasingly running in the order of customer commitment → capacity expansion → revenue realization.

## 8. Where to check the return on CapEx

Q4 CapEx jumped to $556M from $290M in Q3 [1][2]. For the year, FY2026 came to $1.1B, comfortably more than double the prior year’s $441M [1]. The company says this investment directly supports Data Center and Communications capacity expansion [2].

The number alone looks aggressive, and the strain already shows in the financial statements. FY2026 operating cash flow fell to $79.5M from $633.6M a year earlier, and inventory swelled from $1.44B to $2.58B over the year [1]. Cash is getting tied up in the buildout and the ramp. That said, the CFO put the payback on data center capacity investment at around 18 months [2]. I will note alongside that this is a forward estimate, not a realized return.

What I want to say here is not a CapEx eulogy. The point is that across the optical supply chain, customers have already started locking up future capacity in advance. Lumentum tying up AXT substrate with a prepayment-backed long-term agreement [8], and Tower taking $290M of customer prepayments from SiPh customers for capacity reservation [9], are the same structure. That chain is covered in detail in the AXT piece linked in section 2.

So the number I will watch going forward is less CapEx itself than what that CapEx sends back. Whether the six-inch InP ramp keeps pushing actual gross margin higher, whether comparable content converts into actual revenue whichever of CPO or NPO customers pick, whether OCS spreads across more network layers, whether Multi-Rail becomes a new system-level revenue stream for scale-across, and whether the booked FY27 demand carries through to the company’s stated targets of $3B+ quarterly revenue and 42%+ gross margin [2]. Those five are my checklist.

## 9. Checking the Last Call: The Six Items I Flagged in May

In the $20B+ New SAM piece written after the Q3 call in May (linked in section 3), I set this report as the checkpoint. What I put on the list was the Q4 guidance and six monitoring points.

Guidance first. The Q4 guide the company gave at the time was revenue of $1.91-2.05B, non-GAAP gross margin of 39.0-41.0%, and EPS of $1.52-1.72 [4], and the actuals are revenue of $2.05B, gross margin of 40.2%, and EPS of $1.74 [1]. Revenue touched the top of the range and EPS went through it, so the Base Case I wrote down then, revenue within the guide plus sustained datacenter acceleration, played out as written.

The six monitoring items moved this much on this call.

- Six-inch ramp pace: In progress, and the better-than-expected news is that yields already exceed three-inch (section 3). But the share of six-inch in total InP capacity was once again not disclosed. Carried over to the next call.
- 1.6T mix: Answered indirectly, via the comment that internal 800G/1.6T demand is absorbing all InP output (section 2). The mix number itself remains undisclosed.
- OCS revenue: The $4B+ opportunity was held (section 5), but there is still no quarterly revenue breakout.
- CPO timing: “Absolutely no push-out,” with some requests even pulled in (section 4). Whether revenue recognition has actually opened is something the next quarter’s numbers have to show.
- NVIDIA commitment cadence: Management said some LTAs carry minimum-demand or take-or-pay features and run to the end of the decade (section 7). Whether those exact terms apply to the NVIDIA agreement is not disclosed, and the quarterly curve of NVIDIA-attributed revenue is still not visible.
- LITE/COHR margin gap: In two reports one day apart, non-GAAP gross margin came in at 50.4% for LITE [6] and 40.2% for COHR [1]. The gap is still around ten points, and six-inch is one of COHR’s main levers for closing it.

None of the six items broke the wrong way. But only about half got confirmed with numbers, and the other half rolled over to “next quarter” once again. I am writing that down as is.

## 10. Conclusion: These Dates Will Do the Judging

If I had to compress this call into one line, it is this. Coherent’s story is no longer “how many 1.6T transceivers can it sell.” It is how many layers, from InP through lasers, integrated optics, fiber, OCS, and transport systems, Coherent can keep in-house while optics expands across scale-out, scale-up, and scale-across at the same time.

And so far in this cycle, the company’s biggest problem has not been finding demand. It has been making light fast enough.

Whether that read is right, these dates will tell.

- FQ1 2027 report, expected in early November 2026: Whether the $2.2-2.4B guide and 39.5-41.5% gross margin land. Also the first report card on the CapEx from section 8.
- Within 2026: The first disclosure of OCS revenue contribution, and whether CPO-related revenue recognition actually opens. Both are my checkpoints, not company commitments.
- First half of 2027: The initial Multi-Rail revenue ramp (management’s timeline) [2].
- During 2027: Whether the company discloses the six-inch share of total InP capacity, and whether gross margin actually moves toward 42%. The first one is my checkpoint, not a company commitment.
- 2028: Realization of the orders now booking into calendar 2028, and whether the 18-month payback comes back as numbers.

The conditions under which I am wrong live on the same list. If reported FQ1 revenue in November misses even the prior guide’s $2.2B low end, or a six-inch yield problem surfaces, the margin thesis has to step back, and if other suppliers’ calls cross-confirm a CPO push-out, this call’s strong denial gets downgraded to a vendor claim and revisited. And if the CapEx payback keeps slipping past 18 months, I will record the answer to section 8’s question as “no.”

The next Coherent checkpoint is around the FQ1 report in November. Before that, AXT’s results should give us a look at the substrate side of the chain first.
