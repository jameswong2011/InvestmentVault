### Optics

**Lumentum (LITE): The Pure AI Acceleration Story**

**What is going right?** Fiscal Q4 revenue more than doubled, up 109% year over year to $1.01 billion, topping the $987.7 million consensus, with adjusted EPS of $3.23 against expectations in the $2.97 to $2.99 range. Management raised Q1 FY2027 revenue guidance to $1.23 billion to $1.28 billion, far above the $1.16 billion consensus, and guided adjusted EPS to $4.05 to $4.35 against a $3.61 consensus. This strong beat on guidance is what moved the stock higher and it demonstrated that top-line scale is converting into operating leverage rather than being consumed by cost. Adjusted gross margin reached 47.4% against a 44.3% Street estimate.

Underneath the numbers, the product mix is broadening beyond traditional transceivers, Lumentum is capturing early orders for ultra-high-power co-packaged optics lasers, external laser small form-factor modules, and near-packaged optics. Each of these products embeds the company deeper inside the AI server rack itself which expands the addressable content per system.

**Key Risks Going Forward.**

1. **Extreme Customer Concentration Risk:** The vast majority of Lumentum’s top-line acceleration is concentrated across a small handful of cloud hyperscalers and key partners. Any unexpected delay in AI data center buildouts, inventory adjustments, or allocation shifts from these core clients would significantly impact revenue.
2. **Surging Capex & Inventory Build:** Capex rose to $167 million in the quarter as management aggressively expanded manufacturing capacity and secured Indium Phosphide (InP) substrate supplies. Simultaneously, total inventory grew by $59 million, tying up balance-sheet cash.
3. **High Execution Risk on Complex Ramps:** The company’s long-term growth story depends heavily on flawless manufacturing execution as it scales Optical Circuit Switching (OCS), 1.6T transceivers, and Near-Packaged Optics (NPO). Any yield issues during these technical transitions could quickly erode gross margins.
4. **Valuation Compression Risk:** With the stock trading at elevated valuation multiples following its multi-quarter rally, the market has priced in seamless operational execution, leaving zero margin for error if supply delays occur.

**Coherent (COHR): Strong Fundamentals Meet Sell-the-News Pressure**

**What is going right?** Q4 revenue reached a record $2.05 billion, up 33.8% year over year, topping consensus, with non-GAAP EPS surging 74% to $1.74 against market expectations in the $1.43 to $1.62 range. The Datacenter and Communications division grew roughly 59% year over year to $1.62 billion and now represents 79% of total company revenue, with the company actively ramping 1.6T optical transceivers and co-packaged optics modules for next-generation AI clusters.

Management said FY2027 capacity is effectively sold out, customer orders now stretch into calendar 2028, and long-term supply agreements extend through 2030. Guidance followed suit, with Q1 revenue guided to $2.2 billion to $2.4 billion and non-GAAP EPS to $1.85 to $2.05, both above consensus.

**What is going wrong?** These are the reasons behind the post earnings sell off. The legacy Industrial segment fell roughly 15.8% year over year to $430.5 million, which acts as a drag on consolidated margins even as the datacenter business compounds. Capital intensity is climbing, with annual capital expenditure swelling to $1.1 billion and total inventory expanding to $2.58 billion. Investors are reasonably asking what happens to working capital efficiency if the pace of the AI buildout moderates, since inventory built for a 2027 ramp becomes a problem if the ramp slips.

The other explanation for the decline is positioning considering Coherent is already up 200% over the past 12 months. The market prices these names for perfection so even a beat and raise met institutional profit taking rather than new buying.

### The Neoclouds

**Nebius (NBIS)**: the biggest beat of the week. Revenue $582.3M, up 454%, versus $573.8M expected. Adjusted loss of $0.12 versus $0.67 expected, an enormous beat. Positive adjusted EBITDA of $285.7 million, AI cloud revenue up 514%, full-year outlook reaffirmed. Stock is now $277.05, up ~43.52%.

The large short interest sitting at 28.8% of the float which equals three days of buying power added fuel to the rally. This made the squeeze violent as it had already fallen roughly 36% from the June ATH’s of $299.86 going into the print.

**CoreWeave (CRWV)**: Revenue was $2.575B versus $2.56B expected, up 112% year over year. Adjusted loss $1.03 versus $1.20 expected. Stock up 14% after hours, then up 19% Wednesday to $106.37.

The backlog number sits at a staggering $104 billion, and that excludes over $25 billion in new commitments from Q3. In-quarter they announced Meta spending an additional $21 billion, a multi-year Anthropic agreement, and a $6 billion commitment from Jane Street. Q3 guidance of $3.4B to $3.6B. Notably, CEO Michael Intrator addressed the competition directly, “we’re seeing demand, pricing and margin all expanding,” even with SpaceX selling excess capacity and Meta considering its own cloud. However losses widened materially with a net loss of $626 million, up from $290 million a year ago, on **climbing debt financing costs**, and operating expenses more than doubled, marginally exceeding revenue.

That is the credit fragility showing going into 2027 that we have discussed previously with higher for longer interest rates really start biting as lenders will start demanding higher rates to finance data center debt.

### **Supermicro**

**(SMCI):** Up 13.4% to $35.83, was the largest single-day move in the group outside Nebius. We don’t view this move in Supermicro as a reason to own the stock, of course it’s going to run when the neoclouds beat on earnings, especially because it is one of the most heavily shorted names in the space. Supermicro is now facing tougher competition from Dell, HPE and Penguin Solutions for the same integration work but these names have far better balance sheets and obviously cleaner reputations.

### The 500 Billion MOU

Nvidia signed memorandums of understanding with Apollo, BlackRock, Blackstone, Brookfield, Goldman Sachs and KKR to establish independent compute financing platforms, targeting more than $500 billion of third-party capital mobilized over time. Huang disclosed that Nvidia approached exactly six firms and that none declined. The scale of the coalition is without precedent in technology: BlackRock is the world’s largest asset manager at roughly $15.3 trillion, Blackstone runs more than $1.3 trillion, Brookfield more than $1 trillion, Apollo roughly $1.05 trillion as of June 30. Goldman is the only traditional bank in the group.

Nvidia is not doing the lending here. The six firms independently underwrite and deploy through their own platforms, evaluating customer credit, demand, utilization, cash flow and residual equipment value. The $500 billion is aggregate capital the platforms are designed to mobilize over time, not Nvidia revenue and not a committed fund. The partnerships remain subject to execution of final agreements. In Nvidia’s own words, the pitch is that “compute is revenue” and that its hardware is “an investable asset” with the lowest token cost, highest revenue and longest life.

What is being proposed is that GPU compute becomes a financeable asset class in the way that toll roads, energy facilities and commercial real estate are financeable. Those assets share characteristics that make lenders comfortable: long useful lives, predictable cash flows, and residual values that hold up. GPUs are a tough sell here as the useful economic life is an area of intense debate and residual value has never been tested in a downmarket or even just a slow down. To make matters worse the cash flows underneath are contracts with counterparties who are themselves mostly unprofitable. Nvidia has obvious self interest in promoting the long life narrative around GPU’s and having them financed in this manner.

To put this $500 billion in perspective, the total AI related debt issuance in 2026 alone is projected to reach roughly $570 billion. While total data center capital expenditure is tracking near $850 billion. So this MOU even if fully signed, sealed and delivered would fall short of covering one year of the buildout and likely far less considering hyperscaler FCF has gone negative and greater percentage of capex would be financed by debt moving forward. Even Moody’s has warned that unprecedented capex is squeezing free cash flow and pushing the large technology companies into heavier debt loads. BlackRock’s AI Infrastructure Partnership had already set initial targets of up to $100 billion including debt financing, and its Global Infrastructure Partners arm closed a $40 billion acquisition of Aligned Data Centers on July 21. The financing push also arrives immediately after a July swoon in which investors started asking openly whether the AI capex would pay back, and after reports that Nvidia was in talks to guarantee financing for a quarter-trillion-dollar data center for OpenAI.

Now to circle back to CoreWeave. The financing cost is what is moving in the wrong direction, and it is moving in the wrong direction while credit conditions are still fairly accommodive and while a coalition of the largest asset managers on earth are actively competing to lend to this sector. This is about as good as the terms are going to get. The risks are the unknows that lie ahead in 2027 and beyond which could prevent this non binding MOU from even materializing.

This MOU would serve to move an enormous quantity of AI capex risk out of corporate balance sheets and into private credit vehicles, insurance capital and pension funds, where this debt is harder to see and harder to mark. Private credit could make a fortune off of the large fees they book from repackaging and selling these assets. Private credit earns fees on origination and distribution regardless of how the underlying assets perform, and the larger the sector becomes the harder it is to let fail. To the conspiratorial minded one could see an already troubled private credit industry deliberately attempting to make AI debt ‘too big to fail’ in order to have Uncle Sam backstop their poor risky decisions.

While none of this is a 2026 problem, it becomes a problem when the first cohort of these facilities comes up for refinancing into a rate environment that has not cooperated, a slowing consumer that contracts hyperscaler advertising FCF further against ageing GPU collateral held by borrowers who still are not generating cash. That is the uncertainty we face in 2027.
