# Aschenbrenner and Baker’s Q2 2026 13F: What Was a Decision and What Was Drift

On August 14, the June 30 positions of two funds were disclosed on the same day. Leopold Aschenbrenner’s Situational Awareness had pulled off almost all of the roughly $8.4B in puts it carried through the end of March in a single quarter, and Gavin Baker’s Atreides went the other way and doubled its QQQ put contracts [1][2][3][4]. A month later, on July 29, semiconductor and optics names all bottomed together, and while the index fell a bit over 10% that day, SanDisk was down 55% and Astera Labs 48%. This piece is part two of the August 1 article, and it goes back to the SEC originals to see where each of them cut the AI infrastructure value chain and how each reads it. On Baker’s side I looked at one more thing. The speaker list for the TomorrowX Summit he co-hosts in Austin in November overlaps substantially with the top names in this 13F [5]. To put my lead up front, I think the part of Baker’s 13F actually worth reading is less the list of top holdings and more the stretch he did not touch.

## 1. Two 13F Filings Opened on August 14

August 14 was the 13F deadline. The rule is that you file your holdings as of June 30 within 45 days, so every quarter the 13Fs of US institutions all open at once on this day. Berkshire came out that day, so did Druckenmiller, but the ones I looked at with interest were two.

The reasons being

- AI and AI infrastructure and related tech stocks make up most of my brokerage
- Memory, optics, semis, related names, a wide range of stocks that both 13Fs hold
- I see these two as the two masters of the tech long. Leopold Aschenbrenner in particular is someone I think looks carefully at bottlenecks and invests by seeing straight through to those points, and he did it successfully. (Leaving the leverage aside, of course.) Everyone knows this, but he left OpenAI and set up Situational Awareness in 2024, and reportedly grew assets under management from around $200M at launch to tens of billions in two years.

Gavin Baker ran the Fidelity OTC fund for a long time before starting Atreides, and he keeps up an active presence in online communities, so these days he has exchanges with real depth with all sorts of tech industry experts, tech stock supporters and analysts. There’s an event that’s been getting a lot of promotion lately, where he co-hosts something called the TomorrowX Summit under his own name. It’s held in Austin in November, and looking at the speaker list, I get the sense there aren’t many tech longs who can pull semiconductor company CEOs together like that [5].

Part one on these two was written on August 1. In Compute Short, Memory Long: Aschenbrenner’s Unwind, Gavin Baker’s Hold I opened the March 31 filings and covered why compute sells expensive and memory sells cheap. When I wrote that piece, I was looking at why Leopold built his positions the way he did, and by coincidence Baker’s portfolio was also sitting on tech stocks in a fairly similar way, so I found it interesting to analyze.

This piece is the picture from the next quarter.

But the situation of these two has now split completely. Leopold is reported to have had his public equity book forcibly liquidated in July, and Baker is still riding high (there is no public data on the fund returns themselves, so this is just my impression). That said, people say Leopold blew up, but his year to date return is still above the average person’s. I thought his attempt was a bold and admirable challenge, but looking at it now with hindsight it was a reckless challenge, and it feels like he got brutally trampled.

SEC EDGAR filing index for both funds, 2026-08-14

All the numbers were pulled directly from the info table originals posted at the SEC. The aggregator sites were still showing end of March data as the latest as of yesterday, so to check I downloaded and parsed the original XML, and that work took half a day. Thankfully the verification came out clean. The implied unit price you get by dividing reported value by share count matched the June 30 close for every name [6]. Micron $1,154.29, Ciena $490.56, QQQ $736.40. Which means the parsing was not wrong.

Let me draw one distinction up front. What a 13F shows is a slice cut out of the book. Only US listed equity longs and options appear, and shorts, non US assets, cash and private stakes all drop out. So when I talk about weights below they are all on a reported value basis, and the weight in the actual book could be smaller than this. That gap becomes the raw material of this piece in sections 4 and 7.

## 2. Aschenbrenner’s June 30

The thing that stood out most in the March 31 filing was the puts. Around $2.0B on the SMH semiconductor ETF, around $1.5B on NVIDIA, roughly $1B each on Oracle, Broadcom and AMD. Add Micron, TSMC, ASML, Intel and Corning on top of that and it comes to around $8.4B on an underlying market value basis [4]. It was a double structure, long memory and power with puts draped across semiconductors broadly.

Open the June 30 filing and this is gone.

The only put left is Infosys, and that one is around $5M [3]. Everything else disappeared, and Micron and TSMC flipped completely from puts to common. Nothing is public about what judgment happened in between. Someone who held 1.72M Micron puts in March holds 4.83M Micron common shares in June.

So what came in to fill that space. The answer is two memory names. SanDisk at around $5.7B and Micron at around $5.6B, together 55.6% of reported value. After that comes Bloom Energy at around $1.9B, TSMC at around $1.3B, Nebius at around $1.2B, then CoreWeave, Core Scientific, STMicro, Applied Digital, RIOT, IREN [3]. It’s a lineup that runs through power and neoclouds and bitcoin miners, and the big picture is the inputs to compute. It’s a layout that says buy electricity and memory and compute comes out.

Situational Awareness Q1 2026 13F info table, put rows highlighted

The biggest empty seat in this filing is optics. There isn’t a single name. No coherent equipment, no transceivers, no optical DSP, not even connectors. It carries the same AI infrastructure fund label but the connection layer is completely empty. This comes back later as the biggest difference when we compare with Baker.

This picture is right before the liquidation. On June 30 he had all the hedges pulled off and 55% loaded into two names, and you all know what happened in July. It’s said to have been a forced liquidation.

## 3. Baker’s June 30

Atreides’ total reported value jumped from $5.0B at the end of March to $14.3B at the end of June [1][2]. The number of holdings actually went down, from 54 to 49. So what came in to make it grow like that. Yes, as you’ve probably guessed, SpaceX.

27.33M shares, around $4.6B, 32.6% of reported value. It’s the single largest position, and it is more than five times the gap to Micron, the second largest on a common stock basis [1].

I’ve held SpaceX myself since the IPO, and I’ve kept writing in support of the earnings analysis and the compute rental business. What I laid out in SpaceX’s First Earnings Report: Revisiting the S-1, and the Hardware Value Chain Behind 10GW is that story, and my view is that this company is closer to an AI company than a space company. I see it as an AI company wearing a space costume. I don’t think the reason Baker loaded 32% into it is the orbital launch business either.

The second largest is the QQQ put. Around $2.3B on 3.2M shares, 16.4% of reported value. He more than doubled it on a contract basis from 1.4M shares at the end of March [1][2].

This part is interesting, because in the second quarter QQQ went from $577 to $736, up a full 27.6% [6]. While the index was melting up like that, he doubled his index puts. One of them pulled every hedge off and the other doubled his hedge, and both of them are AI infrastructure bulls.

The rest of the top names are Micron at around $800M, Cerebras common at around $600M plus around $100M in calls, Meta calls at around $600M plus around $200M in common, Astera Labs at around $500M, Ciena at around $300M, Credo at around $300M, Coherent at around $200M [1].

Layer by layer comparison of the July 29 trough drawdown. QQQ and NVDA against the AI hardware names

The lows clustered on July 29. Almost every name bottomed together that day, but the size of the drawdown was completely different by layer. Measured against the June 30 close, while QQQ fell 10.1% and NVIDIA 5.0%, SanDisk fell 55.3%, Astera Labs 48.3%, Bloom Energy 45.9%, and Coherent 43.7% [6].

At the time I was studying the representative coherent optics players in order to look into Scale-Across names, and the piece I wrote is exactly Down 40%, 40%, and 14% in Six Weeks from last July, and the moment of publication was the middle of this correction, with the actual bottom coming ten days after that piece.

Up to here is the range anyone can see by skimming public material. The top holdings list and the drawdown table go up on the aggregator sites within a day or two.

There is a separate reason I downloaded the original XML and calculated every share count delta. The share count increases for Ciena and Coherent and Nokia and Semtech were the same down to the second decimal place. What that means, and once you know it, is what separates judgment from plain drift in this 13F.

## 4. What the +1.73% Is

I put the June 30 share counts next to March 31 and pulled the increase rates. The names below all grew by the same number.

Ciena +1.73%, Coherent +1.73%, Nokia +1.73%, Semtech +1.74%, Rogers +1.73%, Veeco +1.71%, Tesla +1.73%, Ambiq +1.73%, ACV Auctions +1.73%, Compass +1.73%, Warby Parker +1.73%, Taboola +1.73%, BitGo +1.74%, Amazon +1.69%, Nebius +1.68% [1][2].

Fifteen names moving together down to the second decimal place does not come out of name by name judgment. It’s the mark left when new money comes in and gets allocated proportionally across the whole existing sleeve (author estimate, the size of the inflow itself is not in the 13F so it can’t be backed out). Put simply, these fifteen names held their existing weights almost exactly as they were. Share counts did go up, so there must have been trades, but it doesn’t look like a judgment made by picking names. That said, there are paths other than an inflow that make the same pattern. A new account coming in all at once, or a model portfolio allocated in bulk, would print like this too. Which one it is is hard to judge from the 13F.

So the connection layer names that fell into this cluster were neither bought more nor sold more. I wonder whether much meaning should be attached to it, but one thing does remain. The fact that he was holding names that had run up this much in the second quarter, exactly as they were. Coherent went from $238 at the end of March to $394 at the end of June, up 65.6%, and Semtech went from $76 to $161, up 110.5% [6]. Both of them are names inside the cluster we just saw. The pressure to take profits would have been considerable in that stretch and he didn’t touch them. That said, it wasn’t the whole connection layer. In that same layer, Lumentum alone went out entirely.

So what did he actually decide.

He put SpaceX in new at 32.6%, doubled the QQQ put contracts, cut the Astera Labs share count by 63.9%, and sold Lumentum outright [1][2]. On top of that come increases of 56% in Palo Alto Networks, 162% in CoreWeave and 124% in Wix, and Amphenol, GitLab, Cipher, Samsara and PDF Solutions came in new. He emptied twenty names completely, worth around $1.1B in total on end of March value, and among those Lumentum at around $180M was the single largest liquidation.

Atreides Q1 to Q2 share count delta. Separating the pro rata cluster from the active decisions

There is one more thing to be careful about when reading a 13F here. SpaceX and Cerebras look like “new buys” and they may not be.

A 13F only covers US listed securities, so shares bought while a company was private don’t show up in this table. The moment a company lists, they suddenly appear as if they were a new position. The Cerebras case lets you confirm this. Looking at the Schedule 13G that Atreides filed on the same day, it beneficially owns 10.54M shares in total, and 6.73M of those are Class B common [7]. Class B is reportedly the stock you receive in pre IPO rounds, and it converts one for one into Class A at any time. The 3.11M shares printed in the 13F are just a fragment, a little under 30% of the whole.

This got long, so to pull it together, the reason reported value grew almost threefold this quarter is closer to a result of private positions he had held for a long time becoming visible as they listed. Read a 13F as a purchase record and you get it badly wrong right here.

## 5. Splitting Interconnect Into Layers

Now let’s do the optics story properly. I divided the June 30 values on a distance basis.

Inside the rack, which is the scale up band, there is Astera Labs at $588M, Credo at $307M, Amphenol at $113M and Rogers at $31M. Together that’s around $1.0B.

In the scale out band, the optical transceiver zone inside the building, there is Coherent at $253M, Semtech at $89M and Veeco at $18M, so around $360M.

In the scale across band that links building to building, the coherent DCI zone, there is Ciena at $338M and Nokia at $34M, so around $372M [1].

Interconnect as a whole is 12.4% of reported value, and scale across is 2.6%. Scale up is close to three times heavier. That said, this number is not revenue exposure, it is the sum of values I got by assigning names to layers myself. A company like Coherent that straddles two layers I counted only once.

To sort out the terms once, these three layers together are what I mean by the connection layer. Within it, the pure optics that actually make and receive light are Lumentum and Coherent, and Ciena and Nokia are systems vendors with coherent optics on top. Credo has AEC and SerDes as its mainstay, so it does optical DSP as well but grouping it as optics is a stretch, while Astera Labs is electrical connection, Amphenol is connectors, Rogers is materials and Veeco is equipment.

Atreides interconnect sleeve by distance layer

Back on July 10, when I wrote One Layer Below SemiAnalysis’s Meta Map: Scale-Across Is Coherent, Not CPO, I set out that the CPO fight inside the rack and the coherent fight between campuses are different markets, and I saw demand opening on the latter side. I still hold that judgment itself. But if you ask me whether this 13F backs up that argument, honestly only partly. To write that he bet on scale across, he would have had to take Ciena and Nokia actively, and those two are inside the +1.73% cluster we just saw. The side he did touch was Astera Labs on scale up, and in the direction of cutting at that.

I wrote in July that this layer is opening, so I feel the pull to read this 13F in a way that favors that, and I shouldn’t. It runs against my argument, so I’m writing it down as it is instead.

So why is the scale up weight still high.

Scale across is optics without question and that much is obvious, but scale out is optics to a degree as well. Split by distance there are three, but seen by the area optics eats, the boundary is blurry.

But you shouldn’t read the reason scale up is three times heavier as him having bought that side this quarter. Astera Labs cut its share count by 63.9% and the position value still grew from $369M to $588M. The stock went up 4.4 times in the second quarter. Credo’s share count moved only 3.5% and its value tripled. The money that actually went in new to this layer this quarter is Amphenol at $113M, about that much.

The accumulated weight itself was built in earlier quarters. It’s possible to read that as a layout weighted toward raising computing power through connection rather than GPU compute itself. If the judgment is that packing the chips inside a rack more tightly is worth more money than adding one more rack, then the combination of Astera Labs and Credo and Amphenol is exactly that layout.

There’s one more thing that catches the eye. There is no Marvell and no Broadcom in this person’s filing [1][2]. The two big merchants that sell silicon for optical interconnect are empty, and instead it’s scattered across systems (Ciena, Nokia), devices and modules (Coherent, Semtech), SerDes and AEC (Credo), and equipment (Veeco). Marvell fell 25.5% after June 30, so as it turned out, not holding it was better [6].

So the scale up exposure itself is still large, but the decisions he actually touched this quarter were not in the direction of growing that side further. The accumulated weight and this quarter’s movement are out of line with each other, and how to read that gap is still homework for me too.

## 6. Why Only Lumentum Was Dropped

Lumentum, 267,389 shares, around $188M as of the end of March, 3.76% of reported value. On June 30 it is zero [1][2].

Honestly I don’t really know about this part. So I took the explanations that came to mind and threw them against the data one by one.

The first thing I suspected was that he sold because it had run up too much, but when I calculated it there’s no clear answer. Line up the second quarter gains within his connection layer names and it goes Astera Labs 340.7%, Credo 189.7%, Veeco 123.9%, Semtech 110.5%, Coherent 65.6%, Nokia 65.2%, Rogers 52.5%, then below that QQQ at 27.6%, then Ciena 26.4%, and right at the bottom Lumentum at 22.1% [6]. It’s the name that went up least in the connection layer, and it went up less than the index. For profit taking, the reason to sell isn’t clear.

The next thing I suspected was that he needed cash. What he made by emptying twenty names is around $1.1B and Lumentum was the largest piece of that, so if raising funds was the goal, it explains. But as we saw in section 4, the big chunk of what’s new this quarter is existing positions revealed by listing, so the amount of cash that actually went out would have been much smaller. In which case he wouldn’t have been in a situation needing quick money either. Why optics of all places, and only one name at that, still doesn’t come apart.

I also thought about the explanation that it overlaps with Coherent so he consolidated into one, but if that were it he should have added to Coherent. That one was sitting at +1.73% too.

Atreides Q1 2026 13F, Lumentum row

I’m on the long side on Lumentum. High power lasers are a must, so I’m curious why he took it out. Looking at the weights by layer it could mean he sees scale up, that is copper connection, as more important, but even so, emptying the light source completely is a different story.

Judging only by the results so far, this decision was wrong. From June 30 to August 14 Lumentum is up 7.9%, the only plus in the connection layer. Over the same period Astera Labs fell 33.4%, Coherent 17.4% and Nokia 19.0% [6]. The FQ4 that came out on August 11 crossed $1B in quarterly revenue for the first time and printed a 36.6% non-GAAP operating margin. I laid that out in Lumentum’s First $1B Quarter: FQ4 2026 Earnings Analysis. Those are numbers that came after he sold, but if he hadn’t sold, this would be the one place he’d be smiling right now.

For reference, Irrational Analysis on X also agrees with most of this 13F but left a comment saying to liquidate Coherent and swap into Lumentum [8]. He’s been a Lumentum long guy from way back so you have to account for that, but the direction is the same as mine. The reasoning behind his call isn’t written in that post, so I don’t know any more than that either.

## 7. What the Two Filings Share, and Cerebras at 10.7%

There are a few names the two filings share. Micron, CoreWeave, Nebius, Cerebras [1][3]. But the weights are completely different. Micron is around $5.6B for Leopold and around $800M for Baker, and Baker cut the share count by 6.5%. Nebius is a new entry at around $1.2B for Leopold, while Baker just leaves a residual 60,000 share position sitting at +1.68%. CoreWeave, Baker grew by 162%, so this one is a real add. What exactly CoreWeave and Nebius do is something I’ve never dug into deeply, so I’ll withhold judgment.

Cerebras is a bit different.

It looked like fresh accumulation, which struck me as odd, but as we saw in section 4 it’s a pre IPO position with 6.73M Class B shares attached. 10.54M shares on a Class A converted basis, 10.7% of shares outstanding. It’s a stake over 5% so a 13G was filed separately, and a 13G is a declaration that you hold with no intent to get involved in control [7]. It’s closer to a stake in the company than a trading position.

So what does he see in Cerebras. Is he looking at SRAM and the wafer scale engine as the way to break the memory wall. The structure uses a whole wafer as a single chip and gets around the bandwidth problem with on chip SRAM, so logically it does line up. But this is my speculation, and the ownership percentage alone can’t reconstruct his argument.

## 8. Baker’s TomorrowX Event

There is already a place set up to hear his argument.

On the TomorrowX Summit speaker list mentioned in section 1 there is Andrew Feldman of Cerebras. Jitendra Mohan of Astera Labs is there too. The event’s own description says public company CEOs are interviewed by the investors who own them [5]. Which means he is putting the founder of a company he holds 10.7% of on a stage he built and asking him directly. The overlap between the top 13F names and the speaker list is probably no coincidence.

TomorrowX Summit 2026 speaker page

The Astera Labs side is a bit curious. He cut the share count by 63.9% and put that company’s CEO on the stage. It’s a spot where you could read the cut as being about valuation rather than the company, and this is my speculation too.

My conclusion is this. Both of them bought AI infrastructure but the point at which they cut the chain was opposite. Leopold took the inputs, memory and electricity, unhedged on a reported basis, and Baker left the connection layer as it was and put a put on the index. That’s where July split them as well. But how much that hedge actually worked can’t be calculated from a 13F. Strike and expiry are not reported items. What can be confirmed is only the difference in drawdown. In July the index fell 10% and his names fell 30 to 50%, so insurance bought on the index would have had a hard time covering all of that hole.

That said, there’s a chance I’m reading this picture wrong. If in the next 13F the Ciena and Coherent share counts move by the same rate as all the other names once again, that means it’s closer to inertia than to an opinion about this layer. It becomes a much duller story than what I read out of it in section 4. If Lumentum comes back in, section 6 has to be written again from scratch. And if the scale up and scale across weights are the same next quarter as they are now, then my July argument that coherent DCI demand is opening gets no support, at least from this fund’s layout.

Let me write down the dates to check. November 16 is the Q3 13F deadline, November 17 and 18 are TomorrowX, and somewhere in between is Ciena’s next quarter earnings. The Q3 13F will print trades from the July correction window for the first time. Whether he was buying or selling on July 29 is something we can know then.
