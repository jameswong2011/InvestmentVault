## 1. An Update After Four Months

On April 11 I wrote *1Y +122%: What Happens When a SiPh Engineer Only Buys What He’s Touched* and disclosed all 40 or so tickers across three accounts. At the end of that piece I wrote “next update: Q3.” This is the piece that keeps that promise.

On a trailing one year basis ending August 17, the combined return across the three accounts is +124.25%. That moved about 2pp from the +122.29% I wrote in April.

Looking at this number alone, it seems like nothing happened for four months. But open the account records and it is the opposite. So instead of walking through allocations ticker by ticker like April, this piece tries to focus on what actually happened during those four months.

So the material for this piece is the records underneath rather than the account screens. I downloaded a full year of deposits and withdrawals plus 1,600 executions, and matched them one by one against the sentences I wrote in April. Whether a ticker I said I would “keep watching” four months ago is still in the account, what orders I actually placed when the drawdown came, and whether anything came in and went out unnoticed between the two disclosures.

For reference, on August 11 and 12 I moved a bit over 20% of the accounts out to a CMA account. That was money for another purpose and it had nothing to do with a market judgment. So part of the balance curve coming down from the June high is the portion I took out. The return numbers in this piece are calculated with all of those flows stripped out.

## 3. How to Read These Numbers

Before laying out more numbers, it seems right to state the calculation method first. The same account can produce different returns.

The +124.25% that shows on the broker screen is a money weighted return. When and how much you put in gets reflected in the result. Money put in during September compounded for a year while money put in during July only compounded for a month, and this method puts that difference into the calculation.

The value from chaining the table above is +132.84%. Because it links monthly Modified Dietz returns, it is an approximation of a strict time weighted return, and months where a large amount came in mid month, like July, carry some error. This one reduces the influence of deposit and withdrawal timing, but what is left is not only stock selection. Position sizing, trade timing, and how much cash was held are all mixed in.

There is a reason the two diverged by more than 8pp. I kept adding money from March through July, and it was with an account grown that large that I took a -30% month. The weight of a bad month taken with large capital late outweighed the good months made with small capital early. That is why the dollar based result comes out lower than the stock picking result.

And, in order to compare my results, I tried comparing against the semiconductor ETF SMH. This was something a subscriber requested, and I thought it was a good reference so I prepared it again this time. If the same money went into SMH on the actual deposit dates and amounts, how much would it be now. That calculation is section 10.

There is one more thing I want to write down here. There are plenty of pieces that disclose returns, but most of them cut out the good stretch and show that. I did the same in April. I announced a one year return about halfway through a month where 58% had been added in a single month. Pieces like that leave the reader with very little after reading. Either envy or suspicion, one of the two.

What I want to do this time is the opposite of that. Account records do not allow you to keep only the bragging. What orders I placed in July, what I sold two weeks after writing that I was convinced in April, how many tickers I bought impulsively while writing a piece and how those ended, all of it is there with the dates. If you lay that out as it is, good judgment and things gained by luck and plain mistakes become distinguishable from one another.

I think what is useful from the reader’s side is that distinction. This is not to say you should buy my tickers. It is about seeing, through one person’s record, how long something written down as conviction actually holds. And then when you look at your own account, you will probably see something similar.

So from here on are the things that only come out when you open more than 1,000 executions. What I did in July, why the 15 tickers that were there in April disappeared, what trades do not show up in either of the two snapshots, and why I still hold the 35 that remain.

## 4. July: Zero Sells

This is one month of July execution history.

12 buys, 0 sells.

While it fell more than 30%, I did not sell a single share. And I added a bit over 10% of the July starting balance. Most of that went in between July 28 and 31.

Honestly, at a time like that the thought does come that you should take some off, but I believed the hyperscalers would keep spending and that the US government side would keep putting investment support into AI, and I did not think this was an AI bubble yet, so I kept betting.

Seen from a distance it was right, but in terms of short term trading results, the outcome was not a hundred points, though I do not regret it.

Return bars for the nine tickers bought in July, versus purchase price through August 17. No dollar axis.

My judgment at the time was that the selling was excessive. In *QQQ Comes Down to the 200-Day*, written on July 30, I laid out the situation with the index a bit over 2% away from its 200 day EMA, and the point when I was writing that piece is exactly the point when I was putting money in. I wrote the judgment down in a piece and executed it in the account in the same week.

One more thing overlapped here. At the end of July there were reports that Situational Awareness had liquidated its entire public equity position [1]. It was an AI focused fund run by Leopold Aschenbrenner, and as semiconductor prices slid it could not meet margin calls and handed its whole stock book to Citadel [1]. Assets that had once reached the 40 billion dollar range shrank to around 10 billion, and leverage was said to have been as high as 4x [1].

Why this matters is that a liquidation like that has nothing to do with corporate results. Supply that comes out because a fund collapsed is not supply that comes out because the name got worse. On top of that it was a fund concentrated in AI and semiconductors, so the supply likely poured out in the same places as the names I hold. If a good portion of the July drawdown had that character, then earnings were not damaged and it should come back, is how I saw it. That said, this is an inference I drew from a disclosed liquidation, and which names took how much was never made public.

The result is August +20.55%. But you should not take the whole account bouncing as the score for a buying judgment, so I measured only what I bought in July separately. Taking the total purchase cost of the nine tickers bought that month as the denominator, and using each ticker’s quantity weighted average fill price against the August 17 close, it comes to +29.9%. AXTI is +62%, LITE +48%, COHR +45%, SNDK +44% in that order, and even SIVEF, which rose the least, is in the +5% range. On timing alone, you could say it was right to some degree.

Still, two things have to be written alongside to be fair. One is that I did not call the bottom. The portion I put in first on July 9 kept falling afterwards and I watched it. Putting the rest in all at once at month end was also because, weighing the QQQ 200 EMA level mentioned above together with the market situation overall, I thought this was the time to go in.

The other is that not selling and buying well are different things. What the fact of zero sells in July proves is only that I did not get shaken, and that is not what produced the return.

Still, this record is worth keeping. Anyone can say “I did not sell” after the fact, but zero executions is a record you cannot manufacture after the fact.

## 5. The Homework April Left Behind

The April piece closed its last section with “what I am still debating.” Four months have passed, so I matched those items against the actual account.

Whether to keep holding ALMU. At the time it was −38.6%, the worst among the optical names, and I wrote that I would watch rather than add. It is not in the account now. I cleared it on April 15. I still acknowledge the scientific appeal of III-V on Si, but in the end I could not find evidence that the gap between academic promise and commercial execution was narrowing. I folded it four days after writing that I would keep watching, so that sentence was already close to a conclusion at the time.

ATEYY and TER, the two that broke the rule. I broke my personal rule of only buying equipment I have handled myself with these two, and I wrote that with returns near 0% it was not validated. Only TER remains now. I cleared ATEYY in pieces from mid April to early June. The reason is simple. Both are ATE and the thesis overlaps, and I could not find a reason to hold both. The one I kept is TER because it is a company that has already put out a photonics specific test line like Photon 100. Judging by results alone, TER is at around +35% now.

Whether I need to hold 6 test equipment names. In April I grouped FORM, KEYS, AEHR, ATEYY, TER, VIAV, counted six, and asked myself the question. But the classification itself was wrong on my part. With ATEYY cleared, it is now five: FORM, KEYS, AEHR, TER, VIAV. In terms of the number it came down one notch, but this homework is still closer to unsolved. VIAV occupies nothing more than a 0.24% slot, and if you ask why I hold it, I can only give the same answer as April, “money parked there to study.” The weight is still small so I plan to hold it for now.

Quantum exposure. This is an item I did not even write down as homework in April, but the outcome is the biggest. INFQ, XNDU, IONQ are all gone. I cleared all three between mid April and mid May. Considering I even previewed a deep dive on XNDU in the April piece, the direction changed quite a bit. It is not that my optical judgment changed. I saw the commercialization timing for quantum hardware as outside the range I can carry. And I judged that investing in other AI related infrastructure names instead of the quantum side was better.

Of the four items I wrote down for myself four months ago, two were answered by clearing them, one was solved halfway, and one went the opposite way from what I previewed.

## 6. The 15 That Disappeared: Judgment and Raising Cash

Writing out the names that were in the April piece and are not in the account now: POET, ATEYY, ALMU, CIEN, IQEPF, FPLSF, HIMX, INFQ, XNDU, IONQ, QCOM, WOLF, NVTS, WDC, JBL. The April piece named around forty tickers, and what remains across the three accounts now is thirty five. If you add and subtract what went out and what came in, one ticker does not reconcile, and that is because names too small to be written into the piece at the time are mixed in. The list in this piece is on an account record basis.

Misunderstanding can arise here so let me separate it out first. Disappearing does not mean the judgment changed in every case.

Between August 10 and 13 I sold close to 30% of the account across 19 tickers. That was selling to raise the CMA transfer money mentioned earlier. I trimmed evenly across the whole account. In order of size, MU, SIVEF, INTC, LITE, AAOI, GFS, TSEM, MRVL, COHR, AXTI, LWLG, AEHR, and the remaining seven were at leftover levels. In the process, names that were only leftovers like JBL, QCOM, WDC went to zero. It was selling closer to arithmetic than to judgment.

Everything folded by judgment finished between April and June. ALMU and ATEYY are written above, CIEN at the end of April, IQEPF and FPLSF and HIMX in mid April, and NVTS and WOLF around a similar time.

And then POET.

In the April piece POET was the second largest weight after TSLA, and the ticker I wrote had made PhotonCap what it is. It is not there now.

The trigger was two things overlapping.

One is the change in the situation on Marvell’s side. That matter was covered separately in *The Real Question Behind POET −47%*.

The other was bigger, and it was a problem with the company itself. On April 27 POET announced that all purchase orders received from Celestial AI had been cancelled [2]. Marvell, which acquired Celestial AI, gave written notice on April 23, and the reason given for the cancellation was that POET had disclosed purchase order and shipping information externally in contravention of its confidentiality obligations [2]. And the person who made that disclosure is reported to have been the CFO [3]. A securities class action followed afterwards [3].

This is not a story about the technology being wrong or the market not arriving. Orders that would have turned into actual revenue were lost because an officer did not keep a contract the company was obliged to keep. I look at a problem at this layer differently from technology risk. A roadmap slipping is something you can wait out, but a company where management’s judgment loses a customer contract can have the same thing happen again. If I saw that in a name I had put 6.1% into four months earlier, I thought vacating the seat was the right call.

Writing the record as it is, I actually bought more POET right after publishing the April piece. On April 14 and 21, in two rounds. And I cleared the whole thing at the end of April. I reversed direction within two weeks.

Summarizing this as “my judgment changed” makes it too tidy a phrase. The fact that a 6.1% weight of conviction went to zero in two weeks also means the size of the conviction I wrote in April was larger than it actually was.

## 8. What the Two Snapshots Do Not Show

If you put only the April holdings list and the current holdings list side by side, four months look fairly tidy. In reality there were 350 executions in between.

Of those, sixteen tickers do not show up in either one. I bought and sold them, and they came in and went out between the two snapshots.

On the nuclear side, CEG, VST, BE. On the laser and glass substrate side, LPKFF, LASR, NYNOF, HSYDF, NASA. And CRWV, DELL, ORCL, DRAM, RNECY, KORU, RPBPF, TSLL. Sixteen tickers.

Adding up the sixteen tickers comes to −5.8% against purchase cost.

The biggest loss was LPKFF at −25.7%, then NASA −25.2%, CEG −19.6%, HSYDF −17.9% in that order. CEG and VST and HSYDF and NASA were bought together on May 26 and cleared together on June 9, and all of them were losses. There is a winning side too. TSLL at +28.9%, RNECY at +11%.

Dividing the realized loss by the current account value comes to around 1.5%, so it is not a large amount. But the character of it bothers me. TSLL is a 2x leveraged Tesla ETF. The title of the April piece was “only buys what he’s touched,” and over those four months I put my hands on nuclear names and a leveraged ETF and a Korea index ETF, and mostly lost.

Most of these trades overlap with the periods when I was writing on those subjects. I wrote a nuclear piece and bought nuclear names, wrote glass substrate and laser pieces and bought that side. When you study a subject you start wanting to buy it, but having studied something and having conviction are different. The tickers I sold back within two weeks show that difference. As a writer I have no regrets on this point, but on the trading side I am reflecting. I learned again that entry timing matters too.

If I had written this piece without this item, the before and after table would have been much cleaner. But I put it in anyway for the sake of the review.

## 9. What Remains: Why I Still Hold Them Four Months Later

That covers what went out and what came in. The remaining side goes layer by layer. Instead of copying over the reasoning from the April piece, I only write whether that reasoning still stands now.

**Substrate and materials.** Only SLOIF (Soitec) and AXTI remain. The SOI wafer is the main platform for telecom SiPh. There are other routes like SiN or bulk silicon, but on the datacenter silicon waveguide side SOI is the default, and Soitec’s position is not the kind of thing that gets shaken within four months. The piece that covered this company on its own is *The Wafer That Used to Roll Around My Lab Is Now an AI Data Center Bottleneck: Soitec and the Investment Case for the Photonics-SOI Monopoly*. It is in the +105% range now. AXTI is InP substrates, and the structure where commercial O-band lasers are usually built on InP substrates is unchanged. It is not that there is no route for putting III-V directly on silicon, but what is used in volume production right now is the InP substrate side. The geopolitical risk from the China production share is unchanged as well. The piece written separately on its first quarter results is *A Company With $88M in Annual Revenue Just Got a $6B Market Cap: What AXTI’s Q1 Changed, and What It Didn’t*. By clearing ALMU, the only negative in this layer in April, I have left only the two that sit in positions that cannot be replaced.

**Foundry.** TSEM and GFS. In April I wrote that I had moved a substantial part of the GFS money into TSEM, and I kept that direction. TSEM is at +84% now and GFS at +54%. The reason for keeping Tower is the same as April. Unlike TSMC or GFS, it is a specialty foundry that has established itself in analog, mixed signal, and photonics specialized processes. The piece that put the two companies’ 300mm expansions side by side is *The More Silicon Wins, the More InP Sells: Why UMC and Tower Both Expanded 300mm on the Same Day*, and the thesis that money reaches the foundry layer first was covered in *Everyone Saw a Laser Shortage. The Money Went to the Foundries First.*

**Optical components.** By weight, this is the center of the portfolio. LITE at 7.9% is the largest single optical name, then SIVEF 5.0%, GLW 3.2%, AAOI 2.2%, LPTH 2.5%, COHR 1.6%, LWLG 0.8%.

The reason I hold LITE the largest is exactly what I wrote in April. In the major CPO designs out now, the optical engine goes inside the same package as the switch ASIC or the accelerator, and the laser tends to be pulled out to an external light source module so it can be replaced. There are also implementations that put the laser inside, so it is not settled as one thing. When that happens, light sources that used to be scattered one per module get concentrated into a small number of high power units. There are not many companies that have low noise, high power, and the ability to stamp that out in volume all at once. Even after checking the fiscal fourth quarter results in early August, I am keeping this judgment. I wrote about this company separately in *A Conversation with Lumentum*.

SIVEF is on the InP laser and GaAs VCSEL side, and I wrote in April that small cap liquidity was a risk, and in July I took that risk exactly as written. It is also still the case that a single trade carries a large fee since it is Sweden listed. Even so, I think I should hold at least one option in the light source layer other than the two large names. The structure where distance and use case split by light source type was laid out in *The AI Light Source War Is Not a Speed Race: InP, VCSEL, and μLED Are Buying Different Distances*.

GLW is now hard to see as a fiber company alone. The glass carriers for temporary bonding are already in volume production, and the core substrate side is still in development. Even taking as a premise that the two stages are different, the judgment that its place inside AI infrastructure has widened has actually gotten stronger since April. The fiber side bottleneck was covered in *InP Makes the Light. Fiber Carries It: Same Cycle, Different Bottleneck*, and the glass substrate side in *[Investment Map] 15 Companies in the Glass Substrate Cycle: From Material to Mass Production*.

How I went from being skeptical of AAOI at first to changing my mind is written in *$AAOI, From $10 to $150: How a Cable TV Parts Company Became an AI Optics Stock*.

LPTH is a ticker I wrote up in April as “very small, still studying,” and it is now at 2.5%. The trigger was looking into the supply structure on the IR optics side while writing *America Needs to Cut Chinese Germanium: Why Loss-Making IR Optics Company LPTH Trades at P/S 16x* in May.

COHR and LWLG have smaller seats now, but the reasons are the same as April. The quarter where Coherent crossed from transceiver player to platform was covered in *$20B+ New SAM on Top of +27% Pro Forma Revenue: The Quarter Coherent Crossed from Transceiver Player to Platform*, and LWLG’s TAM announcement and the share price reaction in *[$LWLG] The Day a $29K Revenue Company Raised Its TAM to $47B, and the Stock Dropped*.

**Assembly, process, and test.** BESIY alone handles the assembly side. The thesis that you need high precision bonding equipment to place dies onto an optical interposer at micrometer level is unchanged, but with POET cleared I have to admit the basis for this seat is weaker than in April. On the process equipment side there is VECO. It is a company that makes MOCVD and ion beam deposition equipment, and since it is used in III-V epi processes it is a seat with exposure to light source materials production. I wrote in *Veeco Q1 2026: Behind the MOCVD Thesis, Spector IBD Was the Real SiPh Lever* that in the first quarter results the Spector IBD side was a bigger lever than MOCVD. It is around +35% now.

Metrology and inspection is five. AEHR at +392% is the highest return in the entire portfolio, then FORM at +80%, TER +35%, KEYS +25%, VIAV +12%. AEHR holds a narrow and hard to replace seat in WLT burn in (*Aehr Test Systems Q3 FY2026: The Burn-In Proxy for Silicon Photonics HVM*), and FORM is in a position where competing products are hard to find on the optical probe and wafer level test side (*FormFactor’s July 29th*). That said, the share in this layer is not something confirmed by public figures. I note that it is my industry sense. The thesis running through this whole layer is one thing. When SiPh moves past the R&D stage into volume production, test demand steps up at the production stage. The piece that split this layer into four stages is *The 100-Second Bottleneck Behind NVIDIA CPO: 7 Companies That Own the 4-Stage Test Stack*. That said, as written earlier, whether all five are necessary is still unsolved homework.

**Chips and networking.** MRVL is 5.3%. It is a company I have watched since the Inphi acquisition, and the reason is that it has a seat in both DSP and interconnect. The argument that the scale across segment is coherent rather than CPO was covered in *One Layer Below SemiAnalysis’s Meta Map: Scale-Across Is Coherent, Not CPO*, and Marvell’s seat was looked at there too. CIEN, which was there in April, has been cleared.

## 11. The Cost of Concentration

The April piece had only returns, and not what they cost. This time I write both.

Measured on a monthly index with deposits and withdrawals stripped out (Modified Dietz approximation), the maximum drawdown from the high is −30.52%. It all opened up in the single month of July so it ended up being the same number as the worst monthly return. The actual balance curve looks like it fell less than this because money came in during July, and measured intraday it is conversely deeper. What is written here is the number after erasing the cash flows. The sample standard deviation of monthly returns is around 20%, and annualized it exceeds 70%. You should take into account that the last August is 17 days treated as a full month, so it is a rough calculation. And eleven of thirteen months ended positive.

The number 70% may not land intuitively, but SMH’s annualized volatility over the same period is around 42%, so it means it swung about 1.7 times more than that. With a sample of only thirteen months this is not enough to discuss a distribution, but even within that short sample a −30.52% month actually happened.
