# Optical Investing, Round Two: Which Names to Watch Now

TL;DR

The fundamentals are unchanged and guidance has in fact moved higher. What fell in June and July was not earnings but the valuation multiple. Supply is still a bottleneck, and with the FCC draft rule and the listing of a dedicated optical ETF, investor sentiment is coming back as well.

The criteria for picking names are two. How much exposure a company has to which content among pluggable, OCS, NPO, CPO and scale across, and whether it holds the bottleneck needed to produce that content.

The layer that fits these criteria best is L2 lasers, where I look at Lumentum, Coherent, AAOI and Sivers. Then come Corning and Amphenol in L6B, where volume grows regardless of form factor, AAOI and Fabrinet in L6A, which is generating profit right now, L6C on a long horizon, Marvell, Credo and Semtech in L3 and L4, and then AXT in L1, Tower Semiconductor in L5 and AEHR in L7, which hold the bottlenecks themselves.

Earlier this year, the theme that led the market’s big rally was without question the optical sector. Almost every optical stock set new all time highs, from at least 100% to several hundred percent, and every investor was looking at optics.

But was it that they had run up too far?

From June on, the entire market including semiconductors and memory began to correct, AI skepticism started to surface, and the optical sector too had to watch its share prices fall by more than half from the highs.

Some investors caught in the drawdown left the game altogether, saying the optical sector was finished.

And now in August, share prices look to be riding an upward trend again on the back of earnings from several optical companies.

Is this really the time to come back to optical investing?

Or is it only a temporary bounce, with a sharp rise like the one earlier this year unlikely for a while?

Those who have followed me on X and Substack will know that I have been arguing, ever since AEHR’s earnings came out, that round two of optical investing had started.

In fact, there was a slight correction after AEHR’s earnings release, but up to now optical stocks have been in an uptrend.

In this article, I want to lay out, in detail, the round two optical investing strategy that follows my earlier optical investment map.

Why this correction happened, where optics stands now and what points to look at closely, and if you were to start investing in the optical sector fresh at this point, which layer and which names in this complex optical ecosystem you should look at first.

If you are an investor who is going to take another serious look at the optical sector, I hope you read this article through.

## Why the June and July Correction Happened

The starting point of the optical correction was SemiAnalysis’s CPO analysis. Along with the provocative phrase Massive Delay, it argued on the basis of low yields and cost burden that CPO mass production in the scale up segment would be pushed out from late 2028 to beyond 2029, and that until then copper and pluggable modules would keep the upper hand. After that publication, valuation adjustment appeared across the optical communications value chain.

On top of this, as AEC (Active Electrical Cable, a copper cable with a signal restoration chip built in) shipments grew quickly, a market consensus formed that in rack connectivity would be handled by copper for the time being, and the Optical I/O argument, that the transceiver module market shrinks once the electrical to optical conversion moves inside the GPU package, came back into focus as well.

Beyond that, optical stocks were already at three digit price to earnings ratios, so profit taking and index rebalancing compounded and the drawdown widened.

## August, What Changed

On August 11, Lumentum said demand for ultra high power CPO lasers is increasing, that it had received an initial order for ELS modules, and that several NPO engagements are underway.

For its lead CPO customer, it said high volume shipments for scale up begin in the second half of 2027 with customer deployment in 2028.

The next day Coherent said there had been no case at all of CPO demand being pushed out and that customer interest had in fact increased.

It added that scale out CPO revenue, connecting rack to rack, begins in the second half of 2026, that scale up, which ties multiple compute racks together within a cluster, follows in 2027, and that it is engaged with multiple customers on both CPO and NPO.

SemiAnalysis’s claim that NVIDIA’s scale up CPO mass production could be delayed beyond 2029 was confirmed to be untrue through the earnings calls of these two companies.

On top of this came OCS (Optical Circuit Switch) results.

OCS is equipment that switches the optical path directly instead of using an electrical switch, reducing the number of electrical switching hops. As Google shifted from what it had developed in house to external sourcing, Lumentum announced it had secured a backlog of more than $400 million from three new customers and will ship roughly $400 million in the second half of 2026. Coherent has already announced initial revenue, and on August 12 raised the size of this market to more than $4 billion.

Another of the most important items was Aehr’s earnings.

Test equipment orders are normally a leading indicator that runs several quarters to more than a year ahead of a customer’s production ramp, and Aehr guided FY2027 revenue to $130 million to $150 million, 2.6x to 3x the prior year, of which $100.6 million was announced as effective backlog already received. Its lead silicon photonics customer is already running an unmanned automated line, a follow on production order came in early August, and a new customer has already placed an order for four systems within a few months of first contact and taken delivery, so the order data showed that optical production ahead is going to grow much larger.

## Why Optics, Why Now, Again

As we saw above, the fundamentals of optics have not changed. If anything, guidance has moved higher through this August earnings round. Coherent’s FY2027 first quarter guidance has a low end above the quarter just reported and gross margin is heading up as well. What fell in June and July was not earnings but the valuation multiple.

That optical connectivity grows as AI data centers get bigger is something no investor with conviction in this sector disagrees with. The problem was the market’s misunderstanding that this optical demand was tied to a single CPO timeline. Exactly as I emphasized in my earlier scale up CPO article, Coherent in this earnings round also reconfirmed that the content opportunity it captures is similar whether things go the CPO way or the NPO way.

Supply is still a bottleneck as well.

InP (Indium Phosphide, the compound substrate that laser chips are made on) wafer capacity is expected to grow by 20 to 30 times through 2029, and until then it looks unlikely to keep up with demand. Lumentum’s revenue and operating margin rising together in the quarter just reported is read as a positive signal that this supply bottleneck is currently converting into profitability.

More important is that optical investors’ sentiment is coming back. On August 4 there was a report that the FCC is preparing a draft rule restricting imports of new Chinese optical transceivers, and after that report the share prices of several optical stocks rose sharply.

Whether this regulation actually gets finalized remains to be seen, but it was good signal news in the sense that investors were reminded once again of how geopolitically important the optical theme is in its connection to AI. In addition, LYTE, the ETF that holds only the optical value chain, traded $72 million on its first day of listing on August 6. That is a bullish factor for optical investing and at the same time a crowding signal.

Still, what makes optical investing hard is that the optical ecosystem itself is so complex, and that each of these factors does not apply to every layer at the same time. To resolve this complexity, back in April I wrote an optical investment map. In this article I intend to use the layer map from that map again for analysis.

Unlike last time’s broad overview, in this article I plan to approach it from a perspective that is more useful to investors. In the end what investors are most curious about is, if I had to start optical investing now, again, which layer and which names among this many layers and names I should look at first, and how to think about investment strategy.

In the rest of this article I want to analyze this investment perspective in detail, together with the current state of optical technology development analyzed above.

## If You Were Starting Optical Investing Now

A note on reading the table

The names highlighted in yellow are ones the author has classified as priority watch candidates according to his own criteria, based on results disclosed to date and the outlook ahead. Whether a name is highlighted is not a recommendation to buy or sell, and a name that is not highlighted does not mean it is unsuitable for investment. The selection criteria and judgments are entirely the author’s own, and investment decisions should be made through your own research.

Among these many layers, the thesis I consider most important right now has two parts.

One is how much exposure a company has to which form of content among pluggable, OCS, NPO, CPO and scale across, and whether that exposure grows from here.

The other is whether it holds the bottleneck needed to produce that content. The first axis points to positions that do not lose revenue whichever form factor wins, and the second points to positions that can set price when demand rises.

## Layer 2 - Lasers

As I have said in several earlier articles, the layer that fits these criteria best is L2, lasers. Lasers are needed even if pluggable lasts a long time, they are needed if things go to NPO, and once things move to CPO the importance of high power external lasers actually goes up. On top of that, OCS has been added as separate demand.

There will be differences between individual laser companies, but personally, in round two of optics, I think there is a chance that every name in this layer receives demand off the single theme of lasers, so I highlighted all four names.

If asked which of these to pick, I would pick Lumentum and Coherent, as I have also said on X.

Volatility has been severe given the nature of the optical theme, but these two companies are the core names in optical theme investing as I see it. My personal approach has been to accumulate whenever the price gets cheaper and to stand aside when it spikes, watching them over a long period.

Personally I am watching COHR a bit more closely from the perspective of optical vertical integration. The differences between the two companies were already covered in the deep dive articles, so please refer back to those two articles for the details.

AAOI and Sivers as well, as said above, I expect to be re rated over a longer horizon and to benefit from optical laser demand.

AAOI makes InP laser chips itself at its own epi fab in Sugar Land. The point to look at from a CPO perspective is the 400mW class high power laser. CPO and NPO place the light source outside the engine and share one light source across multiple channels, so output is needed to compensate for splitting loss, and whether this product moves from samples into volume production is what separates AAOI entering the CPO supply chain from remaining a transceiver assembler. Alongside this, the fact that it has its production base in the United States and would be in a direct position if the regulation is finalized is also worth looking at.

Sivers is the small cap most directly tied to the spread of CPO and NPO. It makes DFB laser arrays at its Glasgow InP fab, and that array is exactly the core component of an ELS module. Because the structure pulls multiple wavelengths from a single device and supplies them to the optical engine, demand rises directly as CPO and NPO adoption increases. It is developing ELS modules together with O-Net, Enablence and POET, and its history of supplying light sources to Ayar Labs sits in this same position.

That said, given the nature of these two names, volatility is severe and there is much more that needs to be proven compared with Lumentum and Coherent, so I recommend taking that into account when considering an investment. AAOI’s data center revenue more than doubled in a year but gross margin is below 30% and on a GAAP basis it is still posting a quarterly net loss, and Sivers still has a small revenue base relative to its ELS assets with volume production conversion not yet confirmed. Personally AAOI is one of the names I keep accumulating whenever the price falls, and I approach Sivers with short term trading in mind. Both names also have deep dive articles, so please read them before investing.

## Layer 6 - Modules, Infra, and Transport

### L6B: Infra

The next layer that fits my optical investing thesis is L6B, fiber and connectivity. It is the layer that most precisely meets the criterion stated above, that content is not lost whichever form factor prevails. Fiber is needed even if pluggable lasts a long time, and once things move to CPO the fiber comes inside the switch box and both the count and the unit price actually go up. On top of that, as scale up spans multiple racks, the rack to rack links themselves increase. It is a position where volume grows regardless of who wins the form factor debate.

The names I watch in this layer are Corning and Amphenol.

Corning makes fiber and CPO connectors itself, and has locked in volume by contract through its partnership with NVIDIA to expand US production capacity tenfold and a multi year supply agreement with Amazon. Optical communications is 44% of core revenue, so purity is high, but you have to account for the fact that it also spans display glass, mobile cover glass, automotive environmental products and solar polysilicon.

Amphenol spans both copper and optics to the extent that the company itself uses the phrase interconnect neutrality. In the second quarter IT datacom was 43% of total revenue and grew 89% year over year, with AI related revenue alone exceeding a $10.5 billion annualized run rate. Through the CommScope CCS acquisition it brought in optical interconnect wholesale, and that business’s IT datacom revenue doubled in a year. Because it is a structure that receives revenue whether copper stays in the rack for a long time or optics accelerates, by the criteria stated above its breadth is in fact wider than Corning’s. The risk is that how much of IT datacom is optical is not disclosed separately. The copper and power share is large so it is hard to judge on the optical argument alone, and whether the CommScope integration proceeds as planned has to be watched as well.

Fujikura in the same layer is a good company, but I could not separately confirm its data center optical exposure so I did not highlight it.

### L6A: Module

L6A is the segment where 800G and 1.6T pluggables are actually generating profit right now, and where, even as NPO and scale up optics come in behind them, there are areas pluggable does not get replaced, so it can carry strong results for a while.

I view AAOI favorably together with L2 lasers, and I think Fabrinet is a name worth watching in that it can capture the spillover benefit from the FCC. For someone with firm conviction on this layer, I think it is an attractive name where you can position your investment judgment around the flow ahead of the August 17 earnings release.

### L6C: Transport (Scale across)

L6C is the scale across area, a field that has drawn relatively little attention in optical investing so far. But looked at over a long horizon, it is also the layer where the pie can grow the most. As AI clusters grow from a rack to a pod and then between campuses and data centers, DCI and coherent transport demand grows with it, and this segment has little to do with whether the optical engine goes inside the package or outside it. In this Coherent earnings release as well, DCI and scale across were named as growth drivers for the communications segment.

Ciena and Nokia are therefore names I consider good from the perspective of holding over a long horizon rather than short term momentum. The risk is that both companies have low optical purity so the conditions of the telecom business are reflected in results as well. I plan to cover this layer separately in a deep dive article before long.

## Layer 3 and 4 - Connectivity IC and PIC platform

In the L3 and L4 layers the company I pay the most attention to is, again, Marvell. If I were to sum up in one sentence why I view this name favorably, I would say it is the company that has internalized every ingredient needed for optical investing, the company NVIDIA needs most. I have shared my thinking on this name several times, so please refer to those articles for the details.

Limiting it to L3, the name I view favorably is Credo. If copper lasts, it captures it through AEC, and if optics accelerates, it can expand into optical DSPs and SiPh PICs. It internalized silicon photonics through the DustPhotonics acquisition and guided FY2027 optical revenue to more than $600 million.

Astera Labs is a company I view favorably but did not highlight this time. PCIe retimers and scale up fabric are its main business so it moves independently of the optical argument, and its optics is limited to the fiber to chip coupling secured through aiXscale, with no revenue yet. That is the point of difference from Credo in the same layer, which is already generating optical revenue. If you want to understand Credo and Astera Labs, please refer to the article that compares the two in depth.

Another company I pay attention to is Semtech. I was not able to cover it in depth in earlier articles, but with the HieFo acquisition in March it internalized InP gain chips and DFB lasers, putting it in the same direction as L2.

What I look at with Semtech is the revenue from its own chips that goes into a single module. In an 800G module it earned high single digit dollars from TIAs and drivers, but in a 3.2T module InP lasers and gain chips are added on top of that and it becomes about $80. The amount earned per module becomes ten times larger, and this amount goes up automatically with each generation transition. Data center is still in the low 20% range of total company revenue, so I see this mix shift itself as remaining a re rating driver, which is why I consider it one of the names to watch with interest.

## Layer 1, 5, and 7 - Materials, Foundry, and Test

L5 and L7 do not make the optical devices themselves but handle manufacturing and verification, so they receive optical demand derivatively. That said, as things evolve toward CPO the requirements for advanced packaging and reliability verification are getting more complex, so re rating of the demand and benefit for these two layers is still ongoing.

L1 is different in character from these. Without InP substrates you cannot make lasers, and this stage is the starting point of the light source supply bottleneck we saw earlier. It is not an indirect beneficiary but the bottleneck itself.

There are many companies, but if I had to pick just one from each layer, it would be AXT in L1, Tower Semiconductor in L5 and AEHR in L7. What these companies have in common is that they sit in a position to benefit directly as each bottleneck intensifies.

For AXT, the InP bottleneck showed up in actual numbers in the July 30 earnings. Second quarter revenue was $47.59 million, up 164% year over year and 77% quarter over quarter, and InP revenue alone was $30.7 million, a record and about 65% of the total. Gross margin rose from 8.0% a year earlier to 44.9%, and it swung from a loss to net income of $11.13 million. The InP backlog passed $100 million and third quarter revenue guidance is about $66 million, up 39% quarter over quarter.

The demand side pie keeps growing. The company said it will raise quarterly InP capacity to $60 million by the end of 2026 and about $130 million by the end of 2027, and Coherent committed a $22.3 million advance payment on a three year six inch InP supply agreement while Lumentum committed up to $87.5 million on a six year capacity reservation. It is the form in which the capacity expansion at the light source companies we saw earlier came straight down into contracts at the substrate stage.

The risk comes from the structure of having the production entity in China. The pace at which export permits are issued directly determines quarterly revenue, and the third quarter guidance is a figure that reflects only volumes for which permits have already been received or are not required. This variable does not go away, so I think the right approach is to check each quarter whether permit issuance continues normally.

Tower Semiconductor is directly affected by the silicon photonics wafer volume bottleneck regardless of which type of transceiver wins. In the August 11 second quarter results, silicon photonics revenue grew 270% year over year to pass a $680 million annualized run rate, and the company set a target of a $1 billion run rate in the fourth quarter. The $1.3 billion of 2027 revenue is already locked in by contracts accompanied by $290 million in prepayments.

It has more than 50 silicon photonics customers so dependence on any single customer is low as well. However, the risks are that margin structure is low given the nature of the foundry business, and the pace at which contracts convert into actual shipments. In my own view, at this point the optical related catalysts are already substantially reflected in the share price, and from here I think the right approach is to check the pace at which the quarterly run rate reaches the contracted volume.

AEHR is not so much a company directly tied to optics as one that benefits directly from the KGD bottleneck across every kind of advanced packaging including CPO and HBM. From an optical perspective, as CPO and NPO increase, the requirement to sort known good die before packaging grows, and those equipment orders come in ahead of the customer’s volume production. However, this name too has already risen more than four times from when I covered it in March, so at this point it is holders’ territory, and rather than chasing the spike right now I think the right approach is to look for opportunities the market offers through volatility.
