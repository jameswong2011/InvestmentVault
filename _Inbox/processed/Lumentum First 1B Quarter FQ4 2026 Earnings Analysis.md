# Lumentum First 1B Quarter FQ4 2026 Earnings Analysis

### TL;DR

- Lumentum’s FQ4 2026 revenue, announced after the market close on August 11, came in at $1,006.3M. Quarterly revenue crossed $1B for the first time, up 24.5% from the prior quarter and a striking 109.3% from a year ago [1].

- The same quarter shows a GAAP net loss of $7.2B, but the source of this loss sits outside operations. The cause is a one-time, non-cash accounting loss of $7.8B from converting convertible notes into stock, and I walk through it in the body [1][2].

- Non-GAAP operating margin of 36.6%. While revenue doubled, operating margin rose 2,160bps year over year. Next quarter’s guidance calls for $1.25B revenue at the midpoint and a 39.5-40.5% operating margin, arriving at the target model the company laid out at OFC more than a quarter early [1][2].

- Here is my lead. I think the real news this quarter is less the revenue figure and more the five signals mentioned alongside transceivers. What those signals are, and why they change the very size of the market Lumentum can address, is laid out in the body.

## 1. August 11, the First $1B Quarter

Lumentum reported FQ4 2026 results (quarter ended June 27) after the market close on August 11. Revenue was $1,006.3M, up 24.5% QoQ and up 109.3% YoY [1]. A year ago this company’s quarterly revenue was $480.7M, so revenue has more than doubled in four seasons.

You may wonder whether this growth is a one-off jump. Borrowing the language from the call, this is the eighth consecutive quarter of revenue growth, and counting only quarters with more than 20% sequential growth, it is the third in a row [2]. The comparison base keeps getting bigger, and the growth rate is holding anyway. Back in May I covered the $808M quarter in The 8 Companies Behind Lumentum’s $808M Quarter: Why Beta Splits by Orders of Magnitude Across the InP Cycle, and just one quarter later another $200M has been stacked on top. As a growth rate, that is an enormous number.

The profitability numbers actually catch my eye more. Non-GAAP gross margin came in at 50.4%, crossing 50% for the first time. The company reportedly planned to cross this threshold at around $2B in quarterly revenue, and it got there first at half that revenue [2]. Operating margin was 36.6%, an expansion of 440bps QoQ and 2,160bps YoY [1]. While revenue doubled, operating expenses stayed pinned at 13.7% of revenue [2], so this is a zone where added revenue drops almost straight through to profit. When operating leverage works the way the textbook says, this is the picture you get.

The stock reaction was, unexpectedly, on the calm side. The August 11 regular session closed at $820.59 (+0.87%), and after the release the stock was up around 2% after hours (as of 2026-08-11, including after hours) [3]. Market cap is $63.8B, and the 52-week range runs from $111.20 to $1,085.68, so even after printing these results the stock still sits more than 20% below its 52-week high [3]. As it happens, the optics-only ETF LYTE listed five days before these results [4], and LAZR had already arrived in late June [5]. I suppose that means the market’s eyes have reached this layer, enough for optics to become a standalone theme product.

But one of the headline numbers probably snagged your eye. A GAAP net loss of $7.2B. What does it mean that a $1B-revenue company lost more than $7B in a quarter?

## 2. What the $7.2B Loss Really Is

To put the conclusion first, this loss involved no cash leaving the company, and it does not mean the business rolled over. It is an accounting loss created by the stock rising sharply over the past year.

Here is what happened, in order. Lumentum had issued convertible notes some time ago. A convertible note is borrowed money, but in the form of a bond the holder can swap into stock at a preset price if they choose. Over the past year, with the stock up severalfold, the value of that conversion option grew. This quarter the company proactively converted a portion of these notes into stock (equitization), and as a result $1.1B of debt was erased from the books. That is about 35% of the convertible notes that were outstanding [2].

Here is why an accounting loss shows up.

On the books this debt is recorded at the amount borrowed, but the market value of the shares handed over in repayment is far larger, by as much as the stock has risen. Accounting books that difference as a loss on debt extinguishment. This quarter that amount was $7.8B, which is how the $7.2B GAAP net loss came about [1][2].

As a rough analogy, it is like borrowing the price of rice while promising “you can take our field as repayment,” and in the meantime the field’s value has gone up severalfold. The moment you repay with the field, a big loss prints on the books, but the cause of that loss is the field’s value, that is, the company’s value, having risen in the first place. So I read this $7.2B as closer to a footnote than a worry. Actual quarterly profitability is told by the non-GAAP side: net income of $326.3M, or $3.23 per share [1].

There is one thing to add. Since debt was swapped into stock, dilution for existing shareholders is real, and unlike the one-time accounting number, it stays. That said, interest-bearing debt did shrink, so I lean toward seeing this as a sensible trade: cleaning up debt while the stock is high.

## 3. Where These Orders Come From

So where does the demand that doubled revenue come from?

The starting point is hyperscaler spending. I covered the capex upgrade trend confirmed in the big four’s late-July results in The GPU Repricing Cycle and Q2 Hyperscaler Earnings: Which Hardware Layer the Capex Flows To, and when that money goes into a data center, a network linking the GPUs to each other always goes in right beside them, and the physical layer of that network is optics. The transceivers that turn the signals running between GPUs and switches into light and carry them, the InP laser chips inside them that make that light, and the pump lasers in the long-haul links connecting data centers to each other: as it happens, all of it is on this company’s product list.

One anecdote from this call captures the scale of this demand well.

For one major hyperscaler, the network capacity connecting two AI data center sites is reportedly double the entire global backbone that company laid over the past decade [2]. A line connecting ‘just two sites.’ The picture is training and inference splitting across multiple sites and inter-site traffic growing, and thanks to that, pump lasers for long-haul links are effectively sold out even after shipments grew more than 80% year over year, and the company plans to quadruple shipments over the next several quarters [2].

I actually found this part interesting and mentioned it in the subscriber-only chat as one of the seven things I was watching.

This flow itself is not a new story. In fact, together with Aurelion Research, I had already handed you a big hint. In July’s A Conversation with Lumentum, which recorded a conversation with Lumentum IR, we had already heard that EML capacity was up eightfold in two and a half years and still could not keep up with demand, and these results are close to the first quarterly closing where those words print as numbers.

Reading the CEO comment, though, I get the impression that what the company really wants to emphasize is not the products selling today. The CEO paragraph of the press release strings together words that are probably a bit unfamiliar: OCS, 1.6T, ultra-high-power CPO lasers, a first ELS module order, and NPO. Five of them packed into one paragraph, and the concluding sentence is that “optics are starting to penetrate in-rack connectivity” [1].

Why is a company that sells transceivers talking about the inside of the rack? And why is the simultaneous appearance of these five words the real news of the quarter?

## 4. Segment Breakdown

Lumentum’s revenue comes in two chunks. Components sells laser chips and parts, the innards that go into other people’s modules, and Systems sells finished products like Cloud Light transceivers and OCS.

This quarter the two ran together. Components $649.4M (+21.8% QoQ, +102.7% YoY), Systems $356.9M (+29.7% QoQ, +122.6% YoY) [1]. Both more than doubled year over year, so this was literally a quarter where the whole front line moved.

Revenue and non-GAAP operating margin, FQ4 2025 through FQ1 2027 guidance

Looking a bit deeper inside Components through the call, it goes like this [2].

- EML: Another record quarter. The center of volume is still 100G per lane, but 200G EMLs are climbing fast and now exceed 25% of EML revenue. The company expects 200G to pass half of EML volume by mid-2027. The comment that supply still trails demand was there again as well, and I cover that part separately in section 7.

- CW laser: This is the part of the call I stared at the longest. If an EML is a self-contained part that generates and modulates light on one chip, a CW laser is a light source that only keeps emitting light and leaves modulation to a silicon photonics chip. Output from the Japan fabs is better than expected, so surplus volume is being turned toward CW, and by shrinking the die size substantially the company has considerably narrowed the margin gap versus EMLs. There was also an explanation that the tight spec distribution produces good yields in customer transceiver manufacturing, which lets them sell at a premium to market price. On what this die shrink actually is, Irrational Analysis reads it as shortening the cavity again, that is, the stretch inside the laser where light travels back and forth and gets amplified, while holding the same linewidth (wavelength purity) requirement [6]. The shorter the cavity, the smaller the die, but the harder it becomes to hold wavelength purity, and if you pull that off, the number of chips per wafer rises accordingly. That is the engineering substance of the narrowing margin gap. With silicon photonics-based transceivers taking a larger share in the 1.6T generation, if the EML company holds CW in its other hand too, the light source is theirs whichever side wins.

For how EMLs and CW lasers differ in operation, a figure I published earlier should make it easy to picture.

- Pump laser: As written above, more than 80% growth while sold out, with a plan to quadruple output. What matters here is that this expansion is backed by three-year long-term agreements. Many of the contracts with network equipment makers are reportedly take-or-pay in nature (the customer owes payment even if they do not take the product), with levers built in that allow price increases when conditions are met [2]. It is a structure where customers share the expansion risk.

On the Systems side, while 800G transceivers hit record shipments, 1.6T production shipments started on plan, and OCS shipments doubled from the prior quarter [2]. Profitability followed. The comment was that transceiver line margins keep improving as higher-priced initial 1.6T volume lands on top of yield and utilization gains [2].

That ran long, so to compress this section into one line: today’s cash (EML, 800G) and tomorrow’s cash (CW, 1.6T, OCS) both grew in the same quarter, and that is the point of these segment numbers.

## 5. Five Signals: Optics Moving Into the Rack

Now let’s go back to the question I threw out earlier. Why is a transceiver company talking about the inside of the rack?

Until now, optics’ job was connecting rack to rack and data center to data center, and the short links inside the rack were copper’s territory. But as GPU generations advance, an era is coming where multiple racks have to be tied together like a single computer.

Copper is said to still be the mainstream here for now, but in the end this domain, called scale-up, will also cross over from copper to optics, and when it does, one more floor of addressable market opens for optics companies. This quarter, the sound of that door opening came from five places at once.

Signal 1, 1.6T production begins. Production shipments of the next-generation cloud transceiver have started, and the company expects 1.6T adoption to intensify from next quarter and continue through 2027 [2]. There was also a comment that in several cases they appear to have reached the market ahead of larger competitors, which should be taken as a vendor claim.

Signal 2, OCS’s first triple-digit quarter. An OCS is an optical circuit switch that redirects light with mirrors instead of converting it to electricity. Shipments doubled from the prior quarter, and next quarter’s guidance includes the first-ever $100M-plus OCS quarter. And “meaningfully above” that mark, at that [2]. On the existing guide of $400M for the second half of 2026, the answer was tracking rather than exceeding, and considering the volume lost to early-ramp supply chain problems, that sounds to me like an answer leaving room in the later quarter. The competitive remarks stood out too: even against the customer that builds its own OCS in-house (publicly, Google is the flagship operator using OCS at scale), they said that by early 2027 they would overtake that internal volume and become the number one supplier [2]. Along with the line that they are effectively the only merchant OCS supplier. On top of that, higher and lower port count variants and in-tray products aimed at 2028 are reportedly on the roadmap, so OCS is growing past the experimental line into a second pillar.

Signal 3, ultra-high-power CPO laser demand accelerating. The lead CPO customer’s production plans (on the public record, NVIDIA has named Lumentum as an ELS vendor) remain on track, and the demand signal has reportedly grown since the last update [2]. The timetable the company gave is high-volume shipments in the second half of 2027, with the customer’s scale-up deployments in 2028. The CFO saw high-power laser revenue passing the $50M level late this year and reaching its first $100M quarter in FQ3 2027 [2].

Signal 4, the first ELS module purchase order. ELS is an external light source module. A CPO switch takes the part that makes the light and moves it into a module outside the switch, and that module is the ELS; the first purchase order (PO) has now been received, with delivery due in the second half of 2027 [1][2]. The ASP is meaningfully higher than individual laser chips, and the margin is above the company average but below lasers [2]. It is the first step for a chip seller moving up to the module level, and on the call they described it as “a product that opens up CPO and NPO for customers who do not know how to handle laser chips.”

Signal 5, NPO engagements broadening. NPO is an intermediate architecture that puts the optical engine not inside the chip package but on the board, right next to the XPU. It gives up power and cost versus CPO in exchange for being faster to build, and customers other than the lead customer are reportedly taking it as their stepping stone toward CPO [2]. Multiple engagements are in progress, and even the largest CPO customer is said to be looking at NPO for specific use cases. The light source approach takes the 400mW-class high-power design and brings it down to 120-150mW mid-power [2]. To label it precisely, though, this fifth signal is still at the engagement stage. That means design discussions before any purchase order, and the revenue timing is also late 2027 into 2028.

Let me add one viewing point here. As Irrational Analysis pointed out, integrating lasers into the optical engine makes the drive-current and thermal environment harsher, so preventing mode hops (the laser’s wavelength momentarily jumping to a neighboring mode and dropping the link) becomes far harder than in an external module, and if one laser dies, the whole engine has to be replaced [6]. With ELS you just swap the module. So with InP scarce right now, the force pushing toward area-efficient integration and the force pushing reliability toward external modules are in a tug of war, and whichever way it tips, the light source company that has proven a wide stable operating range in production statistics comes out ahead. It is also an extension of the thesis we wrote in the July IR conversation piece, that “the specs that do not show up on datasheets are the real moat.”

You will see what the five signals have in common. They are all products outside the existing pluggable transceiver market, and they all carry timetables that land in revenue between the second half of 2027 and 2028. If the EML and 800G that make today’s results are the first S-curve, these five are the reservation list for the second S-curve. The timetable lining up exactly with the answer from the July IR conversation, that “copper hits the wall in late 2027,” is also something to keep an eye on.

## 6. Guidance Check: What a 40% Operating Margin Implies

FQ1 2027 guidance is revenue of $1.225B to $1.275B, non-GAAP operating margin of 39.5-40.5%, and earnings per share of $4.05-4.35 [1]. The $1.25B midpoint is 130% growth year over year, and another all-time record quarter.

Reading the message underneath rather than the numbers themselves, it goes like this.

First, early arrival at the target model. The target the company presented at OFC was $1.25B in revenue, and it arrives there more than a quarter early [2]. Second, the company’s confidence that the margin upgrade is structural. The original target model was a 38-42% operating margin at $2B in quarterly revenue, and on this call the CFO said to think of 42% as more like the midpoint, lifting the range itself by 100-200bps [2]. Third, the composition: half the growth comes from Components and half from Systems [2]. That means the 1.6T and OCS ramps are already in next quarter’s numbers, so whether the signals in section 5 are just talk can be checked directly against the numbers three months from now. Personally, I am looking forward to the OCS ramp.

Separately from Lumentum, I intend to cover OCS as a whole when the occasion comes.

Then why did the market reaction stop at around 2% after hours on results like these? Yes, as you have probably guessed, it is likely because numbers of this level were already largely in the stock price [3]. Considering the bar set by June’s CPO timing debate (the SemiAnalysis-driven correction), this call reads less as fuel for a spike and more as the company’s rebuttal evidence in that debate. At the very least, “the lead customer’s demand signal has grown” and “the first ELS PO” are facts that did not exist three months ago.

## 7. Prior-Call Review: The AXT LTA and the Supply Gap

In AXT and Lumentum’s Prepayment Deal: The InP Substrate LTA Chain, written on August 2, I pinned this earnings release as a checkpoint date. The things to check were whether the supply gap said to be in the 30-percent range was holding, and whether additional substrate LTAs were being signed. It was the evening of the release day, before the official transcript was cleaned up, so I obtained a full transcript of the call and checked these two items against it.

Starting with the first item. A Citi analyst asked exactly this question, and the CEO’s answer was that for EML, no change. The gap has neither widened nor narrowed, and they are still shipping behind customer demand. But the weight of the answer sat somewhere else. On high-power lasers, demand has accelerated and supply has fallen even further behind [2]. The added note was that the ramp execution is on plan, but the demand signal grew faster than that. The persistent-shortage reading of the August 2 piece stays, and the update this quarter is that the center of gravity of the shortage is shifting from EML toward high-power CW.

The second item came from a Wolfe analyst. Why did you do a deal with AXT when just a quarter ago you said substrate supply was fine? Carrying the CEO’s answer over directly: a surge in high-power laser demand arrived over the past three months, and the AXT deal was the result of moving to secure substrates in response. Adding AXT to the existing relationship with the Japanese supplier covers them for now, but he went as far as saying that if this demand vector continues, that judgment could change a quarter or two from now [2]. No additional LTAs have been signed yet, but there was also a preview that they are discussing new arrangements with multiple customers around the Greensboro plant’s capacity, with something to announce within a few quarters [2]. In the August 2 piece I read this contract as “evidence of the InP shortage and the start button on the cycle clock,” and this call comes closer to reconfirming that reading in the company’s own words. The next square in the substrate LTA chain remains checking, at AXT’s earnings release, the receipt of the prepayment and progress on the expansion.

One confession: the answer I was most curious about on this call was the China InP competition question. The answer was that “no impact is visible in the numbers, and the customer yield advantage created by the tightness of our spec distribution will keep protecting the premium” [2]. There was also a pointed comment that some Chinese suppliers throwing out big numbers are not actually delivering into the market yet. This advantage claim comes from the company’s internal comparison, so it is territory that cannot be externally verified yet, but I take the answer as saying that, at least on the measured indicators of price and yield, there are no cracks so far. And I have commented on this point before, in A Conversation with Lumentum.

## 8. Conclusion: These Dates Will Decide

If I leave this quarter in one sentence, it is this. Under the headline of the first $1B quarter, this company’s reservation list for the next cycle (1.6T, OCS, CPO, ELS, NPO) got real, physical evidence across the board for the first time.

The $7.2B loss was an accounting footnote created by the stock’s rise, and the real profit and loss is told by the staircase of operating margin climbing from 36.6% toward 40%. And the next steps of that staircase all have dates stamped on them. These dates will do the judging.

- Mid-August 2026, AXT FQ2 results: confirmation of the $87M prepayment receipt and progress on the expansion. The item carried over from the August 2 piece.

- Early November 2026, LITE FQ1 2027 release: whether the first $100M-plus OCS quarter materializes, whether 1.6T adoption takes off in earnest, whether the 39.5-40.5% operating margin is delivered. The first check date for sections 5 and 6.

- The December 2026 quarter: the gate of EML unit shipments up 50% year over year, and whether high-power laser revenue reaches the $50M level [2].

- Early 2027: the point to confirm the statement about overtaking the customer’s internal volume to become the number one OCS supplier.

- Mid-2027: the crossover where 200G EMLs pass half of EML volume.

- Second half of 2027: high-volume shipments of high-power lasers and the first ELS module deliveries. The first $100M high-power quarter in FQ3 2027.

- Early 2028: first revenue out of Greensboro, and the start of customer scale-up deployments.

The conditions under which I am wrong are on the same list. If the OCS or 1.6T ramp slips in the November guidance, the second S-curve reading has to be pushed back at least two quarters, and if the lead CPO customer’s 2028 deployment schedule wobbles again, the skepticism that drove June’s correction has to be marked as having been right. The moment Chinese InP is confirmed in actual shipments, beyond announcements, is also a checkpoint for the premium logic. Conversely, if these dates hold in order, then while the market hesitates more than 20% below the high, the second S-curve will have gone from reservation to boarding.

The next piece will likely be a check on the substrate chain, once AXT’s results are out.
