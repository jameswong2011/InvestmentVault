Abstract
On August 20 SK hynix used its own newsroom to announce that a co-packaged optics roadmap paper had been published in Nature Electronics. CPO means putting the optical transceiver inside the same package as the processor. The company’s post leans on two things: Seunghoon Hong, its AI Infra team lead, served as a corresponding author, and the work goes beyond HBM innovation to a system-level blueprint. I read the post, opened the 15-page paper next to it, and then walked through the public record of all twelve authors one by one. That is when I found out this track did not start with the paper. The company post has not one line about the step before it. I treat this announcement as a positioning signal rather than a CPO product schedule. What holds up is that the technology choice was already marked in money, with all three memory makers leaving that mark side by side.

1. The company published this itself
Here is the headline that went up on the SK hynix newsroom dated August 20.
“SK hynix’s technology roadmap for co-packaged optics features in ‘Nature Electronics,’ as AI competition shifts from chips to systems” [1]
SK hynix newsroom post
The paper is “Co-packaged optics for high-performance computing and artificial intelligence,” the journal is Nature Electronics, and the format is a Review article. The front page is stamped submitted November 18, 2025 and accepted July 8, 2026 [2].
The company names two corresponding authors. Seunghoon Hong, team lead at AI Infra, SK hynix, and Professor Kyusang Lee of the Department of Electrical and Computer Engineering at the University of Virginia [1]. The collaborating institutions are listed as UIUC, NTU, MIT and Yonsei.
Look at the paper itself and there are twelve authors. Nature titles attach a contact e-mail only to corresponding authors, and the bottom of the first page carries six e-mail lines, one of them on the sk.com domain [2]. So the company took those six and introduced two of them as the lead corresponding authors.
AI Infra is an organization SK hynix set up in January 2024. By the company’s own newsroom description it groups global sales and marketing, the HBM business, and Memory System Research, which does pathfinding beyond HBM [3].
The newsroom even added a footnote defining what a corresponding author is. Principal authors who oversee and take responsibility for the whole process, from planning through submission, peer review response, and post-publication questions [1]. The fact that the company bothered to footnote that phrase tells you where the weight of this announcement sits.
The company did not leave this in an internal research news column either. The official X account pushed the same content on the afternoon of August 19 local time, with the hashtags #SKhynix #CPO #AIInfrastructure [4]. The Korean newsroom date reads August 20 because that is Korea time, so the global channel started the day before.
The X wording carries one more edit, though.
“Published in Nature Electronics, a new paper from @SKhynix and University of Virginia researchers explores how CPO can overcome the ‘bandwidth wall’ through optical connectivity.” [4]
SK hynix and University of Virginia researchers is all that survives in that sentence. The Korean newsroom lists UIUC, NTU, MIT and Yonsei too [1], and they are gone from the global channel. By author count the MIT and UIUC share is not small.
A memory company taking part in an optical interconnect review is not new by itself. A company turning that into an external message is a different thing. And the one partner name left standing in that message is the University of Virginia, which comes back later.
2. The wall the paper draws
The term the paper’s own text uses for this bottleneck is bandwidth wall. Memory wall shows up once, in a reference title. Here are the scaling numbers behind it. Every two years compute throughput went up 3x, DRAM bandwidth 1.6x, and interconnect bandwidth 1.4x, while on the same axis AI model training compute jumped 8.8x [2].
Newsroom scaling graphic, 3x compute against 1.4x interconnect
The company post quotes only two of those, the 3x and the 1.4x [1]. The paper has DRAM bandwidth at 1.6x sitting in the same figure. A memory company issuing a release with the DRAM curve dropped out is a curious edit.
So compute moves 3x while the pipe that carries the data moves 1.4x. The gap bites hardest at the boundary between memory and logic. Here is the paper’s own sentence.
“the gains are confined to intra-package communication; once data goes beyond the memory-logic boundary, electrical interconnects ... incur resistive and capacitive losses that greatly increase with frequency and distance” [2]
What HBM gained holds only inside the package, and the moment data crosses the memory-logic boundary the losses in copper take over again. You can stack DRAM dies with TSVs, which are vias drilled vertically through the chip to make the connections, and pull multiple terabits per second out of them, and that gain still will not follow the data out of the package.
This series has been over that diagnosis several times. Once in Three Routes Around the Memory Wall: Qualcomm’s Stack, Nvidia’s 3 Bits, and What Remains, which laid out three ways of raising memory bandwidth, and once on the optical memory fabric side in The More Anthropic Buys Micron HBM, the Faster Optical Memory Pooling Arrives. The diagnosis has been around for a while. A memory company putting its name on that chart and then issuing a release about it is the part that changed.
3. The table the post left out
The company post summarizes the paper’s technical targets in three lines. More than 100 Tb/s of bandwidth per node, energy below 1 pJ per bit, chip-to-chip latency under 10 ns [1]. The same numbers are in the paper [2].
There is one more table in the paper that the post did not carry over. Table 2, titled representative industrial CPO and optical I/O platforms in the original. It mixes shipped products, demonstrated prototypes and some projected figures, so not everything in it is in production. Broadcom Bailly and Davisson, NVIDIA Quantum-X Photonics, Intel OCI, Ayar Labs TeraPHY, Lightmatter Passage M1000, Celestial AI Photonic Fabric, Ranovus, Avicena, Microsoft MOSAIC. Ten rows, nine companies [2].
paper Table 2, industrial CPO and optical I/O platform list
Switch companies, accelerator companies and optical I/O startups are all in there, and the memory makers are the ones missing from the list.
This gap is something I already mapped once in July. In TSMC Is Ahead in CPO. Samsung Is Putting a Third Chip Next to HBM I mapped who holds each of the three chips that go on the interposer, meaning HBM, logic and the PIC. Samsung was the only one holding all three, and SK hynix held memory while sending the logic base die outside. That piece sorted SK hynix’s routes into CPO into three. Packaging verticalization in Indiana and Cheongju, the memory-system CPO review at the 2025 Future Forum, and the CPO ecosystem on the public-project side.
I went back to the original on that Future Forum item. It was held September 11, 2025 at the Icheon campus, and the third agenda was a joint presentation by KAIST Professor Kyoungsik Yu and SK hynix Vice President Hoyoung Son [5]. The title reads like this.
“A future connected by light: CPO (Co-Packaged Optics) technology evolution and its ripple effects on the memory industry” (author translation from the Korean original) [5]
SK hynix Future Forum 2025 newsroom
Son’s title is worth a second look. He is head of Advanced PKG Development [5], which is a package development organization rather than strategy or planning. And the content, per the newsroom, was sharing a review of silicon photonics technology types, working through how to apply it inside and around the memory system, and setting out what preparation is needed across Architecture, Design, Device and Package [5].
That July piece also left a monitoring item at the end. Watch whether the memory-system CPO reviewed at the Future Forum turns into an actual R&D program or a partner disclosure. This announcement is the first answer to that item. An academic partner is disclosed, and a company employee put his name on it as a corresponding author. It is still not confirmation that an R&D program is running, so I am leaving that item half filled rather than closing it.
A later figure in the same paper puts a memory pool and an XPU pool facing each other across a single photonic interposer [2]. The company post ran that same concept diagram [1]. The side with no name in the table takes up half of the future architecture.
The word CXL never appears in this paper. I checked by searching the full text. UCIe shows up five times in the same document, NVLink and PCIe twice each. The die-to-die standards and the network standards are covered, and the current standard for memory pooling is the one that is missing.
The paper talks about pooling memory without touching the electrical pooling standard the industry actually uses today. The right reading is that the review’s scope sits on the optical architecture side. A missing word and a deliberate exclusion are different claims, and I will not push it that far. Readers should account for the gap either way.
That is as far as you get from putting the paper and the release side by side. Checking the public record of all twelve authors one at a time makes it fairly clear where this collaboration came from. And there was a step before it that appears nowhere in the company post. A trace of money that moved before the paper did.

4. Twelve authors and where they come from
Going through the author list one name at a time shows the character of this collaboration again.
Start with MIT’s Jeehwan Kim. He pioneered remote epitaxy, the technique of slipping a two-dimensional material underneath so a single-crystal thin film can be peeled off [6]. Open that lab’s member list and more of this paper’s authors turn up.
Corresponding author Kyusang Lee is an alumnus who was in that lab from around 2016 to 2017 before going to the University of Virginia [6]. He took his PhD at Michigan, did his postdoc in that lab, and moved to a faculty position at Virginia in 2017 [7]. UIUC’s Hyunseok Kim, also a corresponding author, was in the same lab from 2019 to 2023 [6]. He joined UIUC in spring 2024, and the grant he picked up on arrival was a Samsung GRO project on micro-LED epitaxy and integration [8]. I come back to that grant in the next section. And Young Jin Yoo, one of the six co-first authors, is still listed as a postdoc in that lab, working on integrated photonics and active photonic devices [6].
Three of the six corresponding authors are Jeehwan Kim himself and two people out of his lab. On top of that a current member of the lab is a co-first author.
The rest do split off, of course. NTU’s Sanghoon Chae did pass through MIT, but in Jing Kong’s group, and his postdoc was in James Hone’s group at Columbia [9]. Another co-author, Georgii Zograf, is a nonlinear nanophotonics researcher out of the Chalmers line [10].
Put together, the academic list sits much closer to heterogeneous integration than to silicon photonics foundries. It is the school of peeling films off and moving them onto another substrate, and one MIT lab is at the center of it. This looks less like an industry-academia collaboration and more like SK hynix attaching itself to one lab’s network.
Where the paper’s Outlook puts that technology matters here. Transferable membrane photonic components, remote epitaxy and layer transfer sit in the paragraph describing the near-term path, not the long-term outlook [2]. What is placed on the long-term side is the optics-centric structure, the photonic interposer and the optical TSV [2]. The authors’ own line of work lands in the near-term box.
And among the twelve there is no academic author whose day job is DRAM devices or memory circuits, as far as I could find. The memory share is one seat, SK hynix. On public record alone the weight of this collaboration sits on the optical side. Career composition by itself cannot settle which direction the knowledge actually traveled.

5. Where the track actually starts
On May 14, 2025, Avicena, which builds micro-LED based chip-to-chip optical interconnect, announced a USD 65M Series B. Tiger Global led, and SK hynix is on the investor list [11]. Lam Research and Hitachi Ventures are said to be in the same round.
That release carries a comment from Rene Do, Senior Director of Venture Investment at SK hynix, and it is the most direct thing I found in this whole search.
“Energy-efficient optical interconnects are becoming a major topic in the architecture of next generation disaggregated memory fabrics. Since high speed electrical interconnects have fundamental reach limitations, and laser-based optical links are too power hungry, microLED-based interconnects like Avicena’s LightBundle represent a very promising solution for low power links in future memory fabrics. We are excited to join this round and potentially collaborate with the Avicena team on the development of next generation memory interconnects.” (Rene Do, Senior Director, Venture Investment, SK hynix) [11]
The first sentence has disaggregated memory fabric in it. That is exactly the name the company can put on the memory pool and XPU pool drawing in Fig. 4b of the paper. Fifteen months before the paper came out, someone at the company had already written that phrase into a public document.
The second sentence is a verdict on light sources. High speed electrical wiring has a fundamental reach limit, and laser-based optical links eat too much power. Therefore micro-LED looks promising. For a courtesy line in a venture funding release, that is a fairly specific technical judgment.
The third sentence carries the most weight. They are glad to join the round and see potential collaboration with the Avicena team on developing next generation memory interconnects. That goes past writing a cheque and watching. Development collaboration is said out loud. The word potentially is attached, which keeps it short of a confirmed partnership. In its own words the company is marking a spot somewhere between a pure financial position and a development partnership.
Laying the dates out in order.
SK hynix optical interconnect track timeline, May 2025 to August 2026
May 14, 2025, the Avicena Series B participation [11]. September 11, 2025, the Future Forum CPO agenda [5]. At SK AI Summit 2025, held in Seoul November 3 to 4, Kyusang Lee presented under the title “Evolution Into Next-Generation CPO-based Connectivity Technology for Implementing Massive Computational Networks” [12]. And on November 18, 2025 this paper was submitted to Nature Electronics [2].
The summit structure deserves a closer look. The newsroom introduces the speakers of the sessions organized by SK hynix as three people together: Vice President Kyoung Park of Biz. Insight, Vice President Youngpyo Joo of System Architecture, and Professor Kyusang Lee [12]. Senior Vice President Hoshik Kim of Memory System Research appeared separately, on a keynote panel alongside David Patterson and Philip Wong [12]. So Lee is the outside speaker placed in the same row as company executives. The speaker photo caption in the newsroom groups the four of them on one line as well [12].
SK AI Summit 2025 speaker photo
Which of the two event days he presented on is not in the newsroom. So the gap to the paper submission is either 14 or 15 days, a bit over two weeks. I do not read that order as a coincidence. Whether the two schedules were actually coordinated is not something public sources can tell you.
Earlier I noted that only the University of Virginia survived in the X wording. The outside speaker SK hynix put on its own stage last November was that same Virginia professor [12]. Who the face of this collaboration is, from the company’s side, comes out the same on both channels.
What Lee singled out in the summit talk points the same way. He flagged the heat and wavelength drift problems of laser sources and raised micro-LED based sources as an alternative, as the newsroom summarized it [12]. In the August newsroom interview he named micro-LED again as the next research direction [1]. The same technology surfaces across three public touchpoints tied to SK hynix. An investor’s press release, a company event newsroom, and this announcement.
The paper spends a fair amount of space in that direction too. Avicena’s LightBundle putting a 304-channel micro-LED array on a 16 nm CMOS chip for roughly 1 Tbps at about 1 pJ per bit, and Microsoft MOSAIC’s 460-channel scaling outlook, appear in both Table 2 and the Outlook [2]. At the link level it even carries the numbers: at 800 Gbps the micro-LED approach draws 3.1-5.3 W per end against 9.8-12 W per end for laser-based active optical cables [2].
To be fair about it, one of the references the paper cites for micro-LED is the co-authors’ own work. A 2023 Nature paper making vertically stacked full-color micro-LEDs with two-dimensional material layer transfer, with Jeehwan Kim as corresponding author [13]. A review pointing at technology from its own camp is common enough, but readers should factor it in.
The public signals point toward SK hynix’s interest reaching into the micro-LED path as well. There is still nothing that would let you read it as dropping lasers and adopting micro-LED. What stands is that the interest showed up first in an investment fifteen months ahead of the paper.
6. The same cap table
Wind the Avicena investor list back further and there are two more names ahead of SK hynix.
The USD 25M Series A of August 2, 2022 had Samsung Catalyst Fund, Cerberus, Clear Ventures, and Micron Ventures as a strategic investor [14]. Micron described the investment at the time as a strategic opportunity to support innovation in HPC architectures [14].
So all three memory makers put their names on this company’s investor list. Samsung and Micron in 2022, SK hynix in 2025. Holdings are not disclosed, so whether any of them still hold is unverifiable. The round participation itself is on the record in each company’s release.
What catches the eye is the order. On the same bet in micro-LED interconnect, SK hynix came in 2 years and 9 months later. The company that is ahead in HBM is the late one on this layer.
Overlay the Samsung GRO micro-LED grant to UIUC’s Hyunseok Kim [8] and Samsung money is inside this lineage too. Two memory companies attached to the same school, with micro-LED as the contact point.
On September 3, 2025 Avicena named Marco Chisari as its new CEO. He is described as a former EVP who headed Samsung Foundries and the Samsung Semiconductor Innovation Center [15]. Founder Bardia Pezeshki moved to CTO [15].
There is one more trace on the standards side. SK hynix is listed as a member of the IOWN Global Forum [16]. The forum was set up in 2019 by NTT, Intel and Sony, and its subject is optical disaggregation. Venture investment, forum membership and paper co-authorship share one thing: they are activities at the standards layer rather than a product programs.
A narrow layer called micro-LED interconnect now holds investment from all three memory makers, with a former Samsung foundry executive in the CEO seat. On top of that, SK hynix attached an academic partner and laid a Nature Electronics roadmap over it, which is this August announcement.
I read that as SK hynix organizing the board late. Late on the investment, so it takes a position on the standards and roadmap side instead. The investment, the executive hire and the paper are separate events from separate parties. No source has a sentence tying them into one decision, and reading them together is my interpretation. That Seunghoon Hong’s day job is long-term strategy [1] does not contradict it.
There is material pulling the other way, though. The person on stage for the same subject at last September’s Future Forum was the head of Advanced PKG Development [5]. So the strategy side is not the only place in the company that has talked about CPO in public. Across fifteen months, venture investment, package development and long-term strategy each showed up on a public stage with this subject. I think that is the heaviest fact on this track.
This layer itself was covered before in The microLED Light Source Was Just Avicena. A Quarter Later, Five Listed Tickers Piled In. That piece looked at the listed-ticker mapping and did not look at the memory-three axis. Its judgment stands, and what I am adding here is the layer where three memory makers line up on the demand side.
7. The distance still left
So is this something that attaches to next year’s earnings. The numbers in the paper make that hard to read.
paper Table 2 energy column, pJ per bit by platform
Start with energy. A rack draws 30-100 kW, and to hold interconnect power under 10% of that the paper’s requirement is getting below 1 pJ per bit [2]. Reported values for current optical I/O chiplets are Intel OCI at about 5 pJ per bit, Ayar Labs TeraPHY under 5, and Lightmatter Passage at 4.6 on a wall-plug basis [2]. Against pluggable transceivers at 15-30 that is a long way down, and the target line is still several times away.
The paper names what is holding the gap open. Residual DSP and FEC processing, laser wall-plug efficiency, thermal regulation and SerDes power, none of which shrink just because the photonic devices get better [2]. That is where the micro-LED path gets its appeal. The device modulates directly, so the high-speed electrical conversion step can come out entirely [2].
Micro-LED carries a different price instead. The emission spectrum is over 10 nm wide against under 1 pm for a laser, the beam spreads so custom micro-optics are required, and reach stops in the tens of meters [2]. The paper’s position is that these constraints do not matter much inside CPO, because the chip-to-chip distances are short to begin with [2].
What each light source family buys and gives up is laid out in The AI Light Source War Is Not a Speed Race: InP, VCSEL, and μLED Are Buying Different Distances. It compares which distance bracket each one is really buying, across InP-based edge emitters like EML and DFB, the vertical-cavity VCSEL, and micro-LED. The logic this paper uses to place micro-LED inside CPO comes down to distance as well. Over short spans the price of spectral width and beam shape gets small, and the gain from dropping the modulation stage is what remains.
newsroom optics-centric architecture diagram, XPU pool and memory pool
The price on the memory side is a separate item. The paper lists three tasks as equally necessary for the long-term build, with photonic switching and distributed signal regeneration as the other two, and this as the first.
“optical memory interfaces capable of preserving coherence and minimizing latency” [2]
Coherence is the protocol that keeps several compute units from seeing different values when they look at the same memory address. Coherence protocols are extremely sensitive to round-trip delay. Going optical buys bandwidth but adds conversion stages and link latency. So the moment memory moves outside the package, a latency budget and a coherence constraint get stacked on top of bandwidth.
The paper’s target line is under 10 ns chip to chip, and it also says that in real systems, once switch hops, host SerDes, residual FEC and network protocol overhead pile on, the end-to-end round trip lands in the microsecond regime [2]. A fast optical link and faster memory access remain two different things.
Then there is temperature. Semiconductor laser emission wavelengths shift by tens of picometres per kelvin [2]. What the paper actually writes is that this drift becomes a problem when the CPO laser shares a thermal envelope with the ASIC or accelerator [2]. What happens when HBM enters the same budget is not something the paper settles. DRAM is a temperature-sensitive device to begin with, so I think that combination is not easy, and that is my guess rather than the paper’s claim. Rene Do’s line about lasers eating too much power [11] and Lee’s point at the summit about heat and wavelength drift [12] are pointing at the same problem.
On maturity, here is the wording. Lee assessed the technology as having already left the laboratory and entered the early stage of industrialization [1]. Early stage of industrialization, which is not the same as volume production. The paper itself puts chiplet-based heterogeneous integration on the near-term path and places the photonic interposer and optical TSV in the stage after that [2].
8. How far I take this
Limits first. A review paper and a press release are not a product roadmap filing. They are not a promise to mass-produce a given technology, and they carry no information on development stage or schedule. The same goes for the venture investment. Series B participation is not an adoption decision. The company did write down the possibility of development collaboration, but with potentially attached, and there has been no follow-up announcement that the collaboration actually started. How much SK hynix put into the round, and whether it is also in other optical interconnect companies, is undisclosed, so I do not know how spread the portfolio is either. The paper carries no SK hynix data or demonstration results, and a review by format is a summary of other people’s results.
To be fair I will note the March item too. Domestic reporting said SK hynix was developing a structure that puts optical devices inside the HBM package, and the company officially denied it [17]. The report rests on anonymous expert comment, so I do not use it as evidence. That said, what was denied then and what the company has put out under its own name are different layers. The product development claim was denied. The investment and the roadmap paper went out on its own channels. That split alone tells you which layer the company is moving on.
I still keep this as a signal. Documents pointing the same way came out three times in three different forms, through money, through a company event stage, and through an academic journal. Any one of them on its own would be ordinary enough. Getting all three inside fifteen months, in sequence, aimed at the same technology, is what keeps me on this file.
The optical memory interface is a spec that foundries making interposers, logic companies making accelerators, and companies selling optical engines decide together with the memory company. So on this layer the side that got directly priced by this investment is the optical interconnect vendor. Not far off the three-chip mapping from earlier.
The moment memory actually gets priced here is when the interface spec shows up as a document. Once optical memory link latency and coherence protocol land in a standards document, it turns into a question of who pulls dies matching that spec first. Until then this announcement alone is not something that moves a price target on SKHY or MU.
I could be reading this picture wrong, of course. If SK hynix puts out an optical interface paper carrying its own measured data at the next conference, or writes an optical I/O line explicitly into an HBM roadmap announcement, that is closer to a signal that a product program is running rather than standards-layer participation. Then the timing has to be pulled forward. If instead Avicena does not name a memory customer in its next round or production announcement, and no SK hynix name turns up in primary CPO material beyond this paper and this release through the first half of 2027, this is a venture arm’s investment and a strategy org’s outreach happening to overlap.
The next checkpoints are tied to public schedules. SK hynix’s next-generation HBM technology session at SEMICON Taiwan in September 2026, and the memory interface session at ISSCC in February 2027, are the ones with dates on them. SK AI Summit 2026 was in November for the past two years so it will likely land around then, but the official schedule is not out. Add to those whether Avicena names a memory customer in its next raise or product announcement. That one runs on the release rather than on a calendar date. The collaboration possibility written down in May 2025 turning into an actual joint development announcement is the highest-value mark in this piece.
The one I am personally watching most is the second. They put an outside professor into their own session at last year’s summit, so who takes that seat this year will show the pace of this track directly.
