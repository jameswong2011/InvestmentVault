SK hynix announced on August 20 that its co-packaged optics roadmap paper had run in Nature Electronics[1]. CPO puts the optical chip that moves signals as light inside the same package as the compute chip.
I opened the paper right away and followed the twelve authors’ backgrounds and where the company had put money before that, which I wrote up in One Interconnect Company, All Three Memory Makers on the Cap Table: Fifteen Months of SK hynix’s CPO Track [ https://substack.com/redirect/1b07affa-2edc-4150-b5e8-0a0022c9e9a9?j=eyJ1Ijoib3Q0aG4ifQ.0B7MD9zCy-cjHSiGhq6ULoNd-PLvhKEGaA3BvyeW924 ]. Something stayed with me after I filed that one.
The paper is a review. It reads less like a company product roadmap and more like a summary of where the field has gotten to, with tables of energy and bandwidth per integration method and a list of problems still open.
The newsroom post had a different flavor. There the company put its own name behind a bigger claim, that memory would be bound into a single pool to get past the memory wall[1]. The paper surveys, the announcement points.
So I got curious. Is this only SK hynix’s direction?
Samsung is already out in public. Its foundry declared entry into silicon photonics in March and published a schedule. That leaves Micron, which has no roadmap, no paper, and no press release on optics from the last ten years.
So I went to the patents. And one filing came back with a February 2023 priority date that is still under examination. The title is optical signaling for stacked memory device architectures. Memory dies stacked up, with light running between them.
Something the company has never said out loud is sitting at the patent office right now.

1. Why This Comes Up Now
Memory bandwidth has come up several times in this series. The routes to more bandwidth and the paths for moving capacity outside the package are laid out in Three Routes Around the Memory Wall: Qualcomm’s Stack, Nvidia’s 3 Bits, and What Remains [ https://substack.com/redirect/3d790233-8d6f-42ae-ac71-091616fffa7a?j=eyJ1Ijoib3Q0aG4ifQ.0B7MD9zCy-cjHSiGhq6ULoNd-PLvhKEGaA3BvyeW924 ].
The short version. Putting memory right beside the GPU made it fast and also made capacity hard to grow. Stacking more layers is getting expensive in heat and yield. So the remaining path is to move memory outside the package and connect it through a channel.
Right now that channel is copper. CXL is the one. It works, though round trip latency runs somewhere around double what local memory costs. The number moves with the device, the number of switch hops, and the memory media, so pinning it to one figure is hard. Either way it is slower, which means software has to keep deciding what sits near and what sits far.
The targets in the SK hynix paper are worth noticing here. More than 100Tb/s per node, under 1 picojoule per bit, and chip to chip latency below 10 nanoseconds[1][2]. That last number is aggressive. It is a target for the chip to chip optical link though, not for end to end memory access, since controller, protocol, queuing, and coherence costs stack on top of it. Even so, a link at that level would make tier management much lighter than it is today.
The paper says as much itself. It leaves an optical memory interface that keeps coherence while lowering latency on the open problems list[1]. The picture got drawn and the method got assigned as homework.
Here is where it splits, why a memory company would look at this directly. Turning the channel optical used to belong to the networking companies and the foundries, since CPO started by putting an optical engine beside a switch. But when the thing at the far end of the channel becomes memory, the situation changes. The optical chip has to come inside the memory package, and the company that designs, assembles, and tests that package is the memory company. It stops being somebody else’s problem.
2. Samsung Is Already Standing There
I mapped Samsung once in July. In TSMC Is Ahead in CPO. Samsung Is Putting a Third Chip Next to HBM [ https://substack.com/redirect/a9ca1797-386c-40c1-a923-4482da2c2921?j=eyJ1Ijoib3Q0aG4ifQ.0B7MD9zCy-cjHSiGhq6ULoNd-PLvhKEGaA3BvyeW924 ] I laid out who holds which of the chips that go on the interposer, and Samsung was the only one holding memory, logic, and the optical chip together.
It has moved further since. At OFC 2026 on March 17 the foundry division formally declared entry into silicon photonics and said its PDK was complete[3]. A PDK is the toolkit customers design with. Its arrival means customers have a base to start design and tapeout from. It does not mean production yield and process qualification are finished. The kit reportedly carries more than forty device models with a verification flow, on a 300mm process[3].
Why the PDK matters is that optical devices diverge from simulation more than electrical ones do. A modulator that behaves in the schematic often comes back strange on the actual wafer. The PDK is the foundry’s attempt to shrink that gap before handing it over, and without one you cannot build a repeatable standard design flow. That is also why Samsung bothered to mention that free carrier absorption and self heating went into the models[3].
The schedule went public too. Thermocompression bonded optical engines in 2027, turnkey CPO service in 2029[4].
That is the visible picture. Samsung tells you because Samsung has to sell. A foundry only has a business when customers knock, and that means saying where you are, repeatedly.
So what about Micron?
I searched by silicon photonics, optical I/O, CPO, and conference presentations, and nothing recent comes back. Recent OFC and ECTC programs carry no Micron optical talk, and no optical interconnect startup investment shows up either. As far as I can find, the company has issued no press release on the subject at all.
When there are no announcements you look somewhere else. I opened the patent file and the job postings.
What follows is that record. Line the three companies up by priority date and the order comes out different from the market’s impression, and on Micron’s side one filing is still waiting on examination. The qualifications on a currently open job posting carry a word the company has never put in a press release. The dates on the Boise lab announcement and the SK hynix Indiana groundbreaking sitting a week apart, and Micron’s continued absence from the US government optical programs, both come up later.
The last part is the value chain. Everybody already says packaging benefits when memory gets pooled, so instead I pulled four layers that rarely get named. They are process steps you pass through whichever way the architecture goes.

3. No Announcements, So Open the Patent File
Digging turned up all three companies. And the starting point sits much further back than I had assumed.
First, this idea is more than twenty years old
Binding memory with light predates the silicon photonics wave by a long stretch.
Micron has a patent from 2002[22]. It uses multiple wavelengths to carry more than one bit per pulse and links the hub on a memory module to the memory controller optically. Samsung filed in 2008 on connecting a memory controller and memory modules over optical channels[23].
The technology of the day could not deliver it. Light sources, modulators, and detectors were nothing like today’s, and more to the point, electrical wiring still had room.
So the order below is not when each company first thought about this. It is when each one touched the problem once you narrow to the silicon photonics package we know today, and within what I was able to find.
I will use the SK hynix paper as the yardstick, since several structures it presented this year already appear in these patents. Below, when I write “the SK hynix paper” I always mean that Nature review.
SK hynix: 2014
Two filings.
One is an optical modulator[5]. It was filed jointly with Seoul National University’s technology transfer foundation, and it puts an ITO transparent electrode on a Schottky diode to push past the speed ceiling of conventional modulators. The stated application in the specification is optical integrated circuits for chip to chip communication. An optical modulator is the core active device of silicon photonics, so this is a company designing the device itself rather than reading up on it.
The other one surprised me more. The title is semiconductor packages with optical interconnection structures[6], and the claim reads like this.
“A semiconductor package includes a first transceiver disposed on a top surface of a substrate; and a second transceiver disposed on a bottom surface of the substrate. The first and second transceivers optically communicate with each other through optical signals that permeate the substrate.”[6]
Optical transceivers on the top and bottom faces of a silicon interposer, with light passing straight through the interposer between them. The same company drew a photonic interposer concept with the same bones in that paper this year. Twelve years apart. Priority is November 2014, and it runs to 2035[6].
US9906312 drawing, cross-section of the optical path permeating the interposer
Samsung: three filings in 2018
January, an optical via inside HBM[7]. It routes the data path between the memory die and the logic die optically, while command and address keep some electrical connections. That overlaps with the SK hynix paper’s proposal to link stacked photonic and electronic chips vertically through optical TSVs.
February, a photonic integrated circuit package[8]. October, photonic integrated circuit devices[8]. Device, package, and optical wiring inside HBM, three layers covered within a year. Where the 2008 filing was about linking modules, this set comes inside the package. The foundation of what is now coming out as a foundry PDK is sitting here.
US11398453 drawing, optical via structure inside HBM
Micron: 2012, 2018, 2023
The first generation is 2012. The inventors are Roy Meade and Gurtej Sandhu, and Sandhu is a Micron senior fellow with more than 1,300 US patents. One of the heaviest inventors in the company.
This was the DARPA POEM program era. Micron participated and the award number is printed in the paper’s acknowledgment[12]. The paper’s title is this. Integration of Silicon Photonics in a Bulk CMOS Memory Flow[12].
The abstract:
“Monolithically integrated silicon photonic (SiP) devices have been demonstrated using a modified bulk CMOS flow. Integration was achieved by nominally extending existing process and design collateral without shifting the CMOS parameterization.”[12]
And the objective, in the body:
“The project’s objective is the demonstration of a cost-efficient optical interconnect on a memory process flow.”[12]
That is a Micron paper from 2013. And that sentence is effectively the same problem statement the SK hynix paper wrote thirteen years later. Two papers show up here and it gets confusing, so to be clear: the first is Micron’s own, the second is the SK hynix review I am using as the yardstick.
In the SK hynix paper’s integration hierarchy, monolithic integration sits in the last box, the hardest final step. What Micron touched in 2013 was a small scale version of that box.
One more thing comes out of it. The academic co-authors on that paper are Rajeev Ram and Vladimir Stojanovic of MIT and Milos Popovic of Colorado[12]. Popovic and Ram appear again on the 2015 paper.
And those three co-founded Ayar Labs in 2015[24]. An optical I/O startup, which raised $500M in a Series E this March at a valuation in the $3.7B range[24].
Micron wrote papers alongside the people who built Ayar Labs, inside the same government program. There is no basis for saying Micron’s intellectual property moved to that company though. The academic participants in the program founded it separately, and on Micron’s side the public output stops after that.
The results of the time should not be oversold either. The bulk CMOS flow had to be modified, extra process steps went in, and waveguide loss ran around 18 decibels per centimeter[12]. Active devices were left as future work. It proved a direction rather than approaching a product.
Looking only at public activity, this is where it stops. The patents keep coming.
The second generation is still alive
Priority is October 2018[10][11]. The inventor changed and the content is far more direct.
“A memory device includes a plurality of memories carried by an electro-optical substrate. The electro-optical substrate can include a circuit board and an optical routing layer on the circuit board. The memories can be (a) electrically coupled to the circuit board and (b) optically coupled to the optical routing layer.”[10]
US10928585 FIG. 5A/5B, memories on the electro-optical substrate and a cross-section of the optical routing layer
The other filing from the same date puts memory dies and an optical chip on one substrate together, has the optical chip convert between electrical and light, and connects individual memories directly to optical fiber[11]. Only power comes through the substrate electrically.
The SK hynix paper has a drawing with an XPU pool and a memory pool facing each other across a photonic interposer, and the memory pool side of that drawing overlaps structurally with this patent. The paper draws a whole system while this patent covers one memory module, so they are not the same object. What does hold is that the idea of bundling memory optically and putting it outside was already written into a 2018 Micron filing.
And this family has not closed. A continuation went in during 2021 and registered last May[11].
Continuations and maintenance cost money. Seven years from the 2018 priority to grant, holding on through a continuation along the way, means at minimum there was a reason to keep the family in the portfolio. Keeping a patent and intending to build a product are separate things though. Companies often hold for defense or for optionality, and a continuation itself can be a claim scope strategy rather than new research.
And a third generation is under examination
This one turned up last in the search and its scope is the most specific of the three.
Priority is February 2, 2023, and examination has not closed[20]. The inventors are Timothy M. Hollis and Eric J. Stave. The title is optical signaling for stacked memory device architectures.
The claims run like this. Memory dies stack vertically, and an interface component attaches to that stack carrying an array of optical receivers and emitters[20]. Electrical signaling from each memory die’s channels converts to optical there. And optical vias run vertically through the memory dies to couple each die optically to the interface component[20].
So it addresses the same problem as Samsung’s 2018 optical via inside HBM, filed five years later, still pending.
US20240268131 drawing, optical vias running vertically through the stacked memory dies
The specification also carries something I did not expect. It describes aligning an optical test probe above the optical interface and running full functional at speed testing through that interface directly[20]. How you inspect a memory that talks in light gets handled inside the same document. That comes back in section 6.
Narrowed to the silicon photonics family, Micron touched this three times, in 2012, 2018, and 2023, and the most recent one is the most specific and still in examination.
4. The Role Micron Is Hiring For
Patents are a record of the past. What a company is doing now shows up somewhere else, so I went to the job postings, and that is where I was most surprised.
This listing is up on Micron’s own careers system[13].
“Establish long-term network and optical interconnect strategy aligned to AI-scale and hyperscale datacenter requirements” “Define system and interconnect architectures across electrical and optical fabrics, endpoints, and scale-up/scale-out topologies“ “Lead technical engagements with hyperscale customers and ecosystem partners”[13]
And in the qualifications:
“12+ years of experience in optical interconnect, high-speed networking, silicon photonics, or datacenter system architecture”[13]
The same listing surfaces on some job sites under the title optical interconnect solutions architect, and that version is more direct. It says the hire will own the long-term optical interconnect strategy[13]. Both carry requisition number JR95848, so this looks like one role showing up twice.
Micron job posting, the optical interconnect strategy line
If you are wondering whether I misread an optical metrology or lithography role, silicon photonics sits in the qualifications as written, and the position faces hyperscale customers directly.
What the listing establishes is that this role got approved for hire. Whether the strategy already exists and they need someone to run it, or it does not and they need someone to build it, the posting alone does not settle. Either way the company has come far enough to put a full time person on the subject. Set beside a 2023 filing still in examination, the direction gets sharper.
5. Ten Billion Dollars and August 27
Micron announced on August 20 that it would build a research lab in Boise. Ten billion dollars over ten years, groundbreaking in 2027, capacity for hundreds of researchers[14].
Four research domains are named. Critical memory technologies, advanced memory and compute architectures, packaging, and future semiconductor manufacturing[14].
The word optical is absent. The last two leave a door open though, both because a memory company nailed compute architecture to its research list, and because doing CPO takes packaging capability underneath it. CPO itself is a system of PIC and EIC, light source, thermal management, and test all tangled together, so it does not reduce to packaging alone.
There is a date worth looking at.
SK hynix is building an advanced packaging fab in West Lafayette, Indiana. Around 5.9 trillion won, and along with HBM packaging it carries R&D and reliability evaluation centers[15]. A structure built to sit close to North American customers.
That groundbreaking is scheduled for August 27[16].
Micron’s announcement was August 20. A week apart.
The dates do sit close together, but reading mutual maneuvering into that would be a story built after the fact. Large announcements get scheduled around quarterly calendars and permitting, not by watching a competitor’s diary. So I will not use the proximity as evidence.
What is worth looking at instead is the character of the two investments. What SK hynix is building in the US is a fab, and it is a packaging fab. What Micron says it will build is a lab, and packaging is in the research list. Neither is a story about making memory cells better.
Both investments put weight on packaging and system architecture rather than cell scaling. Pinning the cause on a limit in cell technology alone would outrun the evidence, but where the budget is going is visible.
And there is no trace in the government channels
There was one thing to check here. Would traces of Micron contacting the optical industry or its experts show up in the government programs?
The US has AIM Photonics, a national institute for integrated photonics manufacturing. It sits in New York and takes Defense Department support. I opened the member list.
No Micron. No Samsung, no SK hynix, no Intel either[17]. The membership skews toward photonics specialists and defense contractors.
I looked at the CHIPS advanced packaging program too. The October 2024 solicitation shows the government set five research areas, and the third is connector technology including photonics and RF[18]. Optics named as an explicit line item in an advanced packaging budget. That second solicitation closed on September 24, 2025 though, and no final awards came out of that competition[18]. A live line item and money actually moving are separate things.
The first confirmed awards, from January 2025, put three hundred million dollars into substrates and materials, split evenly across three recipients[18]. A hundred million into a glass core ecosystem, a hundred million into silicon core substrates, a hundred million into fan out wafer level processing. Rather than the government picking glass, it placed three parallel bets of the same size. That glass took a third of it still means something.
There is a more recent one. In July 2026 the Commerce Department issued letters of intent to seven companies totaling $874 million, on a package that names integrated photonics and advanced packaging[21]. Among them is up to $300 million proposed to GlobalFoundries for co-packaged optics[21]. Letters of intent sit before final award, with diligence and approval still ahead. Still, it signals that the government keeps aiming money at this area.
Micron is not there either.
On the national semiconductor technology center, the only Micron item I could confirm in public materials is its chief people officer joining a workforce advisory board. Workforce advice rather than technical participation. The full membership is not public, so this cannot settle whether technical participation exists.
So across the government program lists, industry consortium rosters, and recent conference programs I opened, none of them show Micron optical activity. What does show up is in house patents and in house hiring. The company was not quiet from the start, since it published papers in 2013 and 2015. What stopped, after that stretch, was the activity that faces outward.
I read that as saying something about character. Samsung is a foundry and has to call customers in. SK hynix cannot do this alone and has to gather partners and standards. If Micron is drawing a picture where an optical chip goes inside its own memory package and sells straight to hyperscalers, it has relatively little reason to broadcast. The job listing describing direct technical engagement with hyperscale customers does not contradict that reading. This is my interpretation though, and a company staying quiet simply because it has nothing worth showing yet cannot be ruled out.
6. Four Layers That Are Easy to Miss
When memory moves outside the package and the channel becomes light, who earns. Optical engines, light sources, silicon photonics foundries, packaging. Everyone is already watching that far, and it is priced to some degree.
What caught my eye was the layer beneath. Going through three companies’ patents and the government program lists, the same items kept recurring.
Attaching fiber to a chip
Look at that Micron 2012 patent again. Liquid crystal steers the beam so light out of an optical fiber lands on the waveguide on the chip, and a sensor reads coupling power to adjust the voltage[9].
Why would a company patent that. Because doing the alignment mechanically is that hard. A fiber core is thinner than a hair, it has to land on an on chip waveguide within micrometers, and it has to hold that through thermal cycling for years. CPO yield and cost split right here. Which is why the problem drew the company’s most senior inventor rather than a device specialist.
The further optics comes inside the package, the more weight lands on alignment equipment, fiber arrays, and the materials used at that joint. A place you pass through regardless of who makes the optical engine.
Testing with light
Electrical chips get probed with needles on a probe card. Optical chips cannot. Light has to go in and come out, and doing that at wafer level means entirely different equipment.
And once optics attaches to memory this gets much larger. Memory sells on volume, so per unit test time lands directly on cost. Switch chips and memory differ by orders of magnitude in annual units. A few extra seconds of optical test is margin.
The Micron 2023 patent comes back here. Its specification carries that passage about aligning an optical test probe over the optical interface to run full functional at speed testing[20]. The company laid out an inspection method inside the same document where it designed the optical memory structure. A signal that this process works as a constraint at the design stage rather than as an attachment bolted on later.
I broke this layer into four stages in The 100-Second Bottleneck Behind NVIDIA CPO: 7 Companies That Own the 4-Stage Test Stack [ https://substack.com/redirect/a5fff756-a35e-4b44-b696-5fb3e322cd00?j=eyJ1Ijoib3Q0aG4ifQ.0B7MD9zCy-cjHSiGhq6ULoNd-PLvhKEGaA3BvyeW924 ]. That was written looking at switch side CPO, and moving the target to memory puts the same bottleneck in front of far larger volumes.
The substrate light travels through
Read the Micron claim again and this line is in it. A circuit board and an optical routing layer on top of it[10]. Meaning the substrate itself has to carry a path for light.
The material has not settled. The Micron patent contemplates an optical routing layer that includes polymer waveguides[10]. What is true is that organic substrates get less favorable as reach and dimensional stability requirements climb, which is why glass comes up as a strong candidate. Not a confirmed path.
I mapped this cycle company by company in [Investment Map] 15 Companies in the Glass Substrate Cycle: From Material to Mass Production [ https://substack.com/redirect/42051b4d-6ac3-4278-aed2-4c9e13f7ab93?j=eyJ1Ijoib3Q0aG4ifQ.0B7MD9zCy-cjHSiGhq6ULoNd-PLvhKEGaA3BvyeW924 ].
Design tools
The Samsung PDK came up earlier, and what actually runs a PDK is EDA tooling. However precise the foundry makes its device models, they have to run inside the customer’s design environment to be worth anything.
One more thing stood out in the AIM Photonics member list. Not one large memory company, and two EDA companies present[17]. Optical EDA rarely gets covered because the market looks small, and it is the layer that attaches first once PDKs start landing.
What the four have in common
Whether Samsung wins or SK hynix wins or Micron wins, a good share of these four is needed on any path.
Who builds the optical engine, whether the light source is a laser or a microLED, whether a foundry or a memory company holds the interposer. None of that is settled. But whichever configuration gets chosen, light has to leave the chip somewhere, it has to pass inspection in that state, and design tools have to exist. Configurations using connector style coupling instead of fiber arrays, or routing inside the PIC instead of through the substrate, mean the four layers do not all carry equal weight.
In a stretch where you cannot call the winner, this is usually the kind of place I look. The time axis is what I said earlier though. These layers receive memory volume only after CPO moves toward memory, and that is still drawn in a paper and a pending application. The volume these companies take today comes from switches and accelerators.
So it helps to convert these four into observable items rather than leaving them as a theme map. On alignment and fiber arrays, the CPO share of orders and equipment lead times. On test, new orders for optical probing equipment and the memory customer share. On substrates, when glass substrate pilot lines start running and whether they clear customer qualification. On design tools, whether tools supporting optical PDKs actually get used in customer tapeouts. Until those numbers move, this stays at the story stage.
7. Where This Goes
Let me lay out just the Micron ones by date.
A 2002 optical memory bus patent. A 2012 priority filing from the DARPA POEM period. Papers in 2013 and 2015. A 2018 patent putting memory and an optical chip on one substrate. A continuation in that family in 2021. A 2023 application on optical signaling for stacked memory. Grant in 2025. And as of August 2026, an open hire for optical interconnect strategy.
The filing record has no gap. What stopped after 2015 was conferences and press releases, not applications.
That said, this does not let me claim research ran without pause for twenty years. Patents recurring at several points and an organization running continuously in between are different claims, and I have no way to confirm the second.
Here is as far as I can go. A picture where Micron walked away from optical memory does not match the public record. New priority dates appeared in 2018 and 2023 after the announcements disappeared, and right now a job posting naming optical interconnect strategy is open.
Priority dates say nothing about when a company started researching, or how mature that research is. A patent existing does not make a product either. Samsung and SK hynix have starting points of 2008 and 2014, so their situations are not very different.
And where this flow heads is what I set out in Three Routes Around the Memory Wall [ https://substack.com/redirect/3d790233-8d6f-42ae-ac71-091616fffa7a?j=eyJ1Ijoib3Q0aG4ifQ.0B7MD9zCy-cjHSiGhq6ULoNd-PLvhKEGaA3BvyeW924 ]. Getting past the bottleneck through memory alone is hard. Making cells finer or stacking more layers keeps getting more expensive. As the weight shifts toward packaging and system architecture, optics comes in as an increasingly strong option. Electrical SerDes improvements, near memory compute, compression, and tiering all advance alongside, so calling optics the only destination would go too far.
The time axis needs care. Optical memory is a long story. Of what the three companies have put furthest forward, SK hynix’s is a paper and that paper left coherence protocols as an open problem[1], and Micron’s is an application still under examination[20]. Neither has arrived, so neither is an event that lands in near term results.
What comes in as revenue now is still electrical. CXL memory expansion products hold that slot, and Micron has two CXL 2.0 expansion modules certified on Red Hat Enterprise Linux 9.3[19]. Optics comes after.
Whether the optical interconnect strategy role Micron is hiring for actually gets filled and gains headcount behind it, whether the 2023 filing moves to grant and new priority dates follow, and whether optics gets named explicitly under the Boise lab’s packaging line, are the next steps. On Samsung the equivalent signals are the 2027 optical engine schedule and whether a customer tapeout using the PDK appears. On SK hynix, whether the Indiana site’s scope widens beyond HBM packaging.
The other direction is open too. If the hiring ends at one or two roles and does not repeat, this reads as opening a position rather than staffing an organization. If the 2023 filing fails to register or no new priority dates follow, the patents become assets held and nothing more. And if optical memory cannot solve coherence and keeps slipping, all three companies sit in the electrical tier for a long time, which pushes this article’s time axis back wholesale.
For the record, the hypothesis I started this with was that Micron had fallen behind on optics, because there were no announcements. Digging changed it. I confirmed this time that I carry the habit of ranking by volume of announcements too.
