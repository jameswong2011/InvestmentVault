# Memory to 68% of Cloud Hardware Capex; NVIDIA >15% Server Price Hike

**Source:** https://techtimes.com/articles/325718/20260827/memory-will-cost-cloud-giants-more-gpus-2027-nvidia-uses-that-justify-15-server-hike.htm
**Date:** 2026-08-27 · **Brief score:** 10 · **Tags:** daily-intel-triage

## Why it matters (portfolio)
NEW vs prior Evertiq 68% clip: Bloomberg-reported NVIDIA >15% early-2027 Vera Rubin / Grace Blackwell server price hike driven by memory. Pricing power + wafer diversion for **000660** / **SNDK** / **285A**; ASP pass-through for **NVDA**.

## Article body

Memory has quietly become the dominant line item in cloud infrastructure spending — and the consequences have now arrived at NVIDIA's pricing desk. Market research firm TrendForce published its latest memory industry research on August 25, projecting that DRAM and NAND Flash combined will account for 68% of major cloud service providers' total hardware capital expenditure by 2027, up from 47% this year. That shift — from roughly half to more than two-thirds of every infrastructure dollar — is not primarily a price story. It is a story about who controls cloud architecture: not compute, not networking, not storage, but memory. And three days before TrendForce published that finding, the prediction became concrete: Bloomberg reported on August 22 that NVIDIA has notified its largest customers that AI servers containing its Vera Rubin and Grace Blackwell chips will cost more than 15% more on units shipped in early 2027, with memory costs cited as the primary driver. The two data points form a single argument. Memory inflation is restructuring cloud economics from the inside, and every downstream buyer — including NVIDIA's largest hyperscaler customers — is now absorbing the cost. Why Memory Is Consuming Nearly Three-Quarters of Cloud Hardware Budgets The mechanism is structural and has been building since the second half of 2025. Server DRAM — the primary memory type used in AI and cloud servers — saw contract prices climb a cumulative 64% in that period, then accelerated sharply: TrendForce projects a further 270% increase for the full calendar year 2026. Enterprise SSDs, the NAND Flash-based storage products favored by data centers, followed a nearly parallel trajectory, rising roughly 35% in the second half of 2025 and forecast to surge 235% across 2026. The driver in each case is the same: High Bandwidth Memory. HBM is not simply a faster version of the DRAM inside a laptop. It is a fundamentally different architecture: multiple DRAM dies stacked vertically and connected through microscopic copper pillars called Through-Silicon Vias (TSVs), with the resulting package placed directly alongside the processor on a silicon interposer through a manufacturing process called CoWoS (Chip-on-Wafer-on-Substrate). HBM3E delivers approximately 8 terabytes per second of bandwidth per package; a standard DDR5 module delivers roughly 100 gigabytes per second — an eighty-fold difference that makes HBM irreplaceable in AI accelerators. The tradeoff is wafer area. Producing one gigabyte of HBM requires three to four times the semiconductor fabrication capacity that producing one gigabyte of conventional DRAM requires. Every fab line redirected toward HBM therefore withdraws three to four times as many DRAM bits from the total market as it adds in HBM bits — and the three companies that control roughly 95% of global DRAM production (Samsung, SK Hynix, and Micron) have all made that redirection, because HBM earns margins conventional DRAM cannot match. TrendForce's August 25 report captures the result: HBM and Registered DIMMs combined will account for 51% of total DRAM bit supply in 2026. The majority of the world's DRAM production — measured in raw bits — is now flowing into server and AI accelerator applications, leaving smartphones, PCs, consumer electronics, and automotive markets to compete for the rest. That is not a market imbalance. It is a permanent reallocation. Suppliers Are Choosing Sides — and Pricing Shows It The repricing is visible not just in server contracts but in supplier revenue. TrendForce's separate NAND Flash industry data, published August 18, shows the top five NAND brands combined revenue — Samsung, SK Hynix (including Solidigm), Micron, Kioxia, and SanDisk — jumped 77% quarter-over-quarter in the second quarter of 2026, reaching $68.87 billion. The jump was not primarily a volume story. The driver was pricing power: AI server demand created a genuine supply shortage, and suppliers extracted substantially higher prices through contract negotiations. The competitive rankings within that surge carry their own signal. Micron recorded the highest growth rate in the group, with NAND revenue up 99.2% quarter-over-quarter to $11.85 billion, lifting the company from fourth to third place in global NAND market share. Samsung retained first with $23.06 billion (up 70.7%), while SK Hynix ranked second at $14.27 billion (up 89.5%). Kioxia fell to fourth at $10.72 billion (up 79.9%); SanDisk was fifth at $8.97 billion (up 50.7%), the slowest growth in the group, constrained by conservative bit shipment strategy. In the HBM market specifically, SK Hynix has maintained a dominant position: IDC data cited in the company's 2026 F-1 SEC filing shows SK Hynix held a 56.4% share of HBM revenue globally in the first quarter of 2026, with Counterpoint Research placing the figure slightly higher at 58%. The strategic logic for suppliers is straightforward. AI infrastructure customers have proven willing to sign long-term agreements (LTAs), accept substantial price increases, and provide the kind of revenue visibility that consumer electronics customers cannot match. Some LTAs signed from the second quarter of 2026 onward have included price ceilings that could moderate further increases — but TrendForce still projects HBM contract prices will rise an additional 70% to 140% in 2027, and overall memory contract prices will remain broadly elevated. Related DRAM Buyers Face Two More Years of Scarcity as NAND Tilts Toward Surplus NVIDIA Rubin Ultra AI Chip May Deliver Less HBM Than Rubin, Forcing Procurement Replanning NVIDIA's Pricing Window The most direct consequence of memory's dominance of cloud capex is not the server cost itself — it is the competitive pricing cover it creates for NVIDIA. When memory consumes 68% of a cloud hardware budget, a 15% increase in GPU or accelerator pricing becomes a smaller percentage of an already-escalating total system cost. The buyer is already writing a larger check; the relative impact of a higher GPU price is diminished against a BOM that is already being pushed higher by memory inflation. TrendForce identified this dynamic explicitly in its August 25 report: elevated memory costs provide pricing justification for server and AI chip suppliers such as NVIDIA, and cloud service providers may subsequently need to increase capital expenditures further to maintain their targeted procurement volumes. Three days before TrendForce published that analysis, Bloomberg reported NVIDIA server price notifications to major customers. Server manufacturers supplying major data center operators — including Microsoft, Google, and Oracle — have informed their customers that AI servers containing Vera Rubin and Grace Blackwell chips will cost more than 15% more on units shipped in early 2027. The size of each increase varies by chip generation and memory configuration, with buyers of maximum-memory configurations facing steeper adjustments. NVIDIA has not publicly confirmed the specific figures, but the reporting has been corroborated by Bloomberg, Reuters, Fortune, and Tom's Hardware corroborated the hike , all citing people familiar with private communications. The numbers justify the mechanism. A single NVIDIA Vera Rubin GPU carries 288 gigabytes of HBM4. With HBM4 running at approximately $550 per stack at current market prices per the HBM4 stack cost estimate , the HBM content alone in a single Rubin GPU package amounts to roughly $4,400 to $5,000 at eight stacks — before any other components. An NVL72 rack, which combines 72 Rubin GPUs, carries 20 terabytes of HBM per rack , placing the HBM component cost at potentially $316,000 to $360,000 per rack before accounting for other memory, compute, networking, and power infrastructure. Memory has become one of the largest line items in any AI server's bill of materials. NVIDIA did not create that dynamic — but it has cited it as grounds for the price increase that its customers are now absorbing. Architecture Fork: Pay the Memory Tax or Redesign Around It For cloud service providers, the TrendForce analysis identifies two responses to a world where memory consumes 68% of hardware capex — and the choice between them may prove to be the most consequential architectural decision in cloud infrastructure history. The first response is straightforward: accept higher capital expenditures to maintain targeted AI chip procurement volumes. This is what the largest hyperscalers are currently doing. Goldman Sachs projects combined cloud capex for the leading providers at approximately $750 billion in 2026, rising to around $1.0 to $1.14 trillion in 2027 per the Goldman Sachs hyperscaler capex forecast . Some of that spending is already being funded with debt — Goldman Sachs notes that hyperscalers are expected to issue approximately $400 billion in investment-grade bonds in 2027 against an estimated $1.14 trillion in capex, a debt-funded share of roughly 35%. Morgan Stanley's own 2027 estimate runs higher still, at $1.4 trillion, on the reasoning that consensus growth projections imply implausibly slow non-AI cloud infrastructure expansion. The second response is architectural, and it is where the structural exit mechanism for the memory crisis lives. TrendForce identifies it explicitly: CSPs could more aggressively optimize AI system memory architectures by reducing memory capacity per system — adjusting RDIMM configurations, reducing the amount of HBM integrated into future AI chips, and accelerating the development of AI application-specific integrated circuits (ASICs) with model architecture hardwired directly into the silicon per TrendForce's architectural response analysis . This is not a distant possibility. All four major hyperscalers are already executing ASIC programs at significant scale. Google TPU v7 Ironwood specifications show 192 gigabytes of HBM3E per chip at 7.37 terabytes per second of bandwidth. Amazon Trainium3 HBM3E memory runs at 144 gigabytes per chip at 4.9 terabytes per second. Custom ASICs from the four major hyperscalers are growing at a 44.6% compound annual growth rate , targeting inference workloads that now represent two-thirds of all AI compute. Because inference ASICs are hardwired for specific model architectures, they can execute their target workloads with smaller memory footprints than general-purpose GPUs that must hold large and variable model states in accessible HBM. The feedback loop embedded in this dynamic is the one the data does not name: if ASIC adoption accelerates because of high memory costs, the per-compute-unit demand for HBM could begin to moderate — even before new fab capacity comes online in late 2027 or 2028. ASIC acceleration is not simply a cost-mitigation strategy for CSPs. It is the structural mechanism through which AI compute demand could begin to exert less pressure on HBM supply than current GPU-dominated architectures do. The duration and intensity of the memory crisis depend significantly on how quickly that transition occurs. Will 2027 Bring Any Relief? TrendForce's near-term supply outlook offers a mixed picture. Process node migrations and capacity ramp-ups at new fabrication facilities — primarily in the second half of 2027 — are expected to drive a 27% server DRAM supply increase in combined bit output. That additional supply, combined with still-elevated prices, is what TrendForce says will push memory's share of CSP capex to 68% in 2027: more bits at still-high prices, not a return to historical norms. The caveat is timing. Meaningful new production capacity — from Micron's Idaho facilities, Samsung's Texas plant, and SK Hynix's Indiana packaging center — is not expected to reach volume output before late 2027 or 2028. SK Hynix CEO Kwak Noh-jung stated that 2027 will be "the worst year in the industry's history from the supply perspective."
