# [Seeing Infrastructure] Part 5. Seeing Stack Map: Who Builds the Machine’s Eyes?

### A public-evidence map of four industrial ecosystems across the United States, Japan, Korea, and Europe

As of August 11, 2026

The industrial power of a Seeing Stack does not come from one good detector. What matters is whether an ecosystem can repeatedly produce mission-critical layers, connect them to real programs, and maintain, substitute, and requalify them within an acceptable period if supply is interrupted. Whether that industrial control translates into revenue and profit for particular companies is a separate question.

This is not a ranking of which country has the best infrared detector.

The units of comparison are not fully symmetrical, either. The United States, Japan, and Korea are national ecosystems. “Europe,” as used here, is a multi-jurisdiction ecosystem combining France, Germany, Italy, the United Kingdom, and European Union programs. Leonardo’s infrared detectors are manufactured in Southampton, under British jurisdiction, while the company is controlled by an Italian parent. SPIRIT is a European Defence Fund project involving fifteen organizations across nine countries. Europe’s broad stack coverage therefore reflects both real industrial depth and the aggregation of several national systems.

Nor does this map reveal the entirety of each ecosystem’s industrial capacity. Much of the defense and space supply chain is classified or commercially undisclosed. What this essay maps is the minimum capability that can be verified from public evidence as of August 11, 2026.

To read that evidence properly, four questions must be separated.

1. Does a technical and manufacturing capability exist within the ecosystem?
2. Are the layers linked through an actual program supply chain?
3. Can the relevant jurisdiction control design, production, substitution, and sustainment if supply is interrupted?
4. Does that control translate into economic capture through contracts, margins, software rights, or maintenance revenue?

The existence of capability, supply-chain linkage, sovereign control, and corporate value capture are not the same claim.

## 1. An industrial base is not a list of components. It is a connected supply chain.

Suppose a region has a detector manufacturer, a readout-circuit designer, a cryocooler supplier, and a payload integrator.

That does not yet prove that it possesses a complete Seeing Stack.

Those companies may serve different markets. Their products may never have been qualified together in the same system. Critical process knowledge or design authority may sit abroad. Replacing one supplier could require redesigning the optics, readout electronics, calibration software, and thermal architecture, followed by years of retesting.

The first axis of this map is therefore coverage. Does the ecosystem contain sensing materials, detector devices, readout circuits, focal-plane assemblies, cooling systems, camera modules, payloads, and mission-system capabilities?

The second is program linkage. Are those capabilities connected through a qualified supply relationship inside a specific satellite, aircraft, ground-vehicle, or naval program?

The third is control and substitutability. Who owns the design authority and process knowledge? Where do critical wafers and production tools come from? Can production continue if export licenses are withdrawn? How much redesign and requalification would be required to replace a supplier?

The fourth is economic capture. A supplier can be mission-critical without earning high margins. Low production volumes, heavy capital expenditure, poor yields, fixed-price contracts, and the bargaining power of prime contractors can all suppress supplier profitability.

Mission criticality may be necessary for economic value capture, but it is not sufficient.

Supplier scarcity, switching and requalification costs, contractual leverage, software and data rights, and long-term sustainment responsibility must also be present.

## 2. The Seeing Stack is a nested assembly hierarchy, not a linear chain

It is technically misleading to draw the Seeing Stack as a simple sequence of:

material → FPA → ROIC → cooler.

The readout integrated circuit, or ROIC, integrates, amplifies, and multiplexes the detector output. Depending on the architecture, it may provide analog output or incorporate analog-to-digital conversion and digital processing closer to the pixel.

When a detector array is hybridized with a ROIC, the result becomes a hybrid focal plane array, or FPA. Packaging, a vacuum dewar, and a cryocooler may then be added. Depending on the company and program, this larger assembly may be described as a focal plane module, or FPM, or as an integrated detector cooler assembly, or IDCA.

AIM, for example, presents crystal growth, silicon readout-circuit design, hybridization, dewar integration, cryocoolers, image processing, and thermal-sight production as distinct but connected competencies.

This essay uses the following operational hierarchy.

At the bottom are sensing materials and detector devices: HgCdTe, InSb, InGaAs, T2SL, microbolometers, and related architectures.

The ROIC reads the detector response.

The detector array and ROIC are hybridized into an FPA.

Packaging, vacuum, thermal, and mechanical stabilization are added through a dewar and cooler.

The FPA and its thermal-mechanical assembly form an FPM or IDCA.

Optics, drive electronics, calibration, and video output turn that assembly into a camera core or sensor module.

Stabilization, target detection, tracking, fusion, and communications then create the payload and mission system.

But above this physical assembly hierarchy sits a separate control layer.

- Who owns the design authority?
- Where are process recipes and critical intellectual property held?
- Who controls access to the ROIC foundry and critical production equipment?
- Who owns qualification and acceptance data?
- Who controls calibration software, data rights, repair authority, upgrades, and export licensing?

A product may be assembled domestically while these rights remain abroad. In that case, domestic production does not necessarily equal supply-chain control.

Maturity must also be divided into distinct states. Completing development testing, passing environmental and performance qualification, receiving customer acceptance, obtaining production approval, entering field service, and supporting a system over decades are different forms of evidence.

Confusing them leads to a recurring analytical error: treating a prototype as equivalent to repeat production, repeat production as equivalent to fielding, and fielding as equivalent to long-term industrial control.

## 3. The United States: the clearest public link between programs and repeat production

In the public evidence reviewed here, the United States shows the most direct connection between large government programs, repeat FPM production, and payload or mission-system integration.

The strongest starting point is the Space Development Agency’s low-Earth-orbit missile-warning and tracking architecture.

In December 2025, SDA awarded four teams eighteen satellites each, for a total of seventy-two Tracking Layer Tranche 3 spacecraft under agreements worth approximately $3.5 billion. In July 2026, SDA awarded a separate accelerated program—AMDT3—covering thirty-six additional spacecraft worth approximately $1.75 billion.

The two award sets together cover 108 Tranche 3-related spacecraft. They are not, however, one homogeneous contract group. TRKT3 and AMDT3 involve different supplier teams, spacecraft variants, schedules, and contracting structures.

What distinguishes the US case is that public evidence extends below the program announcement into detector production.

In March 2025, Teledyne announced delivery of the 100th large-format FPM across SDA Tracking Layer Tranches 0, 1, and 2. This is not evidence of a product concept or a supplier selection. It is evidence of cumulative deliveries.

In February 2026, Teledyne announced that it had begun production of infrared FPMs for multiple TRKT3 awards. Together, the two announcements show that Teledyne had moved from repeat delivery across the earlier tranches into production for the next generation.

Above that sits platform integration.

RTX’s public description of the next-generation F-35 EODAS links strained-layer superlattice detector material to an FPA, digital-pixel technology, a digital ROIC, six infrared cameras, and a helmet-mounted display. This is useful vendor evidence of how the detector-to-platform stack is configured. It does not independently validate operational effectiveness or the economics of each layer.

The US advantage is therefore not simply that it contains many detector companies.

It is that public evidence shows a relatively direct sequence:

1. Government demand creates a program.
2. Lower-tier suppliers enter repeat FPM production.
3. Satellite and aircraft payloads integrate those components into larger mission systems.

Yet major uncertainties remain.

Teledyne’s FPM yields, unit prices, and program margins are undisclosed. Supplying multiple primes does not automatically establish long-term exclusivity or pricing power. The lower-tier supply chains of classified programs remain largely invisible. The division of value between detector devices and ROICs, and the time required to redesign and requalify an alternative supplier, cannot be determined from public sources.

In the United States, the program-to-production linkage is clear. Lower-tier excess returns are not.

## 4. Japan: the new dual-band detector is prototype-backed, but the broader industrial base is not merely a prototype

It would be too reductive to describe Japan as a prototype-only detector ecosystem.

A more accurate judgment is:

Japan possesses an established infrared production base and fielded EO/IR integration capabilities. The new MWIR/LWIR dual-band T2SL defense detector, however, is supported publicly by prototype evidence rather than repeat-production evidence.

In March 2026, Fujitsu announced that it had developed and delivered an MWIR/LWIR dual-band T2SL infrared sensor with more than one million pixels under a research-prototype program sponsored by Japan’s Acquisition, Technology & Logistics Agency, or ATLA.

Fujitsu said it intended to begin using the manufacturing technology in new product development and sales from fiscal 2026. That strongly supports the existence of the new detector capability and the completion of a defense-linked prototype. It does not establish adoption into an operational weapon system, repeat production, production yields, or program revenue.

The same Fujitsu announcement says the company had already developed and mass-produced high-sensitivity infrared sensors. That supports the existence of a broader production heritage. Public evidence, however, does not clearly break down which cooled defense detector families were produced repeatedly, for which programs, or at what scale.

The correct label is therefore not “Japan’s detector base is prototype-backed.”

It is that the new dual-band T2SL class is prototype-backed.

Downstream integration is more visible.

Mitsubishi Electric publicly identifies infrared night-vision equipment for the US-2 search-and-rescue aircraft and a 360-degree surveillance imaging system for the Mogami-class frigate. This demonstrates real Japanese defense EO/IR platform-integration capability. It does not prove that every detector, FPA, ROIC, and cooler inside those systems is domestically sourced.

Japan’s public map therefore contains three distinct facts.

1. It has a next-generation detector prototype linked to a defense agency.
2. It has a broader infrared production heritage.
3. It has fielded aerospace and naval EO/IR integration.

What remains unclear is how these layers connect inside the new cooled-detector class, whether they support repeat production, and where the economics of that production would accrue.

The next evidence to watch is therefore straightforward:

- formal productization and program adoption of Fujitsu’s T2SL technology;
- the identities of FPA and FPM suppliers inside Japanese defense platforms;
- the ownership and location of ROIC design and wafer fabrication;
- the supply chains for cryocoolers and compound-semiconductor wafers;
- and the lower-tier sourcing structure inside Mitsubishi’s EO/IR systems.

Japan’s public-evidence bottleneck is not the existence of technology. It is proof of program linkage and repeat production for the new detector class.

## 5. Korea: repeat production is proven, but linkage and control remain partial

The assessment of Korea requires a substantial revision.

Korea can no longer be described simply as an ecosystem capable of payload integration but still waiting to prove a domestic detector base. Regulatory filings show that repeat infrared-detector production and revenue already exist.

i3system’s public product materials list InSb and T2SL MWIR products, T2SL LWIR, uncooled microbolometers, and InGaAs SWIR products. The published portfolio includes cooled arrays in formats up to 1280 × 1024.

This establishes the company’s technical scope and product portfolio. It does not mean that every listed format is produced in the same volume. Product listing, qualification, program adoption, serial production, and product-level revenue must be distinguished.

The repeat-production base is more strongly supported by regulatory filings.

In 2025, i3system reported USD 83.49 million in infrared imaging-sensor revenue, representing 95.4 percent of total revenue. Exports accounted for approximately USD 40.63 million.

The company also states that some military products are designated defense articles and are supplied after development testing, operational testing, and conformity assessment. This is substantially stronger evidence than a product webpage: it demonstrates real infrared-sensor production, sales, and at least partial military qualification.

Some program linkage is also visible.

i3system states that it supplies military infrared imaging sensors to system companies such as LIG Nex1 and Hanwha Systems, or to the Agency for Defense Development, which then integrate them into weapon systems.

This confirms a general defense supply relationship. It does not provide a program-level bill of materials identifying which detector format enters which Hanwha satellite, aircraft, ground, or naval EO/IR system.

Hanwha Systems separately presents satellite EO/IR and SAR payloads, electro-optical modules for military satellites, KF-21 mission-and-display-system integration, avionics, and electro-optical equipment.

This is evidence of payload, EO-system, and mission-system integration. It is not the same as being the final platform prime responsible for the entire aircraft, ship, or vehicle.

Korea should therefore not be mapped as a simple line:

i3system detector → Hanwha payload → every Korean platform.

The more defensible statement is:

Korea contains both repeat detector/FPA production and multi-domain payload, EO-system, and mission-system integration. A general defense supply relationship between these layers is visible, but program-specific linkage still requires verification.

There are also clear upstream dependencies.

i3system states that specialized compound-semiconductor wafers used for cooled infrared and X-ray sensors are imported. Its 2025 raw-material purchases show that cooled-infrared lenses were overwhelmingly imported: domestic purchases amounted to USD 5.38k, compared with approximately USD 3.20 million in imports.

Cryocoolers are sourced through a mixed structure, including the Korean supplier FS and foreign suppliers Ricor and Thales Cryogenics.

By contrast, public evidence does not clearly establish the ownership of ROIC design authority, intellectual property, wafer-foundry access, or country of fabrication.

Korea therefore cannot yet be described as exercising complete full-stack control.

Korea has a production base proven through regulatory filings. But dependence remains in compound-semiconductor wafers and cooled optics, cryocoolers are sourced domestically and abroad, and ROIC control remains unclear.

Supplier concentration creates another tension. A concentrated domestic supplier base may represent national industrial control, but it can also become a single point of failure.

## 6. Europe: a broad industrial base, but not a single sovereign jurisdiction

It would be inaccurate to describe Europe merely as the custodian of a historical infrared-detector heritage.

Across France, Germany, the United Kingdom, and other European jurisdictions, an active industrial base covers material growth, detectors, ROIC design, hybridization, coolers, modules, cameras, and platform integration.

It would be equally inaccurate to call this one complete sovereign European stack.

The European Union and the United Kingdom operate under different legal and export-control regimes. Corporate control and manufacturing location are often separated. Some common programs are still future projects rather than current production capabilities.

France-based Lynred develops and manufactures cooled detectors and uncooled microbolometers. In 2026, the company opened its Infrared Campus near Grenoble, with 8,000 square meters of cleanroom space and a plan to double production capacity by 2030.

According to Lynred’s own disclosures, it delivered its 100,000th cooled detector in 2020 and has supplied more than three million infrared detectors in total. These are company-reported figures, but they indicate an existing industrial production base rather than a purely historical legacy. Planned capacity expansion, however, must be separated from current production.

Germany’s AIM presents CdTe and HgCdTe crystal growth and epitaxy, silicon ROIC design, hybridization, dewar integration, Stirling and pulse-tube coolers, image processing, and thermal-sight production as in-house competencies.

The company reported €90.7 million in 2024 revenue and says its products are fielded with the Bundeswehr and several NATO countries. This is strong vendor evidence of deep vertical integration within the European ecosystem.

But AIM’s ROIC capability is evidence of design authority, not proof that advanced CMOS wafers are fabricated inside an AIM-owned foundry.

Leonardo’s infrared-detector facility is located in Southampton in the United Kingdom.

The facility says it develops and manufactures cooled MCT and uncooled detectors and performs ROIC design support, material growth and processing, device hybridization, vacuum packaging, and environmental testing.

Leonardo UK, however, is wholly owned by Italy’s Leonardo S.p.A. This is a clear example of why manufacturing jurisdiction, corporate control, and program jurisdiction must be separated.

Exosens shows that Europe’s imaging base is also connected to current economic activity.

Its Detection & Imaging segment generated €77.4 million in revenue in the first half of 2026, up 24.3 percent year over year. The company cited defense and surveillance demand, including drone, counter-drone, and long-range surveillance markets, as growth drivers. It also said that it was doubling cooled-infrared-camera production capacity in 2026.

The segment includes acquired businesses and multiple imaging technologies, so the result cannot be treated as a clean measure of detector- or FPA-level profitability.

SPIRIT is the key case for separating current capability from future targets.

The project was announced in May 2026 but is scheduled to begin at the end of 2026 and run for four years. Fifteen organizations from nine countries plan to build a European supply chain extending from ROIC design to camera demonstrators, targeting detectors with more than three megapixels and pixel pitches below seven micrometers using advanced CMOS foundry access.

This is not evidence that an advanced European CMOS-ROIC supply chain already exists.

It is evidence that access to advanced CMOS processes and an end-to-end EU-based supply chain remain recognized gaps that Europe intends to address.

The defensible conclusion is therefore:

Europe contains a broad, active detector and optronics industrial base. That base is distributed across the EU, the United Kingdom, multiple corporate-control structures, and different program jurisdictions. Advanced EU CMOS access and an integrated end-to-end supply chain remain planned gap-closing objectives rather than completed achievements.

## 7. The four ecosystems are strong in different ways

The public evidence reveals different industrial structures rather than one clear ranking.

In the United States, large government demand, repeat FPM production, and mission-system integration are connected most directly in public documentation. The SDA and Teledyne cases provide the clearest program-to-component linkage. Lower-tier yields, margins, and supplier concentration remain undisclosed.

Japan combines an established infrared production base and fielded aerospace and naval EO/IR integration with a next-generation dual-band T2SL prototype. Repeat production and economics for the new detector class are still not publicly demonstrated.

Korea combines regulatory-filing-backed infrared-sensor production, sales, and exports with satellite and aircraft mission-system capabilities. Program-level linkage remains only partially visible, while dependencies remain in wafers, cooled optics, some cooling systems, and possibly the ROIC layer.

Europe shows the broadest coverage across several national jurisdictions: French production scale, German module and cooler integration, British detector manufacturing under Italian corporate control, and current revenue growth among imaging suppliers. But this is multi-country coverage, not unified sovereign control. The advanced EU CMOS layer remains a planned development target.

Reducing these structures to one ranking would obscure the real questions.

- For the United States: Does program scale translate into sustainable lower-tier economics?
- For Japan: Do prototype capability and fielded platform experience convert into repeat production of the new cooled-detector class?
- For Korea: Does domestic detector production expand into control over wafers, ROICs, optics, coolers, and program-specific supply linkage?
- For Europe: Can a broad but fragmented industrial base operate as a continuous supply chain during disruption, and can planned advanced-CMOS capabilities become actual production?

## 8. What matters is more than the existence of capability

What matters is not how many infrared companies a region contains.

What matters is who can repeatedly produce mission-critical layers and pass qualification and customer acceptance.

What matters is not merely whether all the relevant capabilities exist within one region.

What matters is whether those capabilities are connected through qualified supply relationships inside real programs.

What matters is not whether a product page lists T2SL, HgCdTe, or InGaAs.

What matters is whether the evidence supports a product announcement, a prototype, qualification, production approval, repeat production, fielding, or long-term sustainment.

What matters is not a single “domestic-content” percentage.

What matters is where dependence remains across design authority, critical wafers, ROIC foundries, cryocoolers, optics, calibration software, repair authority, and upgrade rights.

What matters is not merely the size of a program budget.

What determines economic value capture is how that budget is divided among detector suppliers, FPA and FPM manufacturers, cooler providers, payload integrators, processing-software companies, and prime contractors.

Industrial control is not the same as autarky.

- Can the mission continue if supply is interrupted?
- Is an alternative supplier available?
- How long would redesign and requalification take?
- Does the ecosystem have the inventory, equipment, and skilled personnel to survive that interval?

Practical control over the Seeing Stack is closer to the answer to those questions.

## 9. How this map could be wrong

This map relies on public evidence. A region with more visible documentation is not necessarily the region with the strongest absolute industrial capability. Different procurement, disclosure, and security practices can conceal real capabilities.

Published product specifications can also diverge from repeat-production performance. A high-resolution, high-sensitivity prototype may not be manufacturable at stable yields. A format listed on a product page may not correspond to a serial defense program.

The presence of all relevant companies within one region does not prove that they form a connected supply chain. Without program-level bills of materials and qualification records, the degree of linkage between a detector supplier and a payload integrator can remain unclear.

Domestic production does not guarantee complete control. If design authority, critical equipment and wafers, export licenses, test data, calibration software, or repair rights are held abroad, a supply disruption can still constrain the ecosystem.

Conversely, foreign sourcing is not automatically a weakness. An ecosystem with multiple qualified suppliers, sufficient inventory, alternative designs, and rapid requalification capability may be more resilient than one dependent on a single domestic source.

Finally, mission criticality and corporate profitability may diverge. An FPA can be difficult to replace but unprofitable because volumes are low and capital requirements are high. Meanwhile, a company controlling calibration data, software upgrades, mission-system integration, or depot maintenance may capture more long-term economic value.

The purpose of this map is therefore not to declare a winner.

It is to show the publicly verifiable floor of capability—and the evidence gaps inside the supply chain.

## Closing

The Seeing Infrastructure series began with one question:

Which missions turn photodetectors into strategic components, and which countries and companies actually control each layer of the stack?

Part 1 showed that a photodetector is not merely a material. What matters is whether weak optical and infrared signals survive optics, FPAs, ROICs, cooling, calibration, and processing to become mission-grade data.

Part 2 traced how an infrared scene becomes an onboard two-dimensional track and then a ground-fused three-dimensional track.

Part 3 examined how passive infrared measurements move through sensor fusion and pilot displays to affect decision and response time.

Part 4 showed how information in different infrared bands survives fog, dust, obscurants, and maritime clutter—or fails to become detection, recognition, identification, and a continuous track.

The industrial map leaves four distinct conclusions.

The United States shows the clearest public linkage between program demand and repeat component production.

Japan combines an established infrared base and platform integration with a next-generation dual-band prototype, but repeat production of the new class remains to be demonstrated publicly.

Korea has proven repeat detector and FPA production through actual revenue, but gaps remain in upstream materials, cooled optics, some cooling systems, and program-level supply linkage.

Europe contains a broad industrial base across several jurisdictions, but it is not one unified sovereign chain, and advanced EU CMOS capacity remains a planned gap-closing objective.

These differences should not be compressed into a simple country ranking.

The industrial power of a Seeing Stack does not end with possessing the necessary technology. It becomes a controllable industrial base only when that technology can be repeatedly produced, qualified and accepted inside real programs, and maintained, substituted, and requalified when supply is interrupted.

One final tension remains.

The layer most essential to the mission may not be the layer most difficult to recover after a supply disruption. And neither may be the layer that captures the most economic profit.

The detector device may be the first physical conversion layer.

The ROIC or cryocooler may be the hardest bottleneck to substitute.

The calibration-software or mission-system integrator may hold the strongest contractual leverage.

The prime contractor responsible for upgrades and sustainment may capture the largest long-term revenue pool.

The final question of the Seeing Stack is therefore:

How should we separate the bottleneck a state must protect from the profit pool a company analyst should follow?

As machines acquire strategic eyes, that distinction will become increasingly important.

## Appendix: Four definitions for reading the map

**Coverage** means that the relevant technical and manufacturing layers exist within the ecosystem. A product page or research history may establish capability, but it does not automatically establish repeat production.

**Program linkage** means that individual capabilities are connected through a qualified supply relationship inside a particular satellite, aircraft, ground vehicle, or naval system. The presence of a detector company and a payload integrator in the same country does not, by itself, prove linkage.

**Industrial control** means the ability to maintain design, production, repair, and upgrades during a supply interruption—or to substitute and requalify a supplier within an acceptable time and cost. Design authority, process knowledge, foundry access, export jurisdiction, test data, and sustainment rights may matter more than the physical location of final assembly.

**Economic value capture** means that mission importance translates into revenue, margins, software or data rights, and maintenance income. Mission criticality is important, but without supplier scarcity, switching costs, and contractual leverage, it may not generate excess returns.
