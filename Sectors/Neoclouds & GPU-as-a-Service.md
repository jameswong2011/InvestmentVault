# Neoclouds & GPU-as-a-Service

The listed names are priced as a commodity GPU-rental cycle heading into glut; the vault's own essays price them as property developers sitting inside a token factory whose cost compounds with watts (~40% a year) while output compounds with efficiency (~3x a year), so that dollar demand has only to grow with the slower of those two rates for rents to hold. We hold both reads as hypotheses. The credit ledger on the leveraged tranche (CoreWeave interest already at the ~20–25% of revenue historical break line; Nscale–Anthropic back-solved at ~$7.3 per Rubin hour on late-2027 MW) is the outside view that has to beat the developer frame before we treat coordination as a layer rather than a famine spike, and [[Theses/NBIS - Nebius Group]] at Full (25%+) in [[Live Portfolio]] is the live expression of that argument, not a sector-wide upgrade.

- Is the right category 'commodity GPU rental' (the multiple the market will not pay) or 'property development' (coordination of rationed power and rationed silicon, with the finished hall almost incidental), and is coordination a layer or a famine spike? ## Industry history

The neocloud category did not exist before 2022. Three pre-existing infrastructure stacks converged into it: crypto-mining operators with surplus power and rack space, VFX/scientific-computing GPU renderers, and a small set of ML-researcher tooling startups. The industry-creating event was not ChatGPT demand as such; it was Nvidia's decision, from late 2022, to allocate silicon directly to non-hyperscaler operators so that Amazon, Microsoft and Google could not corner the cloud layer and, with it, the bargaining power over the next accelerator generation. The most competent datacenter developers on earth (the CSPs) thus sit with empty halls they cannot fill on allocation terms they would like, while a set of five-year-old firms hold the silicon and learn the development trade with hyperscaler money. Microsoft's $60 billion-plus of neocloud commitments and Meta's $27 billion Nebius contract are what it looks like when the incumbent rents from the entrant. As long as that allocation policy holds, the wedge trains the developers to become what they bill; Nebius ships the software layer CoreWeave had to buy for $1.7 billion, and a firm renting GPU-hours to Microsoft while operating its own full cloud stack is a CSP in gestation. The wedge is rented. Nvidia's incentive to keep its largest customers from consolidating the cloud layer does not expire with this GPU generation, which is why the policy persistence, not the next backlog print, is the category's existence condition. The historical pricing-power trajectory: NVIDIA captured the GPU rents from chip sales; hyperscalers initially captured the GPU-hour rents at the cloud layer; neoclouds emerged when ChatGPT-era demand outstripped hyperscaler buildout pace and NVIDIA actively redistributed allocation to keep the cloud layer fragmented. Pricing power is upstream-pinned at NVIDIA; the entire neocloud complex is a derivative position on that allocation, and a coordination franchise on time-to-energisation sitting on top of it. Google paying SpaceX roughly $920 million a month on the xAI Colossus 2 campus (~$11.60 per GB200-class GPU-hour, sub-one-year payback on a ~$5bn 100MW build) is the famine extreme of the same model, not the equilibrium; normalise toward $6 and the payback stretches to about two years, which would still rank among the fastest capital recoveries heavy infrastructure has printed. Businesses that recover capital that fast either own extraordinary technology or sit inside an extraordinary shortage, and the neoclouds, on any honest reading of the reference architecture, own no technology at all.

## Competitive dynamics

The category bifurcates into two structurally distinct sub-segments with different durability profiles, and a third cut (what is sold, and on what tenor) that now does more work than the origin story.

| Sub-segment | Members | Origin | Durable asset | Primary risk |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Pure-play AI cloud** | CRWV, NBIS, Lambda (private), Crusoe (private), Nscale (private) | Built post-2017 around GPU rental from inception | NVIDIA allocation priority + customer contracts + (hypothesised) coordination record | Hyperscaler in-housing; customer concentration; GPU rental-rate compression vs. DDTL amortisation; COD slip |
| **Bitcoin-miner pivot** | IREN, APLD, CIFR, WULF, CORZ | Pivoted from crypto mining 2024-2026 | Land + grid interconnect + power contracts (NOT the GPU layer, except IREN full-stack) | GPU rental rates revert to depreciation-only economics; stranded-asset risk for the second time; IREN also inherits full-stack residual |

### The category: property developer, not commodity GPU renter

A property developer owns no technology. Its inputs all belong to other people: land optioned years ahead, money raised against presales, permission from planners, tenants who must sign before the foundations are poured, contractors who must arrive in the right order at the right price. The finished building is almost incidental; any competent builder can pour the concrete. What the developer sells is the elimination of sequencing failure across a dozen counterparties who do not answer to each other, and its margin is the spread between the cost of assembling those pieces without a hiccup and the value of the stabilised, tenanted asset. The datacenter developer is the special case in which two of the inputs are rationed rather than priced: grid connections in tier-1 markets now carry 24–48 month queues, so a power contract secured in 2023 is an asset no amount of 2026 money can replicate, and the equipment that fills the shell is allocated by a single vendor whose decision about who receives GPUs functions as the industry's planning permission. Preferential access to power and to compute equipment is thus the essence of the neocloud's alpha; everything else in the stack can be bought. ([[Website/2026-08-22 - Neoclouds - The Property Developers of Compute]])

| Development function | Property developer | Neocloud |
|---|---|---|
| Land bank | Plots optioned ahead of planning | Contracted power pipeline (Nebius: ~4.5GW at Q2 2026, >75% owned, 5GW year-end target; IREN 5.8 GW owned interconnect) |
| Planning permission | Zoning, permits, community consent | Interconnect queue (24–48 months), permitting, behind-the-meter generation |
| Presales | Off-plan deposits fund construction | Customer prepayments (~70% of Nebius Q2 deals prepaid, covering 50–60% of associated capex; IREN 45–55% of GPU capex) |
| Anchor tenant | Department store signed before the mall is built | Microsoft ~$17.4–19.4B (~$7B prepaid); Meta $27B on take-or-pay; IREN $9.7B Microsoft; Nscale $45B Anthropic |
| Construction finance | Bank debt drawn against presales | GPU-collateralised term loans (CoreWeave, SOFR+225 on A3 paper, SOFR+550 on the marginal facility) or contract-secured facilities (Nebius, $775M at SOFR+2.50) |
| Scarce input | Prime land | Nvidia allocation; energised megawatts |

A residential developer's presale deposit is a fraction of the unit price; a neocloud's anchor customers now pay half the construction cost up front, before a rack is installed, on contracts running to 2031. When the tenant funds the building, the developer's own capital works twice as hard. Prefunding is still a loan against future rent, not a gift: the $23.5bn advance on a $47bn gigawatt is ~17% of five-year TCV at $12, later collections stay fat, and treating the advance as permanent capital overstates how much of later EBITDA is still cash by about $5bn at the trough ([[Macro & Technology/AI Datacenter Financing Mechanism Design]] T4; [[Research/2026-08-29 - NBIS - Rubin Hour 5GW Funding and Hyperscaler EBIT per GW - synthesis]]).

The commodity critique is true as far as it goes. Anyone can buy GPUs and rack them; hundreds of small operators do, and the sub-scale end of the market (spot aggregators renting H100s by the hour) behaves exactly like the commodity it is. Nothing about racking one cluster scales into delivering forty. The business at contract scale is the continuous, simultaneous coordination of land acquisition, grid applications, substation construction, prefabricated shell capacity, transformer and switchgear queues, GPU allocation timing, ODM production slots, network fabric delivery, commissioning crews, and customer acceptance testing, repeated across sites, geographies and regulatory environments that share none of their bottlenecks. A missed transformer delivery in Pennsylvania does not delay a Finnish hall, but a missed Nvidia allocation window delays both, and a contract written against either slips its revenue start date while the prepayment clock runs. Time-to-energisation is the whole profit and loss statement when capacity is presold on take-or-pay terms, because every quarter between capital deployed and megawatts billing is pure return erosion. Scarcity does to development what yield-sensitivity does to semiconductor supply chains: it converts the qualification into the moat. Capital, the input assumed to gate entry, is the abundant one: $5–10 billion project facilities are available to any operator with creditworthy offtake. The gate is the pair of rationed inputs, which flow toward demonstrated coordinators, a list that at gigawatt scale currently carries about five names, and Nscale's $45bn Anthropic ticket is the reminder that listed oligopoly is not the constraint.

On every traditional axis of the development trade the CSPs win: more capacity built than everyone else combined, lower cost of capital, two decades of site-development experience; supplier disclosures show neoclouds paying an urgency premium of roughly 38–42% gross margin on power and cooling equipment against a ~35% hyperscaler blend, which is what being the less experienced, more desperate buyer costs. They are missing the allocation. That is the whole industry.

### Three-tier split (what is sold, on what tenor)

The twelve-name comparison ([[Research/2026-08-26 - NBIS CRWV IREN vs Neocloud Complex - Competitive Comparison]]) sorts the complex by product and tenor rather than by origin story: full-stack operators (CRWV, NBIS, Lambda, Nscale) sell GPU-hours on five-year take-or-pay and keep the residual; landlords (APLD, CIFR, WULF, CORZ) sell powered shell on twelve-to-twenty-year leases and hand the box to the tenant; IREN is the full-stack operator that owns the power layer beneath its fleet; Crusoe is a developer-builder with project-level debt against Oracle and Microsoft leases; Fluidstack and Together assemble capacity on other parties' shells and credit. Coordination, the moat claim at the essay level, is measurable in energised megawatts and in price kept above input cost, and on both CRWV holds the evidence (1.5 GW active after +~500 MW in Q2; list +25% in July against Nvidia's +17%); NBIS discloses neither active MW nor a named Rubin cash rate, and IREN printed the only in-window hyperscaler-accepted conversion (Horizon 1, 50 MW IT, 13 Aug) then a $4B contracted-ARR claim against a miss. The landlords match the property-developer frame and do not yet earn under it: billing MW CORZ 437 / APLD 175 / WULF 102 / CIFR 0 at 30 Jun (Black Pearl commenced Aug), interest-to-revenue CIFR 269% / WULF 126% / CORZ 14.5% / APLD 4.1%, credit rented from Amazon (CIFR Black Pearl guarantee, notes at 6.125%) and Google (Fluidstack backstops at CIFR and WULF; ~14% of WULF and ~5.4% of CIFR held via warrants). Two of the four landlords are levered to Fluidstack, and CRWV is the tenant at APLD (400 MW) and CORZ (590 MW), so landlord durability sits upstream of full-stack credit except where the lease is IG-direct (CORZ / AMD, CIFR / AWS, APLD's hyperscaler). Hypothesis to test at the November prints: the coordination premium is real and famine-vintage, and the first aging-Hopper re-rent decides whether it is a layer or a spike.

**Pure-play AI cloud: competitive dynamics.** Five operators (CRWV public, NBIS public, Lambda private, Crusoe private, Nscale private) control the bulk of non-hyperscaler GPU capacity. None has differentiated silicon; all run NVIDIA reference architectures with comparable InfiniBand or Spectrum-X networking, liquid cooling, and rack densities (CoreWeave's 130 kW/rack is the public-disclosure ceiling; rack density itself is now gated by power delivery and failure risk, [[Research/2026-08-29 - NVDA NBIS - AI Rack Density Power Cooling Limits - news]]). Differentiation lives in: (a) speed-to-power (Vertiv estimates neoclouds pay 38–42% gross margin vs. 35% hyperscaler blended because urgency premium), (b) NVIDIA allocation priority (CoreWeave's MFN status, Nebius's $2B NVIDIA equity commitment, Nscale's Nvidia/Dell/Nokia/Aker cap table), (c) financing access (only operators with DDTL-grade collateral structures or prepay-grade offtake can scale), and (d) the software layer, where Nebius is the one credible exception (Yandex-inherited managed services, AI Studio, TractoAI; CoreWeave bought Weights & Biases for $1.7B). The customer base is heavily concentrated. CoreWeave's FY25 mix: ~67% Microsoft, with OpenAI + Meta together representing a large share of the $104B backlog; Q2 top-three 36/26/10 is mix-shift inside the same cluster, not diversification (Jane Street $6B is a new vertical, not a new cycle). Nebius's stack: $27B Meta, up to $19.4B Microsoft, plus four >$1B TCVs in Q2 (Reflection, Cohere, a US neolab, a US quant firm). Three customers (Microsoft, Meta, OpenAI) plus Anthropic underwrite the majority of publicly-disclosed neocloud revenue: this is not customer concentration in the conventional sense but rather counterparty consolidation of the entire sub-segment around a handful of hyperscaler/lab capex programmes.

**Public pure-play split.** CRWV is the operationally de-risked but financially fragile operator: 1.5GW active, $104B backlog, 59% adjusted EBITDA coexist with ~$640M of Q2 interest (~24.9% of revenue) and a widened $626M net loss. NBIS is the financially safer but operationally unproven operator: ~$8B cash and customer prepayments fund a build still scaling from roughly 170MW active toward 800MW–1GW connected, with no active-MW figure at Q2. At current prices, conventional EV/FY26 revenue is roughly 14–20x for NBIS versus ~5–7x for CRWV; the ratio prices much of NBIS's survival premium before connected capacity becomes active. The relative trade therefore turns on NBIS active-MW conversion versus CRWV re-rent and credit-spread evidence, not headline backlog. Nscale is the unlisted third: $45B Anthropic Rubin, $51B backlog, September IPO path, Microsoft LOI of 1.35 GW at the same campus walked, Caterpillar 2 GW of G3500 behind-the-meter gas dated 1H 2028 against ~864 gensets. Slip is the base rate; local West Virginia 'January / early 2027' language is builder spin.

**Bitcoin-miner pivot: competitive dynamics.** IREN (5.8 GW owned power, 150,000 GPUs committed including 50,000+ B300s; $9.7B 5-year Microsoft GPU services; $4B contracted 2026 ARR / ~$1B live), APLD (1.41 GW critical IT contracted; ~$36B base-term), CIFR ($11.4B: AWS $5.5B guaranteed by Amazon.com; Fluidstack ~$3B with a Google backstop), WULF (~$27B: Anthropic 20-yr ~$19B; Fluidstack $6.7B), CORZ (>$24B: CoreWeave 12-yr $10.2B; AMD 15-yr >$14B). These operators sell two distinct products: (1) turnkey 'AI superfactory' infrastructure (APLD-CRWV model: landlord economics, the operator never touches the GPU), and (2) end-to-end GPU cloud services (IREN-Microsoft model: same as pure-play neoclouds, with the power layer owned). The landlord variant has higher durability because the asset being sold (power, cooling, fiber, real estate) does not depreciate at GPU-cycle speed. The full-stack variant inherits the same GPU rental-rate risk as CRWV/NBIS, which is why IREN's thesis inverts the 'poor short' read onto the interconnect rather than the fleet. APLD spun its GPU-cloud arm into ChronoScale in May 2026 because hyperscale tenants would not lease from a competitor.

**Pricing power.** Strengthening short-term (rental rates +/+, Nebius reports all capacity sold out with pricing 'continues to strengthen'; IREN three-year pricing +125% since November; one-year H100 contract index up 40% from its low), structurally contested long-term. Three pressure vectors and one offset:
1. **NVIDIA upstream**: NVIDIA's $2B equity stake in CRWV and NBIS each is partly informational rent-extraction: the chip supplier knows operator economics and can price-discriminate on GPU shipments. The ACP ($36B, six-year typical, revenue-share kink at a preset) is a financing credential, not a rate floor; a pause headline is optics, a genuine ACP collapse would reprice the sub-scale tail first (Sharon AI, Firmus) before the Microsoft/Meta books.
2. **Hyperscaler downstream**: Microsoft's $60B+ neocloud commitment is option value, not exclusivity; MSFT continues building Azure capacity in parallel and can shift allocation as in-house ASIC (MAIA) ramps. Meta Compute resale of surplus GPUs is a hyperscaler entering the rental layer against Nvidia-backed clouds.
3. **GPU rental rate compression**: H100/H200 rental rates have already fallen 50–70% in private spot markets per debt-market commentary on DDTL collateral; the cluster-resign mechanic that supports older Hopper economics (per [[Research/2026-04-24 - Dylan Patel on AI Token Supply and Demand - video-transcript]]) is the offset, but it is bounded, and every datapoint in the 55% re-rent record is shortage-vintage.
4. **Offset, the token-factory hurdle**: dollar demand has to grow at ~40% a year for rent per watt to hold; every measured demand print (Google 9.7T→3.2 quadrillion tokens/month in two years; OpenAI 6B→15B tokens/min in five months; Anthropic $9B→$44B run-rate; hyperscaler 2026 capex ~$725B, +77%) is compounding at a multiple of that rate while agentic penetration sits in the low single digits to low tens of per cent. ### The token factory: what a gigawatt makes, and what it costs

A gigawatt of Rubin-class capacity, once its racks are powered, produces roughly ten quadrillion tokens a year in ordinary service and forty at the benchmark limit, about what the whole industry served in a month in mid-2026, and it costs about $6.5 million per megawatt a year to run once the building, the substation, the racks, the networking and the electricity are all charged against it on an accounting basis (~$10.6m economic, 15% on IT over seven years and 10% on the shell over twenty-five). Those two numbers are the supply side of AI compute in full. Token capacity compounds at about 3x a year through 2028 (energised watts +40–50%, fleet-average efficiency ×1.7–2.0); the cost of making them compounds at the rate megawatts are energised, about 40% a year; the gap is an efficiency term. Demand, in dollars, has only to grow at the slower of those two rates for the economics of the compute layer to stay where they are. ([[Website/2026-08-26 - AI Compute - The Token Factory and the Demand Hurdle]]; [[Macro & Technology/AI Supply-Demand Considerations to 2030]])

Blackwell was sold as 30x Hopper and benchmarked at about 4x the throughput for 1.7x the power. Rubin's 'ten times lower cost per token' is a comparison against a year-old GB200 software stack at one interactivity point; against the July 2026 GB300 install the same data shows about 2x per megawatt at ordinary interactivity, 4x at 200 tokens a second per user and 5.4x at the 300-token frontier where GB300 can barely serve at all. The source of the roughly 2x-per-generation gain is the precision ladder, FP16 to FP8 to FP4 to Rubin's 3.125-bit lookup format; below three or four bits accuracy fails, so after Feynman in 2028 the gain has to come from process node, memory bandwidth, SRAM-first decode and co-packaged optics, and we expect the fleet rate to fade toward 1.4–1.7x in 2029–30.

What one megawatt makes depends on how fast each user wants their answer. This 'interactivity tax' is the least understood number in the supply arithmetic. On SemiAnalysis's renormalisation of CoreWeave's Rubin data, a Rubin NVL72 megawatt serves 1.33 million tokens a second at 50 tokens per second per user (batch) and 96,000 at 300 (the fast tier latency-sensitive agents demand): 42 trillion tokens per megawatt-year at one end and 3 trillion at the other, a fourteen-fold spread on identical hardware. In ordinary service, after 60% utilisation and the penalty for frontier-scale models and long agentic context, a megawatt-year yields on the order of 10 trillion tokens. Agentic composition pushes the fleet up that curve, so realised fleet efficiency lags the benchmark generational gain and the fast-tier/batch price spread is also a tokens-per-watt spread.

The cost side is a bill for a watt, built at the rack rather than the accelerator, because the accelerator is a little over half of it. Per Rubin GPU the all-in figure is about $183,000: a rack share of about $118,000 (GPU packages ~$55,000; HBM4 and LPDDR ~$36,000; Vera CPUs, NVLink switch trays, network cards, compute trays, power shelves and in-rack cooling ~$27,000), about $8,000 of scale-out networking and storage beyond the rack, and about $57,000 of shell, substation, switchgear, UPS and cooling plant. At 3.9 kilowatts of all-in utility power per GPU that is $47 per watt, which is the figure Foxconn quotes per gigawatt for a Rubin build ($47B) and, allowing for the 2026–27 memory surge, reconciles to Epoch's $38B for a GB200 build split 56% servers, 13% network and 30% facility. Capex per watt is roughly flat across generations, because shell, power and cooling scale with watts and Nvidia prices each generation to hold system dollars per watt while performance per watt rises (GB300 at $37.4 per watt of marketed IT capex against Vera Rubin at $38.1 despite the jump in power per chip); memory is the one component breaking that into 2027. Electricity is a small line: at 8.76 kilowatt-hours per watt-year and about $90 a megawatt-hour it is 12% of the running cost, so a doubling of delivered power price adds about 10% to the cost of a token, six weeks of efficiency. Power matters as a gate on quantity, and the price signal of that gate is the GPU-hour, not the electricity bill.

The two right-hand columns answer different questions. The accounting basis spreads the principal over the asset's life and is what reported gross margins use; the economic basis charges the principal plus the return the capital has to earn and is what decides whether the next fleet should be built. Per GPU that is $20,300 a year against $36,500, and the $16,000 gap is the profit the machine must earn every year to have been worth buying. A longer asset life moves the first number a great deal (a 5.5-year accelerator life gives $8.0 per watt-year) and the second one barely, because a longer life spreads the principal while the return on it keeps accruing. Charging that bill against the fleet: token-serving cost, accounting, $75B (2026) → $417B (2030); whole-fleet economic $240B → $950B; tokens served ~100 quadrillion → ~4,000; serving cost per million tokens $0.75 → $0.10. The slope of the cost line is the watts line and nothing else.

A $12 contract sits in the sharing zone of that ladder: the operator clears its hurdle with room to spare while an inference-weighted customer cuts its cost per token by up to a third against renting Blackwell. The generational surplus is split rather than extracted, which is the strongest argument that rates near twice the prior generation are an equilibrium price and not a famine artefact: every generation that widens tokens per dollar re-arms the ceiling under which the next contract is written. On pure BF16 arithmetic $12 does exceed the $9.60 parity point, so the paper being signed prices inference throughput and guaranteed availability rather than spot-training parity; in a rationed market the cheap Blackwell alternative is sold out, and the realistic counterfactual to a Rubin contract is unserved demand.

The contract is the other half. The identical fleet run without one, selling by the hour at open-market rates, earns 9–10% and misses the hurdle, because merchant GPU pricing decays every time Nvidia launches a faster generation. A five-year take-or-pay agreement freezes the launch rate through the richest years of the asset's life and hands that repricing risk to the customer; that single clause is worth roughly $160K per GPU, more than the fleet's entire margin over the hurdle. Launch-rate paper is only offered to developers with a completion record, credentialed Nvidia allocation and a bankable balance sheet, which is why identical hardware earns commodity returns in unproven hands and franchise returns in proven ones.

### Funding a 5GW programme, and EBIT per GW

The essay's $12 row on a Foxconn-all-in $47bn/GW Rubin machine, 50% customer prefunding, and a 0→5GW cadence of one gigawatt a year is a two-year equity bridge of ~$33bn at a standing start, not a $117bn lifetime claim on the share count, because two live gigawatts of cash EBITDA (~$19bn/GW-year at $12) more than fund the $23.5bn net cheque for the next building once deferred-revenue amortisation is charged, and the franchise is self-funding from the third building onward. That is [G-7] (ROIIC × a long runway of places to put it) as a funding statement rather than a multiple. Nebius as it actually sits, with ~1GW guided live by YE2026 and ~$8bn of Q2 cash (~$13.7bn pro forma the 24 August converts), needs $0–5bn incremental equity at that $12 row. AWS ~$9.2bn, Azure ~$8.3bn, GCP ~$6.8bn of operating income per *operating IT* gigawatt (Q1 2026 IT load ~6.2 / 5.4–5.5 / 4.7 GW; calendar Q2 / Microsoft FY26 Q4 run-rates) is a real number, and it is the number that makes a mature $10 Rubin GW at $12.3bn EBIT look 'better than a hyperscaler'. Live Nebius at ~$2bn EBIT/GW is below the hyperscalers because it is a small, cheap, wholesale book. The rhyme is peak contracted-GPU EBIT/GW ≈ depreciated hyperscaler OI/GW; the mechanism is depreciation and software mix, not hardware efficiency. The optics say otherwise (~120x trailing sales); the spread between 120x backward and 5x forward is the build itself. A two-to-two-and-a-half-year payback at the contracted band is a 40–50% annual cash yield on total capital; the accounting return prints lower because the machine, unlike a building, consumes itself. Infrastructure with that return profile, secular demand, and a deployment roadmap of more than a gigawatt a year into a 5GW owned power bank is the pairing every capital-allocation framework hunts, and on any standard infrastructure capitalisation that pairing supports an operating multiple of 25–40 times. The re-rating does not require the famine to persist; it requires only that current neocloud economics, take-or-pay contracts at roughly twice the prior generation's cash rate, prove to be the stable equilibrium rather than the spike, and that GPU useful life holds at the seven-to-eight years the resale and re-rental record now supports. The market is paying for a collapse in rent; the hurdle for rent merely to hold is 40% a year of dollar demand.

## Acquisitions and new entrants

**Material M&A (since 2024).** Two transactions have reshaped the operator-side stack:
- **CRWV acquires Weights & Biases ($1.7B, March 2025):** vertically integrates the MLOps layer (experiment tracking, model registry, evaluation tooling) into the GPU compute stack. Strategic logic: capture more of the model-development workflow vs. allowing AWS SageMaker / Azure ML / GCP Vertex to capture the layer above the metal. Outcome unproven: most foundation labs (OpenAI, Anthropic, Meta) use proprietary internal MLOps stacks regardless of where they rent compute. Reads as a defensive cost of staying in the game, not a platform upside.
- **Yandex divestiture / Nebius listing (2024-2025):** not a conventional acquisition, but functionally created a new publicly-traded operator out of a pre-existing Russian AI cluster. Sanctions-driven discount on entry price proved a meaningful tailwind to the relisted entity's compounding, and the software inheritance is why Nebius is the one operator that did not have to buy its stack.

**New entrants and rate of formation.** Capital intensity creates a moat against pure new entry. The binding constraint is not capital ($5–10B project-finance facilities are available to operators with creditworthy customer contracts) but: (a) NVIDIA allocation priority: without an MFN-style relationship, new operators cannot acquire enough GPUs to bid for hyperscaler-scale contracts; and (b) grid interconnect timelines: power capacity reservations now run 24–48 months in tier-1 US/EU markets, making the Bitcoin-miner-pivot category structurally advantaged on the supply side because their grid connections predate the AI demand spike. Nscale is the 2026 test of that filter: unlisted, modular, Nvidia/Dell/Nokia/Aker on the cap table, $45B Anthropic after Microsoft walked a 1.35 GW LOI at the same campus, $51B backlog into a September IPO. A frontier-lab ticket of that size going to a builder that is not listed is a reminder that the public names are not an oligopoly; they are the visible slice of an allocation list Nvidia keeps.

**NVIDIA equity strategy as a category-shaping force.** NVIDIA's $40B+ AI equity portfolio in 2026 (~$30B OpenAI, $2B CRWV, $2B NBIS, $2B Nscale, plus Lambda) is the dominant new-entrant filter. Operators NVIDIA backs are credentialed for hyperscaler-scale contracts; operators it does not back struggle to scale beyond regional niches. This makes NVIDIA's allocation decisions, not customer wins, the binding category-formation event. The ACP ($36B commitments, typically six years, revenue-share above a preset, Sharon AI and Firmus as named first partners) extends the same filter into credit support; a WSJ 'shelved' headline on 28 Aug 2026 moved CRWV/NBIS/IREN on financing-optics, and Nvidia's same-day denial that the model remains in place is the primary, the tape the secondary. Detailed framing in [[Research/2026-04-16 - NVDA - Jensen Huang Moat Persistence Interview - deep-dive]]; tape vs denial in [[Research/2026-08-29 - NBIS CRWV IREN - Neocloud Tape on Nvidia ACP Pause Report - news]] and [[Research/2026-08-29 - NVDA NBIS - Denies Pausing AI Compute Partnership - news]].

**Disruption potential from new entrants is constrained but real on the edges.** Sovereign-AI clouds (Nscale Norway/UK, GCC-funded clouds, potential Indian SOEs) may capture market share in regions where data residency or political sensitivity prevents hyperscaler use. Edge-inference platforms ([[Theses/NET - Cloudflare]] Workers AI, AWS Wavelength) compete for a different workload class (low-latency inference) but do not threaten training and large-batch inference, which is the neocloud bread-and-butter. Meta reselling surplus GPU capacity is a hyperscaler entering the rental layer; CoreWeave fell on the announcement even with a $35B Meta contract through 2032, because the damage is to terminal-value and renewal pricing, not near-term revenue.

### Circular financing: the wrong diagram

The most shared chart of the AI cycle is a circle. Nvidia puts $30 billion into OpenAI. OpenAI carries roughly $1.4 trillion of compute commitments. Oracle and CoreWeave buy Nvidia chips with borrowed money. The arrows close, and the caption writes itself: the vendor is funding its own revenue, the demand is not real. The filings do not match that picture. Vendor credit that has actually been entered runs two orders of magnitude below the $500 billion headline. Most of what has closed is equity, a shareholding, not a purchase order. The next dollar of financing is coming from insurance accounts, private-credit funds, construction banks and bond buyers who do not sit in that loop. Nvidia's $500B is MOUs with six asset managers (Apollo, BlackRock, Blackstone, Brookfield, Goldman, KKR), project-by-project underwriting, zero committed tickets, 10-K: requests received, none entered. The $100B OpenAI LOI stalled and shrank into a $30B equity ticket in a $122B round where Amazon put in $50B and SoftBank $30B; Huang has said the full $100B is 'probably not in the cards'. Equity does not oblige the customer to buy the vendor's product. OpenAI took Nvidia's $30 billion and then committed six gigawatts to AMD, and AMD is paying OpenAI for that commitment in penny warrants. Money is fungible.

Against the annual flow those vendor seats are small. The build is spending roughly $850–900 billion a year. Add Nvidia's equity, the $3.5 billion of site guarantees, Broadcom's $29 billion maximum backstop and Google's lease backstop on a ~$35 billion TPU vehicle, and you can get to the low hundreds of billions of *maximum contingent* exposure. Most of that is equity or a backstop that has not been drawn. Drawn credit is in the single-digit billions. Pimco took ~$18 billion of a single $27.3 billion Meta campus bond. Blackstone anchored CoreWeave's $8.5 billion GPU facility. Datacenter ABS outstanding has grown from $4 billion to $61 billion since 2020. Trace those financings to the end and you usually find a cash payment or a third-party lender with covenants.

A few live arrangements do look more like the late-cycle vendor-finance books people have in mind. AMD's warrant is the cleanest: the vendor is paying its customer synthetic equity for demand. Nvidia's stake in Valor (a fund that buys Nvidia GPUs and leases them to xAI) puts the vendor one layer under its own product. Reported talks around OpenAI's Piketon campus (~$250 billion of lease guarantees and up to $350 billion of chip financing) would, if anything like that closed, make vendor first-loss a fact of the capital structure rather than a footnote. Those belong on a watchlist. They are small against the annual flow. Lucent and Nortel booked equipment sales in 1999–2001 against unsecured loans they themselves made to startup carriers with no revenue; when the carriers died, the receivables were worthless and the revenue had never existed. Almost none of the paper printing today is that pattern. The structures are still hard to read. Terms are negotiated deal by deal, so the clauses that decide who pays in a default are mostly private. None of those contracts has been through a downturn in which customers, chip values and vendor earnings all break together. That is a credit problem. It is not evidence that the revenue was invented. Initial Nvidia capital and residual-value language, where offered, have the effect of reducing the overall financing rate and increasing demand from external funding partners; they also protect Nvidia's share, because alternative chips are not sufficiently capitalised to provide these terms, nor willing to guarantee residual value in the way Nvidia is able to. ### The demand hurdle, and who pays for the watts

The compute layer's revenue is rent per watt multiplied by energised watts, and its cost per watt is flat. If compute expenditure across every payer grows at the rate watts energise, about 40% a year and four-fold by 2030, then rent per watt holds, every watt-owner's margin holds, and revenue and operating profit at each of them grow with the gigawatts they switch on. That is the hurdle, and it is worth stating plainly because it is so much lower than the token figures suggest: dollar demand for compute has to compound at about 40% a year, against tokens compounding at 3x, for the current economics of the industry to be consistent.

Who pays that rent is broader than the 'AI revenue' line the market watches. In 2026 the compute layer receives roughly $300–400B: $110–140B funded by end customers (cloud AI services plus the labs' inference compute cost), $50–100B from the hyperscalers' own franchises (ad ranking, search, recommendations, bundled copilots), $100–120B of training paid from raised equity, vendor finance and increasingly the labs' own gross profit, and $25–40B of open-weight, enterprise self-hosted and sovereign serving at the residual rent. Measured AI revenue of about $165B is well under half of it. Every energised watt has a payer, and against $240B of economic cost the layer as a whole earns about 1.4–1.7x its economic rate; a neocloud on new contracted Rubin paper at $9.60–12 per GPU-hour ($21–26M per megawatt-year) earns about 2x its $10.6M economic cost, with a 15% deployment floor at $17.6M, so today's rents carry a cushion of about 25% before the next fleet stops clearing its hurdle. The four payers are correlated, all of them scaling with token revenue, so the hurdle is met only if end-customer AI revenue runs roughly five-fold to about $850B by 2030; a shortfall of a third takes new fleets below the deployment floor, and the first 25–30% capex cut by an anchor buyer is the trigger.

Every measure of demand we track is compounding at a multiple of that rate. In tokens: Google's surface-wide processing went from 9.7 trillion to 480 trillion to 3.2 quadrillion tokens a month across April 2024, May 2025 and May 2026; OpenAI went from 6 billion to 15 billion tokens a minute in five months; OpenRouter's agentic volumes rose 14x in six months; the aggregate runs 5–9x a year. In dollars: Anthropic's run-rate went from $9B to $44B in about a year at an inference gross margin above 70%, which means the labs' compute spend now scales with their revenue and funds training from gross profit rather than from the next equity round; the hyperscalers' 2026 capital plans total about $725B, up 77%; Nvidia's datacenter revenue grew 85%; AWS carries $496B of backlog. Rationing sits underneath all of these prints (rate limits, quota tiers, waitlists), so they understate demand at current prices rather than overstate it.

The penetration numbers say the curve is early. Agentic coding has reached 15–25% of roughly 35 million developers with about a tenth of those intense; enterprise workflow agents are scaling in 2–5% of business functions; autonomous background fleets are below 2%. An agentic seat consumes 50–100 times the tokens of a chat subscription, and it cannot queue: an agent blocked mid-task is a human waiting and the labour arbitrage breaks, so agentic demand leaves the rationing tier and enters the priority-priced one, concentrating on the frontier model where cross-elasticity to cheap substitutes collapses. The gate on enterprise depth is organisational rather than technical ([[Lens - Automation & AI Readiness]]: the dynamo pattern, firms took decades to reorganise around electric motors), which means adoption compounds for a decade rather than saturating in three years. The 2027 forecast in the supply-demand note is token demand at 3.5–4.5x against supply at 3x: the overhang widens before it closes. Balance of evidence held there: demand at or above the balance line to late-2028 (~50%), extended famine (~25%), financing-led glut (~20–25%). GPU-hour rates and token prices decouple through the efficiency engine (a flat contracted GPU-hour delivers a $/token falling 2–2.5x a year), which is the stable equilibrium the neocloud model requires; the famine premium on top of it is cyclical.

Eighty to ninety per cent of capacity sits on fixed-price take-or-pay, so the surplus arrives slowly and lands with the labs first; for an operator adding a gigawatt a year, though, new signings and renewals are most of the book by 2028, which is exactly where the surplus lands second. That is why the economic profile of the compute layer should improve, not deteriorate, for at least the next few years *if* the hurdle clears, and why the neoclouds can be priced as though rent per watt will collapse into the largest shortage the industry has recorded even while the physical gates (HBM sold out, turbines slotted through 2029–30, PJM closed) have not opened.

### What would prove the factory, and the developer, wrong

- **A token print annualising below 2.5x.** Tokens-per-task compression (Opus 4.5 delivering the same work with 76% fewer output tokens) outrunning penetration would put demand below the supply line even in a strong adoption world.
- **A financing break.** The first 25–30% capex cut by an anchor buyer; hyperscaler capex already crossed above operating cash flow in Q3 2026.
- **Independent Rubin prints at or above 3x per watt at fixed precision.** Supply would leak past the 3x line and the famine would end a year early.
- **Ageing-Hopper re-rents at 35–40% of launch rather than the 55% the record supports.** That is the digestion print, and it arrives before any change in the token numbers.
- **Nvidia lifting system dollars per watt on the Kyber generation.** The flat cost line breaks, and part of the famine leaks upstream to the silicon layer instead of staying with the watt-owner.
- **A named Nebius Rubin cash rate at or below ~$8.22 on five-year-equivalent paper with 2026–27 COD**, or a Q3 active-MW miss that makes the 1GW starting point in the $10 case fail. Nscale $7.3 does not fire this; it is not Nebius paper.
- **Allocation normalising toward the CSPs.** The wedge exists at Nvidia's pleasure. Hyperscaler silicon attacks the same dependency from the demand side on a 2027–2030 timeline.
- **Interest at 20–25% of revenue on the prepay-funded names.** CoreWeave is already there. The prepay-funded developer survives a 25–30% capex cut; the DDTL-funded one may not.

The 2026 AI build is spending roughly $850–900 billion a year on infrastructure against just $150–180 billion of annualised revenue from actual end buyers, a five-to-six-times gap between the capital going in and the money coming out, and a 5–7% snapshot pre-tax return on the $1.0–1.2 trillion invested through end-2026, below its 8–10% cost of capital ([[Website/2026-07-29 - Sustainability of AI Capex]]). None of this means AI demand is false. It means the market is pricing three different variables as if they were one: workload growth, revenue capture, and returns for the asset owners. 'AI capex' is four different economic projects sharing one name: ~30–35% hyperscaler franchise defence (cash, cut last), ~25% merchant enterprise cloud (cash, adoption-exposed), ~25–30% lab-serving capacity (debt, SPVs, prepay, vendor credit: the fragile tranche), ~10–15% labs and sovereigns on story-priced equity or state capital (freezes first). Add it up and 55–60% of the build sits on cash flow from some of the best business franchises ever assembled, which is why a telecom-style systemic collapse is unlikely. The cyclically fragile share is roughly 30–35%, and that is where this sector lives. The token-factory hurdle and the fragile-tranche ledger can both be true: dollar demand can clear 40% while the leveraged names still break.

## Investor heuristics

**What's priced in (consensus view).** Neoclouds are levered AI infrastructure plays with multi-year contracted revenue visibility, validated by hyperscaler counterparties (Microsoft, Meta, AWS) and NVIDIA equity backing, *and simultaneously* a commodity GPU-rental cycle heading into glut, which is why Nebius can be bought for roughly five to six times the operating earnings its contracted book produces in 2028–29 at the essay's $12 row (~4.7× 2028 at the $10 working rate if 1GW lands) while printing ~120x trailing sales. CRWV at $30B+ ARR by end-2027 and NBIS at $7–9B ARR target by end-2026 imply 50–100x revenue CAGRs that the market assigns high probability on the operating line and low probability on the capitalised line. The DDTL 4.0 investment-grade rating is accepted as evidence that GPU-collateralised debt is a legitimate asset class. The miner-pivot operators trade at AI-infrastructure multiples, not commodity-mining multiples, except on print day: IREN dropped ~10–12% on a $137M miss against $4B of contracted ARR the tape did not capitalise. The circular-financing diagram (Nvidia funds OpenAI funds Oracle/CoreWeave funds Nvidia) is the default bear caption. Short interest on the complex sits near 30%. Consensus is internally inconsistent: it prices both the backlog as real and the multiple as if the backlog will not earn.

**Where consensus could be wrong.** The original six angles remain the credit and layer-renter ledger. The essays add four more, which are the variant the Full-weight NBIS position is actually underwriting. Agreement across the bullish set is a trigger to disconfirm, not to raise conviction; the Nscale hour, the CRWV interest line, and the YE2026 energisation binary are the falsifiers we keep in view.

1. **Counterparty concentration is underpriced.** Microsoft + Meta + OpenAI underwrite the majority of publicly-disclosed neocloud backlog. This is structurally analogous to Lucent/Nortel's 1999-2001 telco-capex dependence: the operators are not selling to many small customers but acting as capex-cycle proxies for three hyperscaler capex programmes. If any one of Microsoft / Meta / OpenAI cuts capex 25–30% (which has happened in every prior infrastructure cycle), the affected neocloud's backlog impairs immediately. Disclosure that 67% of CRWV revenue is Microsoft frames this as a CRWV risk; the under-discussed framing is that all five public neoclouds collectively concentrate to the same 3–4 counterparties. Q2 mix-shift at CRWV (top-three 36/26/10, Jane Street $6B) redistributes inside the cluster.

2. **NVIDIA's $40B equity flywheel is being read as Lucent vendor financing; most of the paper is not that pattern.** The pattern (NVIDIA invests, the neocloud uses proceeds to buy NVIDIA GPUs, NVIDIA reports revenue, the neocloud reports backlog, NVIDIA stake appreciates with neocloud market cap) is the diagram. Live drawn vendor credit is in the single-digit billions against an $850–900B annual build; the $500B platform is uncommitted MOUs; the $100B OpenAI LOI became $30B of ordinary equity next to $80B of non-vendor capital. The historical analog ended in 70–90% drawdowns at the financier *when the financier had made the loans*. The residual-value clauses that have not been through a joint downturn remain a credit problem. NVIDIA's 13–28% portfolio concentration in CRWV is still an early-warning marker on *correlation*, not on invented revenue. ([[Website/2026-08-15 - Circular Financing - The Wrong Diagram]])

3. **The DDTL collateral mechanic is broken for the names that use it, and is not the funding model of the names that do not.** GPU rental rates in private spot markets have fallen 50–70% from peaks while DDTL 3.0 / 4.0 covenants reference acquisition cost or contracted rates. The 1.15–1.20x DSCR covenants assume contracted-rate revenue, which holds while the 5–7 year contracts are in their initial period, but the second-cycle re-rent (after 4-year initial contracts roll) economics determine the back-end of the DDTL amortisation. If GPU oversupply emerges by 2027-2028, DDTLs face covenant breach risk before maturity. The 'first investment-grade GPU-backed debt' framing is bull-cycle pattern recognition, not durable credit analysis. The macro-capex leverage ledger ([[Macro & Technology/Sustainability of AI Capex]]) puts the whole neocloud tranche at ~90–100% of drawable leverage capacity, with the marginal builder's interest already at the ~20–25%-of-revenue level (CoreWeave ~25%) that marked the break point in the telecom, railway, electrification and shale booms; the outside-view base rate is that credit-funded capacity overshoots demand, and the correction lands on the leveraged tranche first. NBIS's prepayment funding is covenant-remoter (no DSCR trigger) but is still counted a debt-like claim in that ledger: cleaner within the tranche, not exempt from it. The non-core stakes do not change that arithmetic: Nebius books ClickHouse at ~$1.5B (35–39% of the headline pro-rata at both the Series C and D marks), Avride has no priced round, and the operator raised $5.75B of converts in August rather than sell either ([[Research/2026-08-25 - NBIS - ClickHouse and Avride Private Stakes - synthesis]]). A second bankability axis sits below the GPU rate: the **duration, firmness, and node-level deliverability of the underlying power/colo contract**. Extraordinary short-term compute prices do not prove a financeable long-duration power project; a 90-day-cancellation deal at ~$5,000/MWh underwrites less construction debt than a 20-year take-or-pay at ~$271/MWh.

4. **Miner-pivot durability bifurcates by business model.** APLD, CIFR, WULF and CORZ's landlord model (sell power + space, do not own GPUs) is genuinely more durable than CRWV/NBIS because the asset being sold does not depreciate at GPU-cycle speed. IREN's full-stack model (own and rent GPUs end-to-end via $9.7B Microsoft) carries the same depreciation/rental-rate risk as the pure-plays *on the GPU layer*, and a structurally different one on the 5.8 GW interconnect the vault's own short-construction work rejected as a 'poor short'. The market currently rates them as a homogenous 'Bitcoin-miner-pivot' category; disaggregation creates pair-trade opportunity. IREN's $4B contracted ARR / $20–25m/MW three-year book is the same commercial print as NBIS Q2 landmarks; the $137M miss and $450M mining writedown are the P&L clock, which is the one the tape reads.

5. **Neoclouds are levered NVIDIA derivatives on silicon, and a coordination franchise on time-to-energisation; they are not independent layer monopolies.** The conventional valuation reflex (compare to AWS/Azure/GCP on EV/Revenue or to legacy data center REITs on cap rate) misses that pricing power is upstream-pinned at NVIDIA. If NVIDIA's chip margins compress (custom silicon competition from Broadcom-led hyperscaler ASICs; see [[Sectors/Custom Silicon & Networking Semiconductors]] and [[Theses/AVGO - Broadcom]]), the neocloud rental rates that justify current valuations compress on the same timeline. The cleanest expression of bullish-AI-infrastructure *at the silicon layer* is NVIDIA itself; neoclouds are levered beta on the same factor with idiosyncratic credit and construction risk overlaid. The property-developer claim does not retire this; it relocates the residual to coordination, which is measurable in energised MW and in price kept above input cost, and which has not been shown to survive a glut. VLM: layer-renter on Nvidia allocation and on the grid; the contract wrapper is the closest thing to a temporary value layer (~$160K lifetime EBIT at $12 contracted vs merchant).

6. **Rubin ROIC is a cash-rate, coverage, and contract-vintage problem, not an automatic capex-collapse and not a 1.8×–3.0× corridor.** At a seven-year life, lifetime EBIT per Rubin GPU is **$175K at $9, $282K at $12, $389K at $15, $496K at $18** on $183K invested. $9 fails the $192K gross-book screen and clears 15% IRR. At $12, a five-year take-or-pay GPU earns ~$282K versus ~$122K merchant: the wrapper adds **$160K lifetime EBIT (~$118K NPV @ 8%)**. This is the adversarial bull against the sector's depreciation bear. The bear survives through cash below ~$8.22, a coverage/vintage blend that fails the screen (line ~44% contracted at $12), or 2028–2029 re-rents at 35–40% rather than the modelled 55% blend.

7. **The category error is the multiple.** The market resolves the contradiction (no technology, two-year paybacks) by treating the returns as a temporary spike on a commodity business and refusing to capitalise them. If the conditions that produce the returns are a state (rationed power, rationed silicon, take-or-pay at roughly 2× the prior generation, seven-year life) rather than a cycle, the 5–6× 2028 EBIT multiple is the widest re-rating in large-cap markets, and it does not require the famine to persist. If they are a cycle, developers earn their most spectacular spreads in the two years before a glut, and levered developers do not survive gluts. [[Website/2026-08-22 - Neoclouds - The Property Developers of Compute]]

8. **The demand hurdle is 40% dollars, not 3x tokens, and the market is pricing a glut inside a shortage.** Token capacity compounds at 3x because of an efficiency engine the cost line does not share. Dollar demand at 40% holds rent; every measured print is at a multiple of 40%; agentic penetration is early; scarcity clears posted price last. The neoclouds are priced as if rent per watt will collapse. We think the next few years run the other way *if* the hurdle clears, and we hold Nebius as the highest-weight position on a three-year view as the unlevered developer the customers fund rather than the one the leverage funds. [[Website/2026-08-26 - AI Compute - The Token Factory and the Demand Hurdle]]

9. **$12 is an essay row, $7.3 is not the 2027 book, $10 is the working rate.** Consensus (the 22 August essay, and the tape that treated Nscale–Anthropic as a Rubin clearing price) assumes a going-forward cash rate near $12 is both contracted and sufficient to make a 5GW programme self-funding after two buildings. The only named Vera Rubin offtake back-solves to ~$7.3 and would falsify that if it were the 2027 market. It is six-year late-2027 BTM-gas IPO-anchor paper after Microsoft walked, duration-adjusted closer to the $20–25m/MW 1–3 year band, and not a Nebius contract. Working rate $10: residual equity $0 if Q2 cash remains, $7.8bn if it does not, EV/EBIT ~4.7× 2028 / ~2.6× 2029 on a ~$62bn EV. Do not mark Insight #6 to $7.3. [[Research/2026-08-29 - NBIS - Rubin Hour 5GW Funding and Hyperscaler EBIT per GW - synthesis]]

10. **EBIT per GW is not a hardware-efficiency score.** Rhyming a year-4 $10 Rubin GW at $12.3bn EBIT with AWS's $9.2bn OI/GW is a D&A-and-software-mix comparison, not a reason to reclassify neoclouds as hyperscalers. A new Foxconn-sticker AI GW at a hyperscaler would look like Nebius year 1 ($5.3bn). Industry #13 is the anti-pattern.

**Trade construction implications.** The live book is already constructed: [[Theses/NBIS - Nebius Group]] Full (25%+) is the unlevered developer, prepay-funded, software-inheritance exception, with a hard Q3-2026 active-MW catalyst (≥600 MW for HIGH; >20% miss for LOW; >30% YE26 miss for CLOSE) and a LOW dilution definition that the Mar–Aug 2026 window (~22% on a converted basis) has already evidence-touched pending `$conviction-audit`. Long-bias trades in the rest of the sector, if any, should favour the landlord-model miner-pivots with IG-direct leases (CORZ / AMD, CIFR / AWS, APLD's hyperscaler) and owned power (IREN, draft) over full-stack names whose tenant is a neocloud or Fluidstack. Short-bias trades should target the full-stack operators with the highest customer concentration and most aggressive DDTL leverage (CRWV is the cleanest expression). Pair: short CRWV / long NVDA captures the NVIDIA-derivative spread and isolates idiosyncratic credit risk. Pair: long NBIS / short CRWV isolates the funding-quality spread, and fails if NBIS misses the 4x build or migrates to GPU-collateralised debt. Within the landlords prefer IG-direct over Fluidstack-backstopped shells. The landlords out-contract the full-stack operators (~5x lower revenue density per MW) and do not yet out-earn them. Nscale's IPO, if it prices the $7.3 hour as the going-forward Rubin market, is the listed-book's rate test whether or not we own it.

The strongest case this expansion is wrong is that Nscale–Anthropic is the first honest Rubin print, duration-adjusted or not, and that $7.3 (or even $10.4 on a facility-MW reading) is what 2027–28 MW clear at once Microsoft has walked a 1.35 GW LOI and the IPO needs an anchor; in that world the 5GW self-funding property dies, the 2028–29 5× multiple is competed away by issuance, and the property-developer frame is a famine caption on a commodity that has already found its clearing price. YE2026 energisation is the other door: a >4x build in twelve months has almost no precedent, NBIS disclosed no active-MW figure at Q2, and every $10 residual-equity row assumes 1GW is live and billing. We would rather be wrong on the rate, with cash on the sheet, than wrong on the build.
