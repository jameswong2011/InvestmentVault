There exist no avenue to connect providers of capital to managers of capital in a technology efficient way. The opacity setting on discovery in this industry is set to maximum. Just like how two sided online networks disrupted traditional incumbents in the first internet wave, there uniquely exists an opportunity now to do the same in the investment management industry

	- Number two, regulatory burden with respect to exchange of financial information and betting markets have loosened (online gambling deregulation in the US, crypto legality).

Many in the industry who has played with recent LLMs are aware that these models are about to become fully fledged A+ grade research analysts, with further improvements along the line including MCP (model context protocol, an API hub that allows a model to talk to all external data sources that is API exposed), agent mode (models can pilot computers directly and complete tasks). Section Three: Explaining the core mechanism


Basic Functionality (Features at private launch - MVP / Series A stage)  

- What we are building is effectively a hybrid of r/Wall Street Bets x Substack x Online text based role playing betting arena
- Content and account ranking is to be bifurcated to five research tiers (like DotA or League of Legends) accounts that reach the top rank (Masters Tier) monetises while the average user (Gold Tier) pays little and the lowest rank (Bronze Tier) only has access to poor quality content with all useful content residing at higher tiers behind a paywall. There is a substantial social and monetary incentive to maintain your research tier ranking in order to reduce the cost of engaging with higher quality content, as well as to maintain competitive credibility within your social network - this forms the key objective of the competitive arena / gamification element within Laniakea.
- Accounts have ‘health points’ which is effectively currency associated with each research tier. ANY form of engagement on the platform requires users to spend health points which is ‘staked’ with the engagement they have made. 
- Content itself needs to be imbued with life energy, that is, it costs health points to post content, upvoted content survives while content downvoted is removed. If Initial health allocated + upvotes - downvotes is greater than zero, the content is healthy and is available to be viewed publically and generates ‘interest’. Content with negative health is marked for removal and archiving.
- Accounts that post higher quality content earn health currency while accounts that post low quality content lose their staked content health currency as it gets downvoted. Accounts that lose their entire health pool at each tier effectively dies and needs to respawn at a lower corresponding tier
- Accounts that are in the top quartile of each calibration cycle (by overall health currency) ascend (while those in the bottom quartile that have not died also descend), creating a natural rhythm and flow of accounts between tiers based on the popularity and uniqueness of their content
- Accounts that are inactive or do not make public posts are ‘bare’ and lose health currency passively, accounts may elect to ‘repost’ another accounts content (while staking their own health currency) as an alternative if they do not have high quality original ideas to contribute.
- Accounts may only participate in their own research tier and below, they may ‘view only’ N+1 tier content. 
- Overall the health currency system are in a state of balance with source of health currency being utility token purchases by accounts dropping rank trying to sustain themselves (or simply for accessing paywall content at higher research tiers) and sink of health currency being Masters Research Tier accounts electing to convert their health currency into utility tokens. Utility tokens are ‘real world money’ resources which is tradable into USDC via Stripe and also eventually swappable with Governance tokens / LLC equity eventually (more on tokenomics later - refer to UniSwap).
- There is an overall account level / research tier, and also each sub-topic (e.g. Cybersecurity, Healthcare, Banks, Stat Arb) has their distinct research tiers.

## Example Two

**The capital markets rationale underlying the importance of culture**

- Companies are a function of an existing business which generally requires little innovation or first-principles thinking to maintain or slightly grow its cashflows, and a pipeline of new optionalities that constantly renew across time. These optionalities are where the market presents an opportunity for management teams to design new products to capture new markets that are adjacent to its competitive advantage, reorient the business model to gain a competitive advantage, or simply to wait and return cash to shareholders when there is not an opportune time to deploy capital.
- Traditional investment analysis almost solely focuses on the existing business as is and not the optionality embedded in the organisation's presence in certain economic chokeholds or at the intersection of a technological crossroads.
- The framework to view how these optionalities are assessed is thus:
    - a) companies that exist in an industry with high capacity for change are able to receive a feed of underpriced and overpriced optionalities with high frequency
    - b) those that can recognise when there is a positive NPV opportunity require astute and strategic management with long-term thinking who are experts in their field
    - c) most importantly, they require a ready workforce that is sufficiently adaptable, hardworking and coordinated enough to execute these optionalities in a timely manner
- Companies possessing all three of the above characteristics are generally able to be valued on the basis of their existing business PLUS an optionality capture premium.
- Examples of these types of businesses in the past are abundant in the capital markets (today's vanguard of this theme include companies like SpaceX, Stripe, Cloudflare, etc.) and are generally structurally undervalued because the market often prices new optionalities at zero, and in a majority of instances, at a negative value due to the perception of capital destruction in pursuing new ventures. We exclusively focus on investing in companies that suffer from high degrees of capital markets psychosis with respect to this framework.

**Discussion on the characteristics of organisations with strong management & culture**

- Management team incentives around long-term orientation are often a critical factor for the innovation speed and adaptability threshold and a key reason why founder-controlled companies tend to do better over time.
    - Executives with compensation packages aligned to the short term and those without ascribing their own 'soul' to the organisation seek to maximise short-term stock price through market messaging, a propensity to buy back shares at unattractive prices, engagement in corporate expansionism, and most importantly benchmarking strategic decisions against competitive peers' consensus and hyped market trends instead of on a first-principles basis.
    - This occurs because career risk from standing out outweighs any benefit from first-principles innovation and boosting short-term stock prices is inherent in their incentive structure of positive performance rather than any fundamental gauge of corporate performance. We seek to actively reward and look for companies with high levels of executive and employee stock ownership (and treat executive buys as catalysts) as well as incentive structures tied around concepts like EPS growth, ROIC or product-level volumes for new business lines.
- Management teams' capacity to understand and digest information about what is happening on the ground is also a critical variable; the number of hops a piece of critical customer or operational information takes between management layers before it reaches a key decision maker or CEO is an important factor in assessing adaptability and resiliency to market vicissitudes.
    - A low-level account executive who notices their company's products are failing to adapt to changing market conditions but is not incentivised, or unable, to reach decision makers is a missed opportunity for the organisation to get ahead of competitive headwinds and suffers against an organisation that rewards and incentivises a high flow of strategically sensitive information to management. Most executives at stale organisations only see product-level issues after a few years of poor performance in a Tableau table of declining growth; the best organisations' employees are able to directly message senior leaders to address emerging concerns.
    - Operational staff's capacity to want to report issues and opportunities to middle and eventually senior management is a function of either expected personal reward or the mission alignment score of an organisation. Organisations with a high mission alignment score can do away with strict individual tracking of performance and merit; however those without it, like most mature organisations, need to ensure that it is a cultural imperative to be meritocratic and that subordinates, rather than middle management, are recognised for project-level success.
- Capturing new optionalities requires the workforce to constantly thrive in a state of being on edge; this is not common for most organisations. A firm's capacity to be adaptable and innovative in the context of a changing competitive environment or customer demands and to operate outside of its comfort zone is a function of its capacity to feel rewarded and recognised for its contribution. This is determined more by the 'cultural status quo' at organisations.
    - Companies that have a strong mission (not just fluff around accountability, integrity, customer focus etc.) that is typically related to a unique product or service that it provides for the economy that is in flux or increasing demand provide staff with a sense of achievement when the company itself is doing well and tap into the 'pro-social' aspect of the mind of the employee where work achievements feel like charitable outcomes. These scenarios produce a workforce that is dedicated and has incentives aligned with the organisation such that excessive employee monitoring around productivity and processes around seeking accountability are kept at a minimum and the capacity for employees to explore is maximised.
    - Good organisations have high capacity and demand for rate of learning, and thus are able to adapt to new situations and propose solutions, and thus 'thinking' and 'deciding' can be done closer to where the work gets done and thus organisations become more nimble. But this requires both a growing organisation and also a meritocratic anti-bureaucratic organisation, which typically means younger firms. As firms naturally accumulate 'deadweight' staff over time that simply want to work for status and compete for increasing salary on a negative-sum basis with their colleagues, this is the gravitational attractor base rate for all organisations and it takes exemplary leadership for certain organisations to fight it.
    - Organisations that have growth momentum and are constantly chasing new adjacencies naturally attract problem solvers and higher-calibre employees; they do not need to pay these employees as much as the market rate because these employees feel they are paid in 'learning opportunities' and 'experience'. Thus these firms have a higher 'operating margin threshold' to tackle low-margin opportunities that incumbents find difficult to address.
- Negative indicators are workplaces that reward:
    - sycophantic behaviour where the incentives for the workforce are not to deliver on the project but to make your superior look credible or well respected. This is often due to a 'waterfall' structure where bonuses and promotional opportunities are rewarded down a reporting line rather than assessed on a cross-team and cross-departmental basis
    - limited measurement and attention by higher executives of lower hierarchical incentives and contribution to projects. Limited opportunity for originators of high-performing projects to be recognised and rewarded outside of their direct reporting line
    - excessive meetings where primary transmission of basic information that requires decision making is done through meetings rather than through written forms; meetings should be required to discuss complexity and not as an input/output mechanism
    - fiefdoms and limited internal mobility, subordinates are 'attached or owned' by their hiring managers and compensation is structured around 'share of overall pool' by senior leaders and different teams are benchmarked against each other internally via strictly financial outcomes
- Positive indicators (not overlapping with the above indicators):
    - Organisations with clear documented processes or knowledge wikis that evolve over time provide clarity to employees, increase the rate of onboarding and can tolerate higher employee 'burn and churn'
    - Strong written communication culture that is cross-departmental and cross-hierarchical. The entire organisation has fluid movement of staff and ideas and new projects are often not seeded from existing team structure but rather cross-departmentally. It is often through collaboration between different parts of the organisation that new product opportunities can be forged.

**Product vs. Matrix Orgs**

- There are generally two polar styles for organising people within large companies, one sorting by product for multi-product companies (or function for single-product companies) and another relying on a matrix-style management design. These archetypes are useful in different environments and recognising when and where there is alignment or misalignment has meaningful alpha to gauge execution efficacy.
- Product organisations (e.g. Apple or most Japanese industrials) have a very basic foundational cross-segmental layer of 'corporate functions' that include HR, legal and finance but the rest of the staff, most importantly R&D, sales, and operational executives, support the various products formed. This creates meaningful duplication and cost burdens but it means that agility is maintained for serving the respective end markets.
- Matrix organisations (e.g. early Spotify, current Cloudflare) are typically smaller, function in the <5,000 headcount range, and are generally structurally more adaptable and effective when executed well. In these organisations, employees do not have a fixed reporting hierarchy or role; they sometimes may have multiple managers, or sometimes are directly managed by a senior executive. Matrix-style organisations tend to have lower hierarchy, and are capturing faster-growth markets and tend to have employees structured on a project-by-project basis where employees are assessed at the end of a project and then reassigned to where their interests lie, meaning they are free to explore in the 'menu' of available optionalities.
- Successful 'optionality capturing' fast-growth companies tend to be structurally more similar to a matrix organisation; this is the very essence and embodiment of the 'founder-led' firm with 'start-up' energy. However matrix organisations do not scale beyond a high enough headcount where individual accountability becomes easier to hide and incentive-burden overhead necessitates an ossification of processes and roles into more manageable forms.
- There are three high-signal indicators in applying this framework to assessing capital markets performance:
    - Fast-growth startup pivots to a product-level organisation generally indicate that the period of hyper-innovation is over and that the organisational management overhead has outweighed the relative agility
    - Firms that stick to a matrix structure beyond its useful life may experience an implosion and decline in productivity and become outmanoeuvred by a more mature rival whose management designs a new first-principles organisational structure and processes and pivots to a traditional organisation structure.
    - Decaying firms where a prodigal founder or high-calibre management team returns to rejig the org structure often find themselves moving towards the matrix-structure pole (although still largely a traditional product or functional org). The degree to which this is calibrated (e.g. key blocking stakeholders removed, high-performing subordinates promoted, processes re-examined) determines the relative success of the turnaround. This is the standard private equity turnaround playbook.

**Closing thought**

- There is generally a natural momentum inherent in firms that do execute well and are in favourable markets, and the capital markets often do not ascribe the correct valuation to these opportunities. Firms that lose this momentum may survive and still thrive for a long time on the basis of existing market growth; however they are generally fairly valued and do not deliver outsized returns. On the other hand, stale organisations that suddenly face new competitive disruption are most often melting ice cubes, and the market may still price them at reasonable multiples right before the vultures are feeding on their corpse.
