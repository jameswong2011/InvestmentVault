## Summary

SemiVision, published via TSPA Semiconductor on 17 August 2026 (Gmail `1a00edec83b24d55`; ~5-minute free article), argues that Taiwan’s AI boom has crossed from a semiconductor story into a fiscal one. Lai’s Monday announcement is the political surface: NT$10,000 per eligible citizen next year, framed as sharing an “AI dividend” that would otherwise stay with semiconductor companies, exporters, and shareholders, while the administration claims a balanced budget and effectively zero new borrowing. The author’s economic question is what Taipei does with the rest of the windfall.

The growth arithmetic is the substrate. Government estimates put Q1 2026 GDP at 15.43% (revised from 14.55%), Q2 at 12.93%, first-half at 14.15%, and full-year at 11.05% versus an earlier 9.64% — strongest annual performance in 39 years if delivered. The engine named is the island as a principal manufacturing hub of the global AI economy: TSMC producing many of the world’s most advanced AI processors; Taiwanese firms making AI servers, networking equipment, substrates, printed circuit boards, cooling systems, and power electronics; a large portion of modern AI-datacentre infrastructure either passing through Taiwan or depending on Taiwanese suppliers. That mix has become higher exports, stronger corporate investment, and higher tax revenue. For fiscal year 2027 (year 116 on Taiwan’s calendar), projected central-government revenue is raised to NT$3.9266 trillion. Expenditure is therefore planned up NT$235.7 billion, including the cash payment.

The distribution problem is why the cheque exists. Workers, investors, and firms on the semiconductor/server/tech supply chain have captured the cycle; much of the workforce remains in services, traditional industries, and SMEs where productivity and wage growth are slower. Double-digit GDP can coexist with only modest household disposable-income gains, opening a gap between headline performance and household perception. NT$10,000 will not transform a middle-class budget; collectively it can support consumption among lower-income households with a higher propensity to spend, and it makes the boom visible outside technology clusters.

The more consequential expansion is structural. Five buckets: (1) demographics — about NT$373 billion of family-support (childcare, parental leave, education assistance); subsidies will not reverse East Asia’s fertility collapse, but demographic policy is now part of long-term economic strategy; (2) defence — total spending, regular plus special budgets, expected to exceed NT$1.12 trillion and remain above 3% of GDP, converting economic strength into defence capacity; (3) technology — NT$229.2 billion, up 12.7%, including NT$40.6 billion for the “AI Ten Major Infrastructure Projects” (computing infrastructure, scientific research, talent, critical technologies), aimed at absorbing AI productivity at home rather than only manufacturing hardware for foreign firms; (4) public infrastructure — NT$728.8 billion (transport, water, wastewater, regional development, resilience); (5) central transfers to local governments — NT$1.36 trillion. Taken together, Taipei is trying to convert a cyclical export boom into longer-term capacity.

The author’s test of durability is explicit. Taiwan occupies one of the most valuable positions in the global economy because AI investment needs advanced semiconductors, packaging, servers, networking, and power infrastructure — the exact stack Taiwan accumulated over decades. AI capex will not necessarily expand at today’s pace indefinitely. If the boom produces only higher corporate profits, asset prices, and temporary tax revenue, the economic legacy is narrower than the GDP print. If it is converted into infrastructure, technology, energy resilience, defence, demographic support, and SME productivity, the consequences last. That makes NT$10,000 symbolically significant and economically secondary. The real test is whether extraordinary profits from the current AI investment cycle finance the foundations of Taiwan’s next growth cycle — repeating, at the country level, what the semiconductor industry did when it accumulated capabilities before the rest of the world priced their strategic value.

## Source Excerpts

> “President Lai Ching-te said on Monday that Taiwan’s stronger-than-expected economic growth has given the government enough fiscal room to distribute NT$10,000 in cash to every eligible citizen next year, while still maintaining what the administration describes as a balanced budget and effectively zero new borrowing.”

> “According to the government’s latest estimates, first-quarter GDP growth in 2026 was revised upward from 14.55% to 15.43%, while second-quarter growth was estimated at 12.93%. Growth for the first half reached 14.15%. The government now expects full-year GDP growth of 11.05%, up from an earlier forecast of 9.64%. If achieved, it would represent Taiwan’s strongest annual growth performance in 39 years.”

> “TSMC produces many of the world’s most advanced AI processors. Taiwanese companies manufacture AI servers, networking equipment, substrates, printed circuit boards, cooling systems and power electronics.”

> “For fiscal year 2027 — officially year 116 under Taiwan’s calendar — projected central-government revenue has been raised to NT$3.9266 trillion. The government therefore plans to increase expenditure by NT$235.7 billion, including funding for a NT$10,000 cash payment per person.”

> “Total defence spending, including regular and special budgets, is expected to exceed NT$1.12 trillion and remain above 3% of GDP.”

> “Government technology programmes will receive NT$229.2 billion, an increase of 12.7%, including NT$40.6 billion for the so-called ‘AI Ten Major Infrastructure Projects.’”

> “That makes the NT$10,000 payment symbolically significant but economically secondary. The real test of Taiwan’s AI dividend is not whether households receive a cheque. It is whether the extraordinary profits generated by the current AI investment cycle can finance the foundations of Taiwan’s next growth cycle.”
