# NVDA Superposition — How Do You Underwrite an AI Factory

## Thesis Delta
Consensus prices the Aug 10 [[Theses/NVDA - Nvidia]] sponsor MOUs as a funded >$500B GPU-collateral facility (and Huang’s “up to 25%” language as a ~$125B residual-value put on that whole pool) and prices [[Theses/AVGO - Broadcom]] AI XPV as a clean Anthropic take-or-pay offtake → this source implies both are hybrid credit designs, not completed financings: the $500B is an uncommitted market-building proposal (six MOUs; independent project-level underwriting; existing $3.5B site-layer lease guarantees are a separate 10-K instrument), while the $35B Apollo/Blackstone XPV is a five-year customer-lease backstop with $29B dynamic maximum vendor exposure and post-default rack-sale remedies. The priced variable is the loss waterfall when customer PD and rack LGD rise together — not FLOPS converting into lower WACC. [G-4] [G-13] [VLM]

## Summary
An AI factory is not financed on chip collateral alone. Superposition Part 2 (fact cutoff Aug 11, 2026) argues that lenders ask two sequential questions: in the performing state, do customer contracts and market revenue cover debt service after power, opex, maintenance, and required reinvestment (CFADS / DSCR); after default, what is *net* recovery after downtime, dismantling, transport, porting, requalification, legal enforcement, and price discounts, and which party absorbs the first loss. Two 2026 announcements look like opposite answers — NVIDIA’s Aug 10 plan with Apollo, BlackRock, Blackstone, Brookfield, Goldman Sachs, and KKR to establish independent compute-financing platforms, foregrounding redeployability, CUDA life extension, and residual value; Broadcom/Apollo/Blackstone’s June 9 AI XPV Platform, foregrounding Anthropic multiyear capacity and contracted cash flows — but the filings show neither is a pure type. NVIDIA already guarantees certain partner facility-lease obligations; Broadcom is backstopping five years of customer lease obligations with maximum exposure of $29B. The central claim: factory finance is not a choice between general-purpose chips and long-term contracts. It is the task of combining normal-state cash flow, default-state net recovery, and the loss waterfall among customers, vendors, sponsors, equity, and lenders into one structure. Scope is explicit: the essay does not underwrite hyperscaler corporate bonds; it covers SPVs, leasing, project finance, and object finance around specific compute assets and customer contracts. Basel specialized-lending language (project finance on project revenues vs object finance on a physical asset’s cash flows) is the analog; live AI-factory paper mixes site, equipment, contracts, corporate credit, and vendor support.

The $500B figure is a proposal to build a market, not a closed fund. The agreements are memoranda of understanding whose stated objective is to mobilize more than $500B of third-party capital *over time* across multiple dedicated pools. Three formulations are false on the public record: NVIDIA has not raised $500B; a single $500B vehicle has not launched; NVIDIA has not guaranteed the entire amount. Confirmed facts are narrower: six institutions intend to underwrite individual projects independently (customer, demand, utilization, cash flow, residual value); Huang’s formulation is that NVIDIA supplies the compute platform and ecosystem while capital providers make the financing decisions. That is not a withdrawal from vendor-level risk. FY2026 Form 10-K states NVIDIA had received requests to finance customers’ and partners’ data-center development but had not then entered such financing arrangements — and, separately, discloses guarantees of certain partners’ facility-lease obligations: $3.5B maximum aggregate exposure, five-to-seven-year terms, $712M partner escrow, with the right on partner default to take over the lease, occupy the facility, or sublease. That guaranteed layer is land, power, and building shell — the site layer, not the compute equipment. The August platform is an attempt to institutionalize project-level underwriting at larger, more repeatable scale while preserving the option of limited vendor credit support. There is no public evidence that the $3.5B site guarantees sit inside the August platform, and they are not the same instrument as the residual-value support NVIDIA “may” offer for selected future opportunities. Headline capital is not a WACC cut: actual commitments, capital raised, capital deployed, spreads, LTVs, maturities, and financing costs are undisclosed.

Neither contracted cash flow nor redeployability is a repayment source until the factory completes and enters commercial operation. NVIDIA’s quarterly filing for the period ending April 26, 2026 flags access to data-center capacity, energy, and capital as critical to customers’ and partners’ ability to deploy; energy expansion is a multiyear regulatory/technical/construction process; shortages of capacity or power can delay or reduce deployments. Broadcom warns that customers may underestimate data-center, power, and water requirements and that a bottleneck in one component can stall the whole project. That is why “GPU collateral” is the wrong unit. Superposition splits the asset into four layers: (1) site-fixed — land, shell, grid interconnection, substations, cooling — long physical life, one location, one regional power market; (2) relatively movable equipment — accelerators, servers, racks, some networking — relocatable only if the destination has power, cooling, networking, and physical compatibility, then reintegration and requalification; (3) intangible operating layer — software licenses, model support, monitoring, security certifications, maintenance, operating organization — legal title to hardware can transfer without the rights needed to run it at the same level; (4) contract and credit-support layer — leases, capacity-purchase agreements, SLAs, escrow, guarantees, vendor backstops, residual-value support — the layer that converts a physical asset into performing-state cash flow and post-default recovery. Technical versatility becomes a financeable recovery path only when those layers connect. NVIDIA publicly foregrounds fungibility: one factory runs many models and workloads, can be reused across customers/clouds/operators, and receives CUDA updates that improve already-installed infrastructure; the developer/customer ecosystem is claimed to deepen the replacement-user pool. That is a company claim, not independent evidence that every NVIDIA factory can be redeployed at a high price after default. Three nested concepts must stay separate: *technical versatility* (one system can run many models), *operational redeployability* (move without excessive downtime, reconfiguration, requalification, or capex), and *bankable redeployability* (return to a billable state within a bounded time and cost, with cash flow or sale proceeds material to the lender’s recovery). A rack can run many models and still have no replacement buyer; a buyer can exist and still be blocked by data sovereignty, security, networking, or software-license restrictions; industry-wide overbuild can send many assets after the same replacement customers and crush price. Huang’s “up to 25% of an opportunity” residual-value support is limited, opportunity-by-opportunity, intended to supplement not replace independent underwriting. It is not a guarantee of 25% of $500B. Denominator (equipment cost vs project cost vs loan principal), legal form (guarantee, put, repurchase, exchange), valuation date, triggers, duration, and creditor priority are all undisclosed.

Broadcom’s case is more specific and more hybrid than the press read. June 9, 2026: AI XPV Platform with Apollo and Blackstone Credit & Insurance as initial core capital partners, intended to support more than 20 GW of potential AI compute by 2028 on Broadcom custom XPUs and networking. Initial $35B capital solution led by Apollo alongside Blackstone and global banks, connected to more than 1 GW of Anthropic training/inference infrastructure with deployment beginning mid-2026 at Fluidstack-operated sites; Apollo described committed capital on a multiyear draw schedule and named contracted cash flows as a defining feature of the asset class. The May 3, 2026 Form 10-Q subsequent-events section discloses the credit design: on June 8, 2026, Apollo assumed purchase agreements for AI racks based on Broadcom-designed custom accelerators together with the related customer lease agreements; Broadcom will backstop the customer’s lease obligations for five years. Exposure increases as racks are deployed and declines as the customer makes lease payments; maximum exposure $29B; on customer default Broadcom may assume the leases or seek to sell the racks. The official announcement links the initial $35B to Anthropic; the 10-Q says “customer” and does not name Anthropic — reading the two as the same architecture is reasonable, asserting that the SEC filing names Anthropic is not. $29B is not an unconditional guarantee of the $35B transaction. The structure is five years of customer lease obligations → a Broadcom backstop of up to $29B → post-default option to assume leases or sell racks. NVIDIA does not ignore contracted cash flow (Huang lists customer, demand, utilization, cash flow, *and* residual value); Broadcom does not ignore the physical asset (lease assumption and rack sale sit in the filing). The difference is which logic is foregrounded and where vendor credit enters the stack and the loss waterfall. Apollo and Blackstone appear in both platforms; capital providers can finance NVIDIA, Broadcom, and other silicon simultaneously. Between the two ideal types sits a credit-support layer — guarantees, backstops, escrow, lease assumption, residual-value support — that allocates the interval between contract failure and asset recovery. That layer is not a third underwriting type; it is what turns ideal types into live paper.

Open weights cut both ways (Part 1: [[Research/2026-08-11 - NVDA Superposition Open Weights Execution Share - deep-dive]]). Demand-side fungibility: more customers and workloads can use the same factory, including regulated/sovereign buyers who cannot use a closed API; a model can remain operable if its original provider changes strategy. Silicon portability: accessible weights plus a permissive license let third parties build porting/optimization/validation paths onto AMD GPUs, TPUs, Trainium, or customer XPUs. Open weights alone are not sufficient — production migration still requires compiler/kernel support, memory and networking optimization, numerical and quality validation, throughput/latency testing, security approval, and operational reliability. The lender’s variable is qualification time and cost to an alternative backend at the same quality, functionality, scale, and SLA. As that time and cost fall, customers gain leverage at renewal, expansion, and new-deployment decisions — pressure on renewal rates and architecture-specific residual values. A long-term contract can absorb near-term portability; after expiry, a lender may assign high value to committed-term cash flow and a lower value to renewal probability and post-maturity collateral. The opposite is also live: a custom XPU with a large enough cost-per-task advantage can support a stronger long-term contract that compensates for thinner general-purpose redeployability — until that customer fails. Governing sentence: open weights can increase persistence of demand for a *model* while reducing persistence of demand attached to a *silicon architecture*. Technology does not become WACC directly. Three translation paths sit in between: (1) performance and power efficiency → cost per task and sellable throughput → customer margins and utilization → CFADS → spread and leverage; (2) power, cooling, networking, reliability → completion and performance acceptance → contractual performance obligations → utilization and cash-flow volatility → maturity, reserves, pricing; (3) software support and silicon portability → supported workload range and switching cost → renewal probability and replacement-demand depth → remarketing time and price discount → net recovery, LTV, maturity. A spread prices more than expected loss; LTV is recoverable value after enforcement, relocation, downtime, and stress costs, not a percentage of purchase price; maturity should align with the customer contract, economic competitiveness, vendor-support period, and redeployability, not physical life; WACC is the blend of debt cost, equity cost, and capital structure. AI chip competition is not moving from FLOPS to WACC. FLOPS, power efficiency, software, networking, and operational reliability are translated into cost per task, utilization, cash flow, and net recovery; those outcomes shape spreads, LTV, maturity, residual value, and then WACC. Finance does not replace technical competition; technical advantage shows up in financing terms only when it produces defensible performing-state cash flow and recoverable default-state value.

Five failure modes, and a four-criterion screen. (1) Completion and performance-acceptance failure — grid, permits, cooling, rack and network delivery, integration, commissioning; if customer payment starts only at commercial operation, pre-completion risk stays with sponsors, vendors, contractors, and capital providers. (2) Oversupply — versatility of one machine ≠ resilience of the portfolio; customers that look independent can share one AI capex cycle. (3) Technological obsolescence — accounting life, physical life, and economic life diverge; software updates cannot remove memory, precision, or networking walls; high current utilization is not stressed residual value at maturity. (4) Correlated exposure — if the customer’s business and the collateral’s value both depend on AI demand, PD and LGD rise together; Broadcom’s five largest end customers were ~45% of net revenue in H1 and Q2 FY2026 (concentration of the vendor’s book, not a PD for XPV/Anthropic). A lender can hold several nominally separate projects and still be concentrated in one silicon generation, one frontier-model demand pool, one power market, and one investment cycle. (5) Silicon portability — exit option for the customer, potential replacement demand for the lender, renewal/residual-value risk for the incumbent silicon; low portability binds the current customer and starves replacement demand if that customer fails. What does *not* matter: the $500B headline, theoretical multi-model compatibility, current GPU rental prices, the nominal vendor-support amount, and the slogan “NVIDIA is general-purpose / Broadcom is custom.” What does: signed transactions and project-level terms; the share of capacity that can be requalified after a customer change, and the time/cost to make it billable; re-contract time and *net* recovery when attrition and oversupply arrive together; trigger, duration, valuation method, priority, enforceability, and the support provider’s ability to pay under stress; which layer fixes performing-state cash flow and who holds first loss and last loss. Four criteria come first: completion and who bears delay; first performing-state repayment source and DSCR reliability; bankable redeployability and net recovery; duration alignment and the loss waterfall. For the August initiative to succeed, NVIDIA compute must become an asset capital providers can underwrite repeatedly — site/power, hardware generation, networking, software, operator, contracts, and vendor support measured separately, not one “GPU” residual. The self-reinforcing finance loop is conditional: if demand persists, oversupply stays limited, and economic life exceeds financing maturity, cheaper capital can accelerate deployment and deepen the replacement-customer pool; if capital expands faster than real demand, the same loop amplifies oversupply and “highly redeployable” boom-time assets chase the same replacement buyers. Unresolved question: when the same AI demand shock weakens the customer’s ability to pay *and* the rack’s remarketing value, who among customer, vendor, sponsor, equity, and lender absorbs the first loss — and who absorbs the last. AI factories become an infrastructure asset class only if that waterfall, plus completion conditions, collateral rights, performance measurement, secondary-market pricing, and vendor support, can be turned into repeatable standards. ## Framework / Mental Model
Superposition names two *analytical ideal types*, not legal categories. Actual paper mixes project finance, equipment finance, leasing, corporate credit, vendor guarantees, and equity cushions.

| Ideal type | Performing-state assumption | Default-state assumption | What still must be underwritten |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fungibility / residual-value | Factory can be reassigned to other customers/workloads and restore market revenue | Re-lease or sale of equipment reduces loss severity | Paying customers *now*; CFADS covers debt before anyone leaves |
| Offtake / contracted-cash-flow | Current customer stays obligated under a long-term contract or lease | Debt service continues without an immediate replacement buyer | Collateral value *after* customer default; termination, take-or-pay, and performance-acceptance terms |

**Four-layer asset map** (why “GPU collateral” fails):

| Layer | Contents | Mobility / life | Credit implication |
|---|---|---|---|
| Site-fixed | Land, shell, grid interconnection, substations, cooling | Long physical life; one location, one power market | NVIDIA’s disclosed $3.5B guarantees sit here |
| Movable equipment | Accelerators, servers, racks, some networking | Relocatable only with destination power/cooling/networking + requalification | Residual-value support, if any, sits here |
| Intangible operating | Licenses, model support, monitoring, security certs, maintenance, operator | Title can move without the rights to run the box | CUDA / software updates are claimed life-extenders; they can also travel poorly |
| Contract / credit-support | Leases, CPAs, SLAs, escrow, guarantees, backstops, RV support | Legal, not physical | Converts layers 1–3 into CFADS and into a loss waterfall |

**Three nested redeployability tests** (NVIDIA’s public claim occupies only the first):

| Concept | Test |
|---|---|
| Technical versatility | One system can run many models and workloads |
| Operational redeployability | Customer/workload change without excessive downtime, reconfiguration, requalification, or extra capex |
| Bankable redeployability | Return to a *billable* state inside a bounded time and cost; resulting cash or sale proceeds material to recovery |

**Performing vs default, then expected loss.** Performing: CFADS = contract/lease/merchant revenue − power − opex − maintenance − taxes − required reinvestment; DSCR = CFADS / scheduled principal and interest. Default: do not add recovery legs at headline value — sequence enforceable vendor/sponsor support, assignable contract value, re-lease/redeploy/sale revenue, then subtract dismantling/transport/porting/requalification, downtime, legal enforcement, and the separate value of fixed-site assets. Heuristic EL = PD × LGD × EAD. Distinctive AI-factory problem: the three are not independent. An AI-demand downturn can raise customer PD, cut rack remarketing values (raise LGD), and leave EAD high immediately after a rapid deployment. Broadcom’s exposure path — rises with rack deployment, falls with lease payments — makes that time dimension visible.

**Credit-support layer** (not a third ideal type): guarantees, backstops, escrow, lease assumption, residual-value support allocate the interval between contract failure and asset recovery.

**Three translation paths (technology → credit terms):**

| Path | Chain |
|---|---|
| Unit economics | Performance and power efficiency → cost per task and sellable throughput → customer margins and utilization → CFADS → spread and leverage |
| Completion / ops | Power, cooling, networking, reliability → completion and performance acceptance → contractual performance obligations → utilization and CFADS volatility → maturity, reserves, pricing |
| Software / portability | Software support and silicon portability → workload range and switching cost → renewal probability and replacement-demand depth → remarketing time and discount → net recovery, LTV, maturity |

**Five failure modes:** (1) completion / performance-acceptance; (2) oversupply (machine versatility ≠ portfolio resilience); (3) technological obsolescence (accounting ≠ physical ≠ economic life); (4) correlated PD/LGD; (5) silicon portability (exit option / replacement demand / incumbent residual risk).

**Four-criterion screen** (what to underwrite first): completion and who bears delay; first performing-state repayment source and DSCR reliability; bankable redeployability and net recovery; duration alignment and the loss waterfall.

**Open-weights overlay:** demand-side fungibility deepens the replacement-customer pool; silicon portability shortens qualification time onto alternative backends. The swing variable is time and total cost to qualify an alternative backend at production quality, scale, and SLA.

Outside view, run against the above: specialized-lending books on short-cycle equipment with correlated offtake and residual rarely get cheaper as the installed base scales into a capex boom — they get larger, then tighter. Agreement across CUDA-moat, contracted-XPU, and “finance unlocks deployment” lenses is the cue to hunt the single falsifier: a downturn print where customer PD and rack LGD move together and the loss waterfall is disclosed.
