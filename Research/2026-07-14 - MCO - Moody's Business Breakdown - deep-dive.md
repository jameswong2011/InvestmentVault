## Summary

Moody's is the story of a published opinion becoming load-bearing financial infrastructure. John Moody released *Moody's Manual of Industrial and Miscellaneous Securities* in 1900; after selling out in the 1907 panic, he returned in 1909 with *Analysis of Railroad Investments*, which introduced the standardized letter-grade credit scale that founded the modern rating industry. Fitch, Standard Statistics, and Poor's followed within two decades — Standard Statistics and Poor's later merged into S&P Global, forming today's "Big Three" (Moody's, S&P, Fitch). Dun & Bradstreet acquired Moody's in 1962; the 2000 spin-off and NYSE listing created the independent public company. The mechanism that matters: as capital markets deepened and financial regulation formalized, ratings stopped being advisory and became embedded — issuers need a rating to borrow at scale, investor mandates reference rating floors, and regulators lean on ratings to gauge systemic stability. That embeddedness converts an opinion into a toll on debt issuance, and the toll is collected regardless of whether a given rating proves right.

Today Moody's is a two-segment integrated risk-assessment firm of ~16,000 employees across 40+ countries. Moody's Investors Service (MIS) runs the issuer-pays model: the borrower pays Moody's to rate the security that investors rely on, then pays recurring surveillance fees as the rating is monitored over its life — a near-zero-marginal-cost annuity mixing upfront and recurring revenue. Moody's Analytics (MA), launched nearly two decades ago, monetizes the same data and research through subscription software for risk, portfolio, insurance-model, and compliance (KYC) workflows sold to banks, insurers, corporates, asset managers, and governments. On top sits the capital-allocation engine CEO Robert Fauber calls "the compounding machine": roughly 80% of free cash flow returned through consistent buybacks and a fast-rising dividend, the remainder reinvested in organic growth and targeted M&A. The result is a business that compounds EPS through both a growing numerator (embedded pricing power) and a shrinking denominator (share count).

Two structural reads follow from the numbers. First, the margin split is the whole thesis in one line: a >60%-margin ratings annuity ("gold standard," gated by NRSRO status and a century of methodology trust) subsidizes and cross-sells a >30%-margin software business — the blended 42% EBIT margin understates the economics of the core layer. Second, capital return, not organic growth, has been the dominant return driver: a 44% share-count reduction plus a 17% dividend CAGR compounds owner returns well above the 11% revenue CAGR. Sustaining 11%/10% revenue/EBIT CAGR for 24 years is a rare, justified outlier to the ROIC/growth fade base rate — the source presents no forward numbers, no bear case, and no discussion of what could break the streak.
