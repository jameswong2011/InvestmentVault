# [Seeing Infrastructure] Part 5. Seeing Stack Map: Who Builds the Machine’s Eyes?

## Thesis Delta

Consensus treats infrared-detector industrial power as a country ranking of who has the “best” HgCdTe / T2SL / InSb device, or as a domestic-content percentage → Superposition’s public-evidence map implies the investable object is a four-axis stack (coverage, program linkage, industrial control, economic capture) and that the layer a state must protect is often not the layer that captures profit. No vault thesis owns Teledyne, Lynred, AIM, Leonardo, i3system, or Fujitsu IR; the live question this raises for existing book names is whether [[Theses/TSM - Taiwan Semiconductor]]’s advanced-CMOS foundry seat (Europe’s named SPIRIT gap) and [[Theses/NVDA - Nvidia]]’s physical-AI “eyes” narrative are being asked to do work that actually sits in classified FPM / ROIC / cryocooler / calibration-software layers the vault does not model. Hypothesis to test: [G-13] the mispriced operating lever is requalification time and design-authority location, not detector-spec leadership; Semis #1/#2 (bottleneck vs qualification-gate) and VLM §1 Interface/standard control fire on ROIC foundry + calibration/data rights, not on the pixel.

## Summary

Superposition_V’s Part 5 is a public-evidence industrial map of four Seeing Stack ecosystems — United States, Japan, Korea, and a multi-jurisdiction “Europe” (France, Germany, Italy, UK, EU programs) — dated as of 11 August 2026. The piece is explicit about what it is not: it is not a ranking of the best infrared detector, not a claim that public documentation equals absolute capability, and not a statement that mission-critical supply equals corporate excess return. The units of comparison are deliberately asymmetric. The US, Japan, and Korea are national ecosystems; Europe is an aggregation that mixes real industrial depth with the summing of several legal and export-control regimes. Leonardo’s detectors are made in Southampton under British jurisdiction and controlled by an Italian parent; SPIRIT is a European Defence Fund project of fifteen organizations across nine countries that has been announced but has not started.

The argument’s mechanism is that a Seeing Stack is a nested assembly plus a separate control layer, not a linear bill of materials. Sensing material and detector device (HgCdTe, InSb, InGaAs, T2SL, microbolometer) sit under a ROIC that integrates, amplifies, and multiplexes; hybridization produces a hybrid FPA; dewar, vacuum, and cryocooler produce an FPM or IDCA; optics, drive electronics, and calibration produce a camera core; stabilization, tracking, fusion, and comms produce a payload and mission system. Above that physical hierarchy sit design authority, process recipes, ROIC-foundry access, qualification and acceptance data, calibration software, repair and upgrade rights, and export licensing. Domestic assembly with foreign rights is not control. Maturity states are also split: development test, environmental/performance qualification, customer acceptance, production approval, fielding, and multi-decade sustainment are different evidence classes. The recurring error the author wants to kill is treating a prototype as repeat production, repeat production as fielding, and fielding as long-term industrial control.

The US is scored as the clearest public program-to-production link. SDA Tracking Layer Tranche 3 is 72 spacecraft at about $3.5B (December 2025, four teams × 18) plus a separate AMDT3 award of 36 spacecraft at about $1.75B (July 2026) — 108 related spacecraft that are not one homogeneous contract. What matters below the award is Teledyne: 100th large-format FPM delivered across Tranches 0/1/2 (March 2025) and infrared-FPM production started for multiple TRKT3 awards (February 2026). RTX’s next-gen F-35 EODAS description links T2SL material to FPA, digital-pixel / digital ROIC, six IR cameras, and a helmet display — vendor architecture evidence, not an independent operational or economic validation. The US sequence in public documents is government demand → repeat FPM production → payload/mission integration. Yields, unit prices, program margins, exclusivity, classified lower-tier chains, and device-vs-ROIC value split remain undisclosed. Program-to-production linkage is clear; lower-tier excess return is not.

Japan is not a prototype-only ecosystem. It has an established IR production heritage and fielded EO/IR integration (Mitsubishi Electric night vision on the US-2; 360-degree surveillance on the Mogami-class frigate). The *new* MWIR/LWIR dual-band T2SL class is the prototype-backed object: Fujitsu delivered a >1-million-pixel dual-band T2SL sensor under an ATLA research-prototype program (March 2026) and said it would use the process in new products from fiscal 2026. That is capability plus a defense-linked prototype, not operational adoption, repeat production, yield, or program revenue. The public-evidence bottleneck is program linkage and repeat production for the new class, plus FPA/FPM identities, ROIC design and wafer location, cryocooler and compound-wafer supply, and Mitsubishi’s lower-tier BOM.

Korea is the largest revision versus a “payload-only, no domestic detector” map. i3system lists InSb and T2SL MWIR, T2SL LWIR, uncooled microbolometers, and InGaAs SWIR, including cooled arrays to 1280×1024 — portfolio, not volume. The production base is the 2025 filing: $83.49M infrared imaging-sensor revenue (95.4% of total), ~$40.63M exports, and military product supplied after development test, operational test, and conformity assessment. Supply is described as going to LIG Nex1, Hanwha Systems, or ADD for integration — a general defense relationship, not a program-level BOM. Hanwha Systems shows satellite EO/IR and SAR, military-satellite EO modules, KF-21 mission/display integration, avionics, and EO equipment — payload and mission-system evidence, not platform-prime evidence. Upstream, compound-semiconductor wafers for cooled IR and X-ray are imported; 2025 cooled-IR lens purchases were $5.38k domestic vs ~$3.20M imported; cryocoolers mix Korean FS with Ricor and Thales Cryogenics; ROIC design authority, IP, foundry access, and fab country are not publicly established. Repeat production is proven; full-stack control is not. A concentrated domestic supplier is both national control and a single point of failure.

Europe has a broad active base and is not one sovereign stack. Lynred opened an Infrared Campus near Grenoble in 2026 (8,000 m² cleanroom; plan to double capacity by 2030) and claims the 100,000th cooled detector in 2020 and >3 million IR detectors in total — company-reported production heritage, not a clean current-run-rate. AIM reports CdTe/HgCdTe growth and epitaxy, silicon ROIC *design*, hybridization, dewar, Stirling and pulse-tube coolers, image processing, and thermal sights; €90.7M 2024 revenue; fielded with the Bundeswehr and several NATO countries. ROIC design is not proof of an AIM-owned advanced CMOS foundry. Leonardo UK in Southampton does cooled MCT and uncooled detectors, ROIC design support, growth, hybridization, vacuum packaging, and environmental test, and is wholly owned by Italy’s Leonardo S.p.A. Exosens Detection & Imaging printed €77.4M in H1 2026 (+24.3% YoY) on defense/surveillance including drone, C-UAS, and long-range, and said it was doubling cooled-IR camera capacity in 2026 — segment mix includes acquired businesses, so it is not detector/FPA profitability. SPIRIT (announced May 2026, start end-2026, four years) targets >3 MP detectors at <7 µm pitch via advanced CMOS foundry access from ROIC design to camera demonstrators. That is a recognized gap, not evidence the EU CMOS-ROIC chain already exists.

The closing tension is the one that maps onto the vault: the mission-essential layer, the hardest-to-substitute bottleneck, and the profit pool can be three different seats. The detector is the first physical conversion; the ROIC or cryocooler may be the substitution bottleneck; calibration software or the mission-system integrator may hold contractual leverage; the prime on upgrades and depot sustainment may take the long-term revenue. For a company analyst the follow is the profit pool; for a state the follow is the bottleneck that cannot be requalified in time.

## Framework / Mental Model

Superposition’s reusable object is a four-axis industrial map plus a nested assembly hierarchy. It is not a country scorecard.

**Axis 1 — Coverage.** Do sensing materials, detector devices, ROICs, FPAs, cooling, camera modules, payloads, and mission systems exist inside the ecosystem? A product page or research history can establish capability without establishing repeat production.

**Axis 2 — Program linkage.** Are those layers connected through a qualified supply relationship inside a named satellite, aircraft, ground vehicle, or naval system? Co-location of a detector company and a payload integrator is not linkage.

**Axis 3 — Industrial control / substitutability.** Who holds design authority, process knowledge, critical wafers and tools, ROIC-foundry access, qualification/acceptance data, calibration software, repair and upgrade rights, and export licenses? Can production continue if licenses are withdrawn? How long does redesign and requalification take? Domestic final assembly with foreign rights is not control. Autarky is not the test; inventory, alternate qualified suppliers, and requalification time are.

**Axis 4 — Economic value capture.** Mission criticality is necessary and not sufficient. Low volume, heavy capex, poor yields, fixed-price contracts, and prime bargaining power can suppress supplier profit. Scarcity, switching/requalification cost, contractual leverage, software/data rights, and long-term sustainment responsibility have to be present for excess return.

**Assembly hierarchy (physical) vs control layer (rights).** material/device → ROIC → hybrid FPA → dewar/cooler → FPM/IDCA → camera core → payload/mission system. Control sits beside that stack, not at the end of it.

**Maturity states (do not collapse).** development test ≠ qualification ≠ customer acceptance ≠ production approval ≠ fielding ≠ multi-decade sustainment.

**How the map can be wrong (author’s own falsifiers).** More visible documentation ≠ stronger absolute capability. Spec sheets diverge from repeat-production performance. Co-located firms may not share a qualified BOM. Foreign sourcing can be resilience if multi-sourced and fast to requalify. Mission-critical FPAs can be unprofitable; software/sustainment primes can take the rent.

## Contradiction Check

No existing thesis owns the cooled-IR / FPM / IDCA layer, so this note does not confirm or break a named detector-company conviction. It does bear on three vault objects:

- [[Theses/TSM - Taiwan Semiconductor]] §Non-consensus (CoWoS / COUPE as separable lines) and the foundry-platform claim: Superposition’s Europe section names advanced CMOS-ROIC access as a *planned* SPIRIT gap, not a current EU capability. That is weakly supportive of TSMC (and other advanced CMOS foundries) as the hidden ROIC-wafer seat inside “sovereign” detector stacks — including AIM’s in-house ROIC *design* without an owned advanced CMOS fab. It does **not** give TSMC a defense-IR revenue line, and it does not touch TSM’s re-armed HIGH (FY26 >40% / Q3 GM ≥66% / 2027 capex ≥$70B) or LOW (HPC growth, GM, Arizona, CoWoS alternative) triggers. Treat as a second-order foundry-adjacency, not a thesis confirmation.

- [[Theses/NVDA - Nvidia]] physical-AI / “machines acquire strategic eyes” rhetoric: the Seeing Stack’s profit-pool warning cuts the other way. The detector may be mission-essential and still a poor equity; calibration software, mission-system integration, and depot sustainment may take the rent. Consensus that “physical AI needs cameras → buy the sensor name” is the error this map is written against.

- [[Theses/AMAT - Applied Materials]] / [[Theses/BESI - BE Semiconductor Industries]] hybridization, bonding, and process-tool adjacency: the map lists hybridization, epitaxy, and ROIC wafers as control points but gives no tool-qualification or WFE dollar path. Do not propagate as a WFE beat.

Honest read: this is a high-quality public-evidence floor for a sector the vault does not cover. It challenges the lazy “domestic IR = sovereign stack = investable monopoly” heuristic more than it challenges any named thesis assumption.
