# UBER — Sentiment, Valuation, and Autonomous Vehicle Risk

## Thesis Delta

**The prior “Android of autonomy” claim no longer survives unchanged: Waymo's planned direct apps in Austin and Atlanta trigger the thesis's explicit disintermediation falsifier.** The financial impact is still distant—AVs account for less than 0.5% of Uber trips—but the risk has shifted from theoretical technology competition to observed supplier bypass. Uber's response requires more than $10B of capital and proof that non-Waymo partners can move from announcements to thousands of exclusive paid vehicles.

The trough multiple is not explained by AV alone. The $14.8B Delivery Hero offer, a multi-year integration, slower buybacks, rising cash taxes, stock compensation, regulatory exposure, reported-growth noise, and extreme sensitivity to small guidance misses all contribute. At roughly $70, 16.2x NTM earnings and a 7.1% FCF yield look cheap against forecast EPS growth above 30%, but the market is discounting post-2027 cash-flow durability and lower returns on reinvested capital. **Conviction should be reassessed rather than automatically downgraded: current earnings refute an immediate-demand bear case, while the old AV falsifier has fired.**

## Summary

Sentiment is more negative in price action than in published research. Uber is down roughly 23% over 52 weeks, trades below its 200-day average, and has de-rated from a 24.2x forward P/E at the end of 2025 to 16.2x. Yet 43 of 50 analysts remain at Buy or Strong Buy, the average target is $102, and short interest is only 2.6% of float. This is not a crowded short or a sell-side capitulation. The marginal investor is applying a terminal-value haircut: Waymo headlines caused repeated 4%+ declines, and a Q3 guide only fractionally below consensus produced a 5.3% post-earnings fall.

Waymo is the credible source of that duration risk. It ended the Phoenix partnership, exceeds 500,000 weekly rides, and plans to offer its own app in Austin and Atlanta from January 2028. Uber retains counter-evidence: its category share and trip growth accelerated in Los Angeles, San Francisco, and Phoenix; AVs remain below 0.5% of its roughly 300M weekly trips; and only 30% of U.S. bookings and 25% of U.S. profit come from the top 20 cities where robotaxis should scale first. The next two years are therefore a bargaining-power test, not an earnings-cliff forecast.

The non-AV discount is partly earned. Uber is replacing an asset-light, buyback-led narrative with two large capital programs: Delivery Hero and autonomy. The $14.8B Delivery Hero transaction will not close until H2 2027 and its systems migration extends into 2029. Uber also expects more than $10B of AV equity, infrastructure, and vehicle-offtake commitments without disclosing the P&L burden. Those bets may strengthen the platform, but they move the thesis from demand aggregation with low capital intensity to capital allocation under technological uncertainty.

### 2. The self-driving risk has hardened

**Evidence for the bear case**

- Waymo ended the Phoenix Uber partnership at contract expiry and returned those vehicles to its own app.
- Waymo told Uber it intends to launch direct Austin and Atlanta apps in January 2028. The exclusivity ends, although hundreds of vehicles are expected to remain on Uber until at least May 2028.
- Waymo exceeds 500,000 weekly paid rides. BTIG estimates roughly 3,800 of the approximately 4,100 paid U.S. robotaxis are Waymo vehicles.
- BTIG estimates roughly 1,000 AVs are currently available through Uber, around 800 of which are Waymo; only about 200 are exclusive non-Waymo supply. The announced partner pipeline is therefore orders of magnitude larger than deployed evidence.
- The largest AV supplier has its own consumer app, brand, fleet economics, and access to Alphabet's capital. It can internalize demand acquisition in dense cities and use Uber only where incremental utilization justifies the fee.

**Evidence against an immediate earnings cliff**

- AV trips remain below 0.5% of Uber's roughly 300M weekly trips.
- Uber reported higher category share and faster trip growth in mature AV markets—Los Angeles, San Francisco, and Phoenix—while robotaxi supply grew.
- On-platform AV utilization is in the high-20s to low-30s trips per vehicle per day, supporting the claim that Uber's demand density raises fleet utilization.
- Only 30% of U.S. Gross Bookings and 25% of U.S. profit comes from the top 20 cities. Suburbs, smaller cities, airport corridors, and complex geographies will automate more slowly.
- Uber has roughly 120,000 partner vehicles committed, operates AV services in seven cities, and targets up to 15 by year-end. Supplier fragmentation remains possible, but commitments are not deployed supply.

**The falsifiable bridge:** count exclusive non-Waymo vehicles in paid service, not partner logos or announced units. At least 1,000 such vehicles by end-2027 with roughly 25–30+ daily trips would show a second scaled supplier base forming. Fewer than 1,000 would leave Waymo with effective hold-up power before the Austin/Atlanta direct launches. Whether Waymo vehicles stay on Uber after May 2028 is the subsequent distribution test.

The 16.2x NTM P/E and the roughly 21x FY2026 P/E are not contradictory: NTM includes more of higher-EPS 2027, while FY2026 includes already-reported lower-EPS quarters. Trailing GAAP P/E is a poor anchor because equity revaluations create large non-operating gains and losses.

The earnings-growth forecast assumes operating leverage survives low-teens reported revenue growth. It can: Q2 non-GAAP operating income rose 40%, adjusted EBITDA 33%, and EPS 35%. The outside-view challenge is that sustained 30% EPS growth becomes harder as the base grows and as Delivery Hero, AV, taxes, and SBC absorb cash. At 16.2x, the market does not need current earnings to collapse; it only needs the 30% growth period to end quickly or the cash to be reinvested below prior returns.

## Contradiction Check

**What disproves the simplistic AV bear case:** Q2 demand and profit growth remain strong; AVs are economically immaterial today; Uber's share rose in mature Waymo markets; long-tail geographies reduce the speed of fleet substitution; and high AV utilization on Uber demonstrates that demand aggregation has value. Waymo going direct does not prove it can match Uber's liquidity, pickup coverage, support, and cross-modal demand in every market.

**What disproves the old bullish framing:** the named falsifier has occurred. Waymo is no longer merely keeping its own app elsewhere; it is taking the direct channel into the two markets that anchored Uber's exclusivity argument. Uber's response also ceases to be purely asset-light once it commits more than $10B and guarantees vehicle offtake. Delivery Hero compounds the capital-allocation test.

**What remains unknown:** Uber has not disclosed AV take-rate economics, depot-level returns, vehicle-offtake liabilities, the timing of the >$10B outlay, or a deployed non-Waymo fleet bridge from roughly 200 exclusive units to thousands. These variables matter more than total announced vehicles.

**Balanced conclusion:** current multiples compensate for a meaningful portion of the risk, but “cheap plus 30% EPS growth” is not sufficient to retain HIGH conviction after an explicit falsifier fires. Keep conviction unchanged in metadata until a status decision is requested, but treat it as weakened pending non-Waymo fleet evidence and Delivery Hero/AV ROIIC disclosure.

## Framework / Mental Model

- **[[Generalist - Overview]] [G-6], two-sided marketplace disintermediation:** Uber's demand network is valuable while supply is fragmented; Waymo tests whether a scaled supplier can internalize demand acquisition.
- **[[Generalist - Overview]] [G-8], capital allocation:** Delivery Hero plus AV commitments must beat the return from repurchasing shares at a 7% FCF yield.
- **[[Generalist - Overview]] [G-13], price-implied expectations:** the key question is not whether 16.2x is optically cheap, but whether the market is too pessimistic about exclusive non-Waymo fleet scale.
- **[[Lens - Value Layer Monopoly]] §1A/§2, interface control vs layer-renter:** Uber controls the rider interface only if no AV supplier becomes indispensable. Waymo's direct app activates the supplier-hold-up disqualifier.
- **[[Lens - Automation & AI Readiness]] §3 Lens A / §5:** near-100% developer-tool adoption and doubled code output support internal operating leverage, but a physical and regulated transport core limits how far software automation can protect bargaining power.
- **Base-rate disconfirmation:** marketplaces can survive direct-booking campaigns when supply remains fragmented; a dominant supplier with its own app is the adverse reference case. Agreement between network-effect and value-layer lenses is therefore a reason to test the supplier concentration bear case, not a buy signal.
