## Summary

SemiVision, published via TSPA Semiconductor on 18 August 2026 (Gmail `1a0127b5e0307ae9`; ~6-minute free article), recasts TSMC’s Arizona build as customer-pull rather than a political hostage story. The geopolitical frame is granted — Washington wants advanced production on US soil, Taipei worries about industry migration, critics are right that Arizona wafers cost more than Hsinchu — then set aside. The load-bearing fact is geography of *customers*, not of fabs: in H1 2026, US-headquartered customers were 75.64% of TSMC consolidated revenue per the company’s financial statements, versus 6.85% Taiwan, 6.76% China, 3.62% Japan, 4.05% EMEA. For every NT$100 of revenue, ~NT$76 comes from American HQs. Named demand: Apple, Nvidia, AMD, Broadcom, custom-AI accelerator developers, and US cloud giants spending on datacentres, networking, and AI infrastructure. The author’s mechanism is that TSMC has become strategic infrastructure for those firms, not an ordinary foundry vendor; once more than three-quarters of revenue sits in one country, putting meaningful capacity there is ordinary multinational behaviour. Customers pull suppliers closer.

The cost objection is not denied. Clustering still favours Taiwan: engineers, contractors, equipment-support, chemicals, gases, wafers, components in a compact geography. Arizona has higher labour, harder construction and hiring, suppliers that must localise, and Hsinchu management practices that do not transplant into the Sonoran Desert. A wafer-cost comparison will always make America look unattractive. The author’s move is to change the objective function: TSMC is choosing how to serve customers whose chips are economically and politically indispensable, not where a wafer is cheapest. For Nvidia, Apple, or a hyperscaler, some advanced US capacity is a resilience layer. That does not make Arizona cheaper. It may make it valuable. The new empirical kicker is that the factories have started to print: Arizona ~NT$36.1bn ($1.2bn) H1 2026 profit; after unrealised intercompany-gain adjustments, TSMC recognised ~NT$31.2bn investment income. One six-month period does not settle US manufacturing economics — utilisation, depreciation, mix, wafer pricing, and government support will swing the print — but it weakens the claim that Arizona is *only* an expensive political project. Four forces now point the same way: American customers, American demand growth, American incentives, and now American-fab earnings.

Politics is not written out. CHIPS Act, export controls, and US–China tech rivalry all push diversification; Washington wants more of the chain onshore. The author still rejects “TSMC as victim of Washington” as the complete story. Any manufacturer whose largest customers are concentrated in one country *and* raising capex at an exceptional pace would build closer to them. The useful question therefore flips from why America to how much capacity belongs there. The qualification that protects the Taiwan core is accounting, not rhetoric: 75.64% US-HQ revenue does not mean 75.64% of production should move, and does not mean three-quarters of chips are made in America. TSMC books geography by operational HQ; an Nvidia processor fabricated in Taiwan, packaged in Asia, and installed in a server elsewhere still sits in the American customer bucket. Customer map ≠ factory map. Leading-edge R&D, manufacturing expertise, supplier network, and advanced packaging remain concentrated in Taiwan, and 2nm / A16 / 3D integration may *increase* the value of co-location. Arizona will not replace Hsinchu. The plausible structure is two-tier: Taiwan as technological and manufacturing core; America as the most important overseas production base next to the largest customers; Japan for auto / industrial / specialty; Europe around auto and industrial. Not de-Taiwanisation — globalisation of manufacturing around a Taiwanese core.

For investors the author ranks 75.64% above Arizona’s profit. That share is the commercial centre-of-gravity print. AI reinforces it: the firms spending most aggressively on GPUs, custom accelerators, networking silicon, and cloud infrastructure are overwhelmingly American, so pressure for geographically diversified manufacturing should rise as those requirements get larger and more strategically sensitive. The factory map is beginning, slowly, to resemble the customer map. Preserving Taiwan as the centre of the technological ecosystem remains essential to the competitive advantage; a company whose customers are global cannot stay as geographically concentrated as before. Arizona, on this reading, is neither aberration nor merely the price of political pressure. It is TSMC evolving from a Taiwanese manufacturer serving the world into a global manufacturer whose indispensable core remains in Taiwan. When more than three-quarters of revenue comes from American customers, building some of the most important factories in America “may simply be good business.”

## Contradiction Check

**Supports, with a definitional haircut,** [[Theses/TSM - Taiwan Semiconductor]] §Insight #2 (silicon shield quantified) and §Mental Models #15/#16: the US footprint now has an H1 P&L print (~NT$36.1bn Arizona profit; NT$31.2bn recognised income), not only a $265B co-investment / CHIPS political put. That is colour on the *commercial* leg of the shield, consistent with the already-recorded “Arizona Q1 profit exceeded FY25” mental-model line, and it does **not** shrink the island-tail. The source is explicit that HQ geography ≠ fab geography and that packaging + leading-edge remain Taiwan-concentrated — which **supports** the [[Research/2026-04-19 - TSM - Stress Test]] / Foundries Q9 read that Arizona 5–8% through 2030 is not a hedge at the thesis horizon.

**Challenges the framing, not the arithmetic, of** §Risk 4 (Arizona as permanent 300bps+ cost centre) and §Bear #4 (yield-parity delay). A profitable H1 weakens “purely political cost centre” and is why the author says wafer-cost comparison is too narrow. It does **not** falsify blended GM dilution: subsidiary profit can coexist with 2–3pt (→3–4pt) overseas-fab drag at group level, and the essay gives no Arizona mix %, no N2 Fab 22 yield, and no wafer-cost delta. Outstanding Q2 (Arizona yield gap closing vs widening; answer dated to Q2 2027 when Arizona N4 mix crosses 15%) remains open.

## Source Excerpts

> “In the first half of 2026, customers headquartered in the United States accounted for 75.64% of TSMC’s consolidated revenue, according to the company’s financial statements. That compares with just 6.85% from Taiwan, 6.76% from China, 3.62% from Japan and 4.05% from Europe, the Middle East and Africa.”

> “Put differently, for every NT$100 of revenue generated by TSMC, roughly NT$76 now comes from customers headquartered in America.”

> “Its most important customers include the companies driving the global computing boom: Apple, Nvidia, AMD, Broadcom and a growing collection of developers of custom AI accelerators.”

> “TSMC Arizona reported approximately NT$36.1bn ($1.2bn) in profit in the first half of 2026. After adjustments including unrealised gains from intercompany transactions, TSMC recognised roughly NT$31.2bn in investment income.”

> “The customers are American. The fastest-growing demand is American. The government incentives are American. And now the factories themselves are beginning to generate meaningful earnings.”

> “The fact that 75.64% of TSMC’s revenue comes from American-headquartered customers does not mean that 75.64% of its production should move to America. Nor does it mean that three-quarters of TSMC’s chips are currently manufactured there.”

> “TSMC classifies geographical revenue largely according to the location of a customer’s operational headquarters. An Nvidia processor fabricated in Taiwan, packaged in Asia and installed in a server elsewhere can still contribute to the company’s American customer category.”

> “Taiwan remains TSMC’s technological and manufacturing core. America becomes its most important overseas production base, positioned close to its largest and strategically most important customers.”

> “This is not de-Taiwanisation so much as globalisation of manufacturing around a Taiwanese core.”

> “For investors, the most revealing number may therefore not be Arizona’s profit. It may be 75.64%.”

> “When more than three-quarters of your revenue comes from American customers, building some of your most important factories in America is not particularly surprising. It may simply be good business.”
