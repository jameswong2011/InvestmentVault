## Summary

SemiVision, published via TSPA Semiconductor on 19 August 2026 (Gmail `1a017ea271a9c87f`; ~11-minute full article, paid/full subscription delivered in Gmail PLAIN_TEXT), uses SEMICON Taiwan 2026’s unusual shared stage — TSMC, ASE, MediaTek, Unimicron, and Hon Hai/Foxconn in the same discussion — to relabel those five names Taiwanese media’s semiconductor “Five Tigers.” The term is not an official industry classification. The analogy is the Chinese “Five Tiger Generals”: five commanders on different fronts who collectively form the military core of one camp. The five companies are not direct competitors; each controls a different and increasingly critical layer of the AI hardware stack. MediaTek = AI ASIC design. TSMC = advanced logic manufacturing. ASE = advanced packaging and test. Unimicron = high-end ABF substrates and PCBs. Foxconn = system-level integration of processors, networking, power, cooling, and mechanical infrastructure into deployable AI servers and racks. Viewed together, they are a framework for why Taiwan’s AI position is much broader than wafer fabrication alone.

The author’s mechanism is bottleneck migration. The AI infrastructure race is no longer simply about who can secure the most GPUs. Constraints have already moved from GPU availability (early generative-AI boom) to HBM, then to CoWoS, and are now becoming more distributed across interposers, substrates, thermal design, test capacity, optical connectivity, and package yield. The competitive unit is gradually moving from the chip to the package, from the package to the rack, and from the rack to the AI factory. That is the backdrop against which the Five Tigers should be analysed.

MediaTek is still primarily associated with smartphone SoCs; that description is becoming incomplete. The strategically important roadmap is expansion into AI data-center ASICs, where hyperscalers want alternatives to a purely merchant-GPU architecture. These customers are not necessarily trying to replace NVIDIA across every workload. They are developing custom silicon for workloads where they can improve cost, power efficiency, memory architecture, networking, or software control versus a general-purpose accelerator. Building a competitive AI ASIC requires more than RTL design: leading-edge process technology, advanced SerDes, HBM integration, chiplets, advanced packaging, and close coordination with system-level power and networking architectures. MediaTek has accumulated many of those capabilities through years of complex mobile and connectivity SoCs, which is why the data-center move is more credible than it first appears. The key investor question is not whether custom ASICs replace GPUs. NVIDIA’s ecosystem remains extraordinarily difficult to displace because the advantage extends beyond silicon into CUDA, networking, rack-scale architecture, and software. The relevant question is how much *incremental* AI compute migrates toward custom accelerators developed by Google, Amazon, Meta, Microsoft, and other hyperscalers. Even if GPUs remain dominant, a relatively modest shift in incremental workloads toward custom silicon could create a very large addressable market for MediaTek and Taiwan’s broader ASIC ecosystem. If the mobile-chip → infrastructure-silicon transition succeeds, it could be one of the more important structural changes in Taiwan’s semiconductor landscape over the next several years.

TSMC’s importance to AI is obvious; the nature of that importance is changing. The conventional “semiconductor foundry” description is becoming too narrow because modern AI accelerators depend not only on leading-edge transistors but also on HBM integration, silicon interposers, redistribution layers, advanced bonding, chiplets, and increasingly complex packaging architectures. The manufacturing problem is expanding from wafer fabrication into system integration. CoWoS is the most visible example of that expansion. What TSMC is effectively selling is changing: a next-generation AI accelerator is less like a conventional monolithic semiconductor and more like a miniature computing system assembled within a package. As compute dies, I/O dies, HBM stacks, interposers, and eventually optical engines are integrated more tightly, advanced packaging becomes part of processor architecture rather than a downstream manufacturing step. Therefore the most important SEMICON Taiwan question is no longer simply how much additional CoWoS capacity TSMC plans to add. The more fundamental issue is how large and complex an AI package can become before existing manufacturing architectures encounter physical or economic limits. The answer will determine demand for new bonding tools, inspection systems, substrate materials, thermal interfaces, panel-level packaging, and potentially optical I/O. The author’s label for this is system foundry economics.

ASE illustrates the OSAT reversal. Historically, value creation was perceived to sit toward the front end: design the chip, fabricate the wafer, send it downstream for packaging and test. That hierarchy is becoming less accurate because packaging is now one of the primary determinants of how much compute, memory, and I/O can be integrated into a usable AI system. ASE’s VIPack platform spans advanced redistribution, fan-out, 2.5D and 3D integration, and heterogeneous chiplet architectures. Strategic value of those technologies increases as AI packages grow larger and more complex, because package size directly affects yield, warpage, thermal behavior, routing density, and assembly economics. Panel-level packaging is the interesting fork: wafer-level processes work extremely well when package dimensions remain relatively small, but AI accelerators are moving in the opposite direction — more compute dies, more HBM stacks, more I/O, larger interposers — which increases pressure to process larger rectangular areas more efficiently. At some point the economics of fabricating very large packages on circular wafers becomes increasingly uncomfortable. For ASE the strategic question is therefore not simply how much advanced packaging TSMC may outsource. The more important issue is whether ASE can develop architectures and manufacturing platforms that are complementary to, rather than merely downstream from, TSMC’s packaging roadmap. If that happens, OSAT moves from manufacturing support back into the critical path of AI system development.

Unimicron is the substrate bottleneck the market underestimates because ABF looks passive next to GPUs, HBM, or advanced packaging. Larger AI accelerators turn the substrate into a hard engineering problem: higher routing density, more I/O, greater layer counts, tighter line-and-space, and increasingly severe mechanical and thermal stresses. Larger substrates make warpage harder to control; higher-speed electrical interfaces tighten signal-integrity requirements; more layers, more vias, and larger panel areas reduce manufacturing margin for error. These effects compound, so the substrate can become limiting even when silicon and HBM are available. The useful Unimicron question is not simply whether ABF pricing will rise, but how large next-generation AI substrates need to become and what material systems can support that scaling. Once package dimensions move higher, demand shifts upstream into low-loss glass cloth, ABF films, copper foil, specialty resin systems, drilling and laser equipment, plating, inspection, and potentially glass-core substrates. Substrate and advanced packaging are the same scaling problem: AI processors becoming physically larger, electrically denser, and mechanically more difficult to manufacture. If the next generation of accelerator packages pushes substrate dimensions substantially higher, this area could become one of the most important bottlenecks in the supply chain.

Foxconn is the reminder that the first four Tigers can produce world-class semiconductor components but none of them alone creates usable AI infrastructure. Someone still has to integrate GPUs, CPUs, switches, power systems, cooling hardware, cables, NICs, trays, racks, and management systems into a platform that can operate reliably inside a data center. AI servers are changing the product: the unit of deployment is moving away from an individual server toward the rack, and the rack itself is becoming a tightly integrated computing system of power distribution, liquid cooling, high-speed networking, mechanical design, and increasingly complex system validation. This is particularly important as NVIDIA moves from standalone GPU platforms toward rack-scale architectures. Blackwell accelerated this transition; Rubin is likely to push it further. The ODM challenge is no longer assembling a motherboard inside a chassis; it is coordinating a complete system in which power, thermals, networking, and compute architecture all constrain one another. The strategic question is whether Foxconn remains primarily a manufacturing partner or evolves into an infrastructure integrator capable of delivering increasingly complete AI factories. If customers begin procuring not only servers or racks but megawatt-scale computing blocks, the value captured by system integrators could rise materially — Foxconn moving further upstream in system design and further downstream into data-center deployment at the same time.

Five watch items at SEMICON Taiwan 2026, none of which is a single new piece of equipment. First: speed of custom ASIC demand. GPUs remain central; hyperscaler silicon programs are multiplying; that benefits design houses such as MediaTek *and* foundries, HBM suppliers, substrate vendors, advanced packaging companies, and test-equipment makers. Custom ASIC does not need to displace NVIDIA to become one of the industry’s largest growth markets. Second: whether advanced packaging remains the dominant scaling constraint. Slowing monolithic scaling was answered by integrating more compute and memory at the package level; that does not eliminate bottlenecks, it moves them. HBM, interposers, bonding, substrates, package yield, thermal management, and test capacity are increasingly interconnected; the manufacturing chain is no longer linear because a limitation in one layer propagates across the system. Third: package size. Larger packages offer more routing, more memory bandwidth, and more compute integration, but worsen warpage, thermal stress, substrate complexity, and yield. That is why larger interposers, panel-level packaging, glass substrates, hybrid bonding, and optical I/O deserve attention. Much of the next AI hardware cycle may be determined by how effectively the industry can keep increasing package scale without letting manufacturing economics deteriorate too rapidly. Fourth: whether the product itself is becoming the rack. The industry historically optimized chips, then packages, and is now increasingly optimizing entire rack-scale systems. Compute, networking, memory, optics, power, and cooling have to be co-designed, creating opportunities far beyond semiconductor vendors — power companies, liquid-cooling suppliers, connector makers, cable vendors, rack builders, and system integrators. Fifth, and arguably the most valuable for investors: where the next bottleneck will emerge. AI has already created extreme tightness in HBM and CoWoS, and it is putting pressure on high-end PCB materials, advanced substrates, copper foil, glass cloth, power systems, and optical components. The largest opportunities often appear when an item previously treated as a low-value component suddenly becomes capacity-constrained or technologically difficult.

The closing claim is that the biggest investor mistake is analysing Taiwan’s AI advantage company by company. TSMC is critical, but TSMC alone does not explain Taiwan. Neither do MediaTek, ASE, Unimicron, or Foxconn individually. The deeper advantage is the density of the industrial ecosystem and the speed at which different layers can interact. Within a relatively small geographic area Taiwan contains semiconductor design houses, leading-edge foundries, OSATs, substrate makers, PCB suppliers, ODMs, power-electronics companies, cooling suppliers, connector vendors, test-equipment companies, specialty chemical producers, and precision manufacturing firms. That concentration materially reduces engineering iteration time: larger-substrate requests can be worked with material suppliers directly; warpage problems can be adjusted in parallel by packaging houses, equipment makers, and chemical suppliers; when rack power rises toward hundreds of kilowatts, server ODMs can redesign with power and cooling companies rather than treating those systems as downstream components. The advantage is therefore not simply lower manufacturing cost. It is engineering velocity. The Five Tigers are not five versions of the same company. They represent five layers of the same AI infrastructure machine. As AI moves from chip-level competition toward system-level optimization, the value of that coordination increases. For investors at SEMICON Taiwan 2026 the objective should not be the next fashionable semiconductor technology but where AI systems are becoming physically difficult, expensive, or capacity-constrained to build. Wherever complexity rises fastest, the next bottleneck — and often the next major supply-chain opportunity — is usually not far behind.

## Framework / Mental Model

**Five Tigers (SemiVision / Taiwanese media, not an official industry classification).** Five companies as five layers of one AI infrastructure machine, analogous to the Chinese “Five Tiger Generals” (different fronts, same camp). Components:

| Layer | Company | Function in the stack |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Design | MediaTek | AI ASIC / custom data-center silicon (mobile SoC heritage → infrastructure-silicon) |
| 2. Logic manufacturing | TSMC | Advanced logic foundry expanding into system-platform / system-foundry economics |
| 3. Packaging & test | ASE | OSAT; VIPack = redistribution, fan-out, 2.5D/3D, heterogeneous chiplets; panel-level packaging as the large-package fork |
| 4. Substrate / PCB | Unimicron | High-end ABF substrates and PCBs; warpage, line-and-space, layer-count, glass-core optionality |
| 5. System integration | Hon Hai / Foxconn | Rack-scale AI servers: processors, networking, power, cooling, mechanical; path from EMS toward AI-factory integrator |

**Methodology the source applies.** Do not analyse the five as competitors or as five versions of TSMC. Ask, at each layer, where the bottleneck is migrating and whether that layer is becoming physically difficult, expensive, or capacity-constrained. The five SEMICON watch questions (custom-ASIC demand speed; packaging as dominant constraint; package size; rack as the product; next bottleneck) are the instance-level application of the same framework.

**Bottleneck-migration sequence (named, sequential).** GPU availability → HBM → CoWoS → distributed (interposers, substrates, thermal design, test capacity, optical connectivity, package yield). Parallel competitive-unit sequence: chip → package → rack → AI factory.

**System foundry economics (TSMC-layer label).** Once compute dies, I/O dies, HBM stacks, interposers, and eventually optical engines are co-integrated, packaging is processor architecture, not a downstream step. Capacity questions therefore broaden from “how much more CoWoS” to “how large/complex can a package become before physical or economic limits,” which pulls demand into bonding tools, inspection, substrate materials, thermal interfaces, panel-level packaging, and optical I/O.

**Industrial density = engineering velocity (Taiwan-cluster label).** The moat is co-location of design, foundry, OSAT, substrate, PCB, ODM, power, cooling, connectors, test equipment, specialty chemicals, and precision manufacturing inside a small geography. Cost is secondary; iteration time is the advantage. Worked examples in the source: larger substrate → substrate vendors work with material suppliers; warpage → packaging, equipment, and chemicals adjust in parallel; rack power toward hundreds of kilowatts → ODMs redesign with power and cooling rather than treating them as downstream.
