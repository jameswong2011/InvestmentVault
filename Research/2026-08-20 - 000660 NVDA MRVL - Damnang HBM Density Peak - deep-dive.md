## Summary

Damnang’s 20 August 2026 essay, “HBM Density Peak: Why AI Value Is Moving From Memory to Connectivity,” stitches three public threads into one system-architecture claim. Rubin Ultra’s HBM configuration is being reviewed downward from HBM4E 12-Hi toward HBM4 8-Hi. TrendForce (4 August 2026) said NVIDIA is evaluating several lower-capacity configurations and that the final specification is unset. The Information reported 192GB and 256GB prototype testing. SemiAnalysis sees the main SKU moving to HBM4 8-Hi 192GB with compute held and memory bandwidth slightly higher. The author’s field check is that major memory makers are now in daily meetings with the customer on 8-Hi as the main Rubin Ultra configuration; a 4-Hi rumor is named and discarded for lack of a verifiable basis. Supply is the direct cause: DRAM tightness through 2027, HBM4E qualification uncertainty at the performance level NVIDIA is asking for, and 12-layer stacking costing more in throughput and yield than eight. What makes the cut *workable* is system structure. On Rubin Ultra, bandwidth and data movement have become a larger constraint than memory density. Adding stacks around the GPU raises package area and interposer-edge I/O cost; raising layer count inside a stack raises stacking complexity, yield loss, thermal load, and package complexity. A wider scale-up domain lets some capacity move to peer HBM and to memory tiers outside the package. Software and memory-placement techniques that keep performance intact at HBM4 8-Hi 192GB were covered in the author’s 10 August 2026 companion, “What Does Rubin Ultra’s 8-Hi HBM Mean?”

That direction leads to optics and memory disaggregation, not to deleting local HBM. A Nature Electronics review published 20 August 2026 — Kim, B. et al., *Co-packaged optics for high-performance computing and artificial intelligence*, with an SK hynix researcher as corresponding author — states that compute throughput has tripled roughly every two years while interconnect bandwidth advanced only about 1.4-fold over the same cycle. Even when HBM lifts in-package bandwidth, resistive loss and capacitive loading on copper traces grow again once data crosses the board and cables; equalization, retimers, DSP and FEC correct for that while adding power and latency. The paper’s integration roadmap moves the optical conversion point closer to compute at each step: pluggable and on-board optics, 2.5D CPO, 3D TSV, hybrid bonding, monolithic integration. 2.5D is the manufacturability/performance compromise; 3D delivers higher bandwidth density and lower energy per bit at thermal and bonding-yield cost. SemiAnalysis reads the optical technology in Rubin Ultra NVL576 as NPO. NVIDIA has disclosed that NVL576 binds eight racks of 72 GPUs into a single 576-GPU NVLink domain with direct optical rack-to-rack links; public NVIDIA technical material does **not** state whether that optical-link packaging topology is NPO or CPO. The author therefore treats NVL576 NPO as an intermediate step that lowers package-redesign and serviceability burden, not as the end of the optical transition.

Local HBM remains the fastest tier. On the author’s field checks, Rubin local HBM bandwidth is about 21 to 22TB/s, while NVLink 6 delivers roughly 3.6TB/s of GPU-to-GPU bandwidth per GPU. Another GPU’s HBM does not replace local HBM. What changes is how much of the weights and KV cache must sit in one GPU’s HBM. NVL576 implements that change inside the scale-up domain first: active weights and hot KV stay local; distributable weights and KV spread to peer HBM; software hides remote latency by prefetching or by sending the token to the GPU that holds the data. One step further is memory disaggregation — capacity itself moving outside the GPU package. The Nature Electronics Fig. 4 drawing of an XPU pool and a memory pool linked by a photonic interposer is named as a research direction, not an SK hynix commercial product roadmap; optical memory interface coherence, low-power photonic switching, and multi-hop regeneration remain unsolved. SK hynix’s shipping products move on electrical disaggregation first. CXL pooled memory lets multiple hosts share memory. CMM-Ax, disclosed 5 August 2026, attaches compute to CXL memory so long-context inference can handle a KV cache larger than GPU HBM; it is Marvell Structera A plus SK hynix DRAM and software. The confirmed SK hynix–Marvell contact is custom HBM and CMM-Ax, not a joint optical-memory product. Marvell’s Photonic Fabric (Celestial AI, acquisition completed 2 February 2026) was disclosed at FMS August as a shared warm KV-cache tier of up to 32TB over an optical fabric within 50m, with 2 to 3x token throughput as **vendor targets**, not commercial-deployment figures. The Photonic Fabric Module is a 2.5D package: ASIC + HBM cache + DDR5 on an active photonic interposer; up to eight DDR5 DIMMs, 72GB of HBM cache, 7.2Tbps of optical bandwidth. Fig. 4c of the paper (ASIC/CPU/HPU/HBM chiplets on a photonic interposer with embedded waveguides) and Marvell’s module share a layer structure; the paper’s inclusion of Celestial’s Photonic Fabric in an industrial-platform table is verifiable, but sharing a structure is not a joint product.

NVIDIA invested $2B in Marvell (announced 31 March 2026) and the two are co-developing NVLink Fusion scale-up networking and silicon photonics. The author’s three-company division of roles: NVIDIA offsets local HBM density with system-level scale-up; SK hynix extends the memory tier outside HBM (custom base die locally, CXL/CMM-Ax externally, photonic pool as a longer-dated paper direction); Marvell supplies the electrical and optical interconnect both structures require. HBM implications if 8-Hi is confirmed: mix-down in HBM content per GPU, but not a 1:1 revenue decline. Stacking time up to 1.5x and stack yield 1.084x under a 98% per-core-die yield assumption multiply to an effective output ceiling of about 1.63x. If NVIDIA absorbs that volume into additional GPU shipments, total HBM revenue lands at about 81.5% of plan if 8-Hi is priced at 50% of planned HBM4E, and about 98% at 60%. These are stack-count sensitivities, not company guidance or shipment forecasts. The 8-Hi configuration itself is unconfirmed industry-check information; the final specification may change. Competitive axis: if 8-Hi life extends, yield, capacity, supply stability and base-die functionality outweigh the 12-Hi / 16-Hi stack-height qualification premium. For Marvell the read-through runs the other way: keeping accelerator utilization intact while lowering local HBM capacity raises the importance of scale-up bandwidth, optical reach, and external memory tiers. As optics moves closer to compute (pluggable → NPO → CPO → photonic interposer), supply-chain value moves with it — optical engines and laser sources first, then silicon-photonics PICs, drivers and TIAs, fiber coupling, 2.5D interposers and 3D bonding. The author calls this a second round in optical investing and the reason the memory theme is running.

## Framework / Mental Model

**Name:** HBM Density Peak — system budget shifts from local memory density to connectivity (Damnang). A named inflection: pushing HBM stack height is no longer the only path to system optimization once a wider NVLink domain and software tiering can absorb per-GPU capacity loss.

**Components.**
