# Cloudflare: The Fourth Act Is Already in the Price

On 6 August Cloudflare reported second-quarter revenue of $696 million, up 36% year on year and accelerating from the 34% of the prior two quarters at a $2.8 billion run-rate, raised full-year guidance to $2.87 billion, printed dollar-based net retention of 120% against 114% a year earlier, added more than 80,000 paying customers in a single quarter, and disclosed that for the first time more than half of the traffic crossing its network had not been generated by a human. Over the following five sessions the stock rose 16% to a $331 close, then gave the entire move back across the next eight; at $285 on 26 August it trades where it closed before the print, roughly $101 billion of market value, 35 times this year's guided revenue, 40 times trailing, and 44% above where it began the year.

The case for Cloudflare rests on a position nobody disputes and a business that does not yet exist. Cloudflare proxies roughly a fifth of the web; it can see, classify and refuse every automated request made to that fifth; and it has spent thirteen months building the machinery (default blocks, a payment gateway, agent wallets, a stablecoin still unshipped) to charge for what it currently refuses. We own the position for the first fact and hold it at a medium weight because of the second: the market is paying for 'Act IV', management's name for the monetisation of machine demand, as though it had already been performed, and the framework we use to grade organisations that capture optionality, which names Cloudflare among its vanguard, closes its second gate on the stock at this price.

## A network that sells what already passes through it

Cloudflare's business model is easiest to see by contrast with the clouds it is usually compared to. A hyperscaler must attract a workload into a regional data centre and then bill for the compute, storage and bandwidth the workload consumes; Cloudflare sits in the path between any user, human or machine, and any origin, and bills for what it does to traffic that was going to flow anyway: inspecting it (DDoS mitigation, web application firewall, bot management, email security), accelerating it (content delivery, routing), processing it (Workers), storing what it produces (R2) and, prospectively, charging for it. The network that makes this possible is fourteen years of peering agreements with more than 13,000 networks across 335 cities in 125 countries, 534 terabits of capacity, and one architectural decision the hyperscalers have never copied: every server runs every service, so a new product deploys to the whole edge in seconds and a customer that arrived for DDoS protection adds compute or zero-trust access at zero incremental network cost. Roughly 95% of the connected population sits within 50 milliseconds of a Cloudflare server, a figure that mattered little when the visitor was a person reading a page and matters a great deal when it is an agent waiting on a round trip.

Free customers are the acquisition engine, and the reason the traffic share dwarfs the revenue: about 20% of the web, 36% of the top 10,000 sites, 45% of the Fortune 500 and 78% of the top fifty generative-AI web products (a16z's March list) run through Cloudflare, most of them paying little or nothing, which gives the company the largest threat-intelligence sample and the largest developer funnel in its category at close to zero marginal cost. Reported revenue splits into three functional layers the company does not disclose separately: security and performance (roughly 55-60% of revenue, the highest margin, the product most customers arrive through), Zero Trust and SASE under the Cloudflare One brand (20-25%, the highest revenue per user, annual recurring revenue up 43% in 2025), and the developer platform (15-20%, ARR up 137% in 2025 and, per the second-quarter call, now a 'meaningful revenue contributor'). In the second quarter the funnel visibly converted. Paying customers grew 74% year on year, customers spending more than $100,000 reached 4,698 (up 27%, a record 986 added over twelve months, 73% of revenue), remaining performance obligations of $2.73 billion grew 38% against 36% revenue growth, and growth itself has reaccelerated from 27% in the first quarter of 2025 to 34% by the fourth, 34% in the first quarter of this year and 36% in the second, a sequence rare at a $2.8 billion base and one the outside view (few companies hold above 20% growth for a decade, and the rate collapses as the base grows) says the company must re-earn every quarter before we extrapolate any of it.

## The isolate bet, nine years on

In 2017 Cloudflare chose to build its compute platform on V8 isolates, the sandboxing primitive inside Chrome, rather than on containers or virtual machines. An isolate shares one runtime across many executions, so a Worker starts in effectively zero milliseconds, scales to zero when idle and costs a fraction of a container to keep warm; a Lambda cold start runs 150 to 1,000 milliseconds, and Cloudflare bills Workers at $0.50 per million requests with the first 100,000 a day free. For eight years this was a developer-experience advantage, and what changed is the shape of the workload, because a hyperscaler's architecture is optimised for many copies of one application, the microservices model, whereas agents generate what management calls infinite ephemeral applications, many per user, each spun up for a task and discarded, a pattern isolates were built for and containers were not. Management's Investor Day arithmetic (one billion knowledge workers running ten agents each at ten agents per CPU, against global server-CPU production of 35-45 million units a year) is a claim we have not verified, as are the cost comparisons (blended agent cost of ownership about half of published AWS and Vercel pricing, three-quarters lower for a million agent sessions), but the adoption data is management's least contestable material: 7.4 million active developers, nearly two million of them added in the second quarter alone, more than the company added in all of 2025; Workers handling more than a tenth of the requests on the network; the Agents SDK at 3.2 million downloads a month. What the adoption numbers do not show is why a hyperscaler cannot match the isolate model without dismantling the idle-capacity revenue embedded in reserved instances and provisioned concurrency, which is the same innovator's-dilemma mechanism behind R2, the object store with zero egress fees ($15 a month to move ten terabytes out against roughly $923 on S3) that has already forced AWS into defensive price cuts and free migration egress: Cloudflare wins by making the hyperscaler cheaper to use, never by replacing it, and intercepts the margin before it reaches AWS.

Winning this way costs gross margin, and management has stopped pretending otherwise. GAAP gross margin has fallen from 77.7% in the fourth quarter of 2024 to 71.8% in the second quarter of 2026, the developer products are described as 'not yet optimised for gross margin', and at the June Investor Day the long-term floor was cut from 75% to 70% while investors were redirected to a 44% 'unit economic margin' (lifetime revenue less cost to book and cost to serve) and to Microsoft's Intelligent Cloud, whose gross margin fell fourteen points between fiscal 2015 and 2025 as Azure grew from 4% to 71% of the mix while operating margin held at 42%. Microsoft's is a valid mechanism, since paid-traffic mix is neutral to operating margin and platform businesses do compress cost to book faster than gross margin erodes, and it is also precisely the reframe every company reaches for when its gross margin is going down; the test that separates the two is whether operating leverage arrives without growth deceleration, and on that the second quarter was mixed. Sales and marketing fell to 33% of revenue from 36% and research and development to 15% from 16%, while general and administrative rose to 11% from 10% against a long-term target of 3-5%; six to eight hundred basis points of the raised 30% operating-margin model sit in that one line, and it is the line most dependent on the restructuring discussed below.

## More than half the traffic is not human

Cloudflare's own data records the open web's business model failing before publishers' accounts do. The open web's share of online attention has fallen from 55% in 2015-22 to about 25%; search referrals to the top technology publications are down 58% since 2024; human traffic to retail, software and financial-services sites fell 35-40% between June 2025 and April 2026 while daily AI-agent requests on the network rose 1,700%; AI training now accounts for the majority of crawler requests, up from roughly a fifth in the spring of 2025; and the network serves two billion HTTP 402 'payment required' responses a day.

Chief executive Matthew Prince's line on the August call, that the internet's business model for the last 27 years 'has been largely defined by advertising and Google' and that the next 27 will be different, is the premise of Act IV, and the Investor Day put the strategic shift in one sentence: from protecting websites from bots to monetising trusted automated demand.

Everything Cloudflare has shipped toward that shift fits in one dated ledger, and the ledger's revenue column is empty.

| Date | Shipped | Status on 27 August 2026 |
|---|---|---|
| July 2025 | Default block of AI crawlers; pay-per-crawl (a charge per fetch) | Block live; pay-per-crawl retired after one year |
| September 2025 | NET Dollar stablecoin announced | Unshipped; 'available soon', partner talks with Coinbase and Zerohash |
| January 2026 | Human Native acquired (AI data-licensing marketplace) | Folded into the content-marketplace roadmap; no volumes disclosed |
| April 2026 | Agent Cloud (Dynamic Workers, Think, Artifacts, Sandboxes) | Live; no product-level revenue split |
| 9 June 2026 | Investor Day: 'Act I + Act III = Act IV'; Act IV placed outside the $238-384 billion headline TAM | Strategy and a raised long-term model |
| 1 July 2026 | Monetization Gateway (sites charge agents per request in stablecoins over x402); pay-per-use (publishers paid when their content appears in an answer) | Private beta; launch partners You.com and Ceramic.ai; both sides need Cloudflare accounts; broad availability 'later in 2026' |
| 1 August 2026 | Cloudflare Wallets (account and virtual wallets with spending caps); cloudflare.pay handles | Handle reservations open; funding, virtual-wallet issuance and on-ramps 'in the coming months' |
| 15 September 2026 | Default block of mixed-use (search plus training) crawlers on ad-supported pages | Pending; applies to new customers, new zones and every free customer |
| Every quarter since July 2025 | Disclosed Act IV revenue | None |

Three facts sit against the ledger. The first is Cloudflare's own one-year retrospective in July, which disclosed no revenue, no opt-in counts and no transaction volume and conceded that 'licensing today remains largely bespoke and unlikely to fully replace lost referral, advertising, and affiliate revenue'. The second is the rail: x402 is an open protocol governed at the Linux Foundation, whose founding members include Amazon Web Services, Coinbase, Circle, Google, Microsoft, Stripe, Visa and Mastercard alongside Cloudflare, and on 17 June AWS shipped the identical capability, a 'Monetize' action in CloudFront and WAF Bot Control that charges agents per request in USDC over x402, generally available at no charge beyond standard WAF pricing, two weeks before Cloudflare's own gateway left its waitlist. The third is scale: across every chain, facilitator and merchant on the protocol, x402 has settled about 160 million transactions worth roughly $41 million in its lifetime, an average of 26 cents (Agent Economy tracker, August 2026), against a company whose network processes thirty trillion transactions a day and whose market value is $101 billion.

What survives the commoditisation of the rail is the position, and the parts need naming. Investor Day listed six capabilities a functioning market in machine demand requires, and claimed all six; four of them are ordinary (analytics, value-discovery signals, workflow, a payment system) and two are scarce. 'Reliable scarcity', the ability to actually refuse an unpaid request, exists only for whoever fronts the origin, and Cloudflare fronts a fifth of the web with the bot-management classifier that sees each new crawler first; neutrality, the absence of a frontier model or a publishing business, is the property that AWS (which sells Bedrock), Google and Microsoft cannot claim, and it is why People Inc. went to Cloudflare for the block that brought AI laboratories to the negotiating table. The residual asset is therefore enforcement plus trust plus, if Wallets and the merchant-of-record role take, custody of the balances agents spend from, which is a real layer and also a layer that pays only if two things happen that have not yet happened: agent demand for open-web content has to keep routing through the public web, when direct licensing deals and platform-mediated indexes both bypass it, and per-answer pricing has to settle high enough that a fifth of the web's coverage compounds to billions, where a fraction of a cent per crawl compounds to a rounding error. Measured adoption says the timing is long: McKinsey's survey has agent deployment scaling at 10% or less in every business function, and Stanford HAI's 2026 index puts it in the single digits across nearly all of them.

## The enforcement position and its price

Installing a new layer on the open web means disciplining the largest crawler on it, and the largest crawler is also the layer through which the open web is currently monetised. Cloudflare's 15 September rule sorts every bot into Search, Training and Agent and applies the strictest applicable setting to any crawler that does more than one job; Googlebot, Bingbot and Applebot all crawl for search and for AI at once, so a publisher who blocks Training will by default also block Googlebot on any page carrying advertising, which for an ad-funded publisher is most pages. Google's answer is Google-Extended, a separate opt-out that removes a site from Gemini training without affecting Search inclusion, but content excluded through it still appears in AI Overviews, which are assembled from the search index that Google-Extended never touches, and that is exactly the bundling Cloudflare is trying to break. Its public argument, now carried to regulators in the United Kingdom and the European Union, is that remaining discoverable in search effectively requires consenting to AI use, giving Google access to roughly twice the content available to rival laboratories and, by Cloudflare's own count, giving the largest crawler up to a hundred times the access of the smallest. Whatever makes scarcity reliable also threatens its publishers' search visibility, and the cost of that lands on the publishers, not on Cloudflare, so the observable over the next two quarters is whether ad-funded sites keep the new defaults or quietly re-enable mixed crawlers, because a default that customers opt out of is not scarcity. A second leak runs through agentic browsers: OpenAI's Atlas and Perplexity's Comet ride Chromium and present as human Chrome sessions, and the public dispute with Perplexity over rotated user agents and ignored no-crawl signals showed the classifier chasing a moving target. Layers that everything above them must traverse eventually meet a political ceiling, and Cloudflare is meeting Google's before it has collected a dollar.

## The organisation, graded against our own framework

Our companion essay on culture names Cloudflare among the vanguard of companies whose pipeline of optionalities the market prices at zero, and this stock is the case that tests the framework's two gates in opposite directions. The first gate, an industry feed of mispriced optionalities, passes without argument: edge security, zero trust, developer compute and now machine commerce arrive at the same network continuously. Recognition and execution, the organisational conditions, also grade well on dated evidence. Recognition is the 2017 isolate decision, made seven years before the workload it suits existed, and the speed with which the company registered the open web breaking and reorganised its entire narrative around it inside twelve months; the retirement of pay-per-crawl after one year and its relaunch as pay-per-use reads to short sellers as a failed product and to us as both a failed product and an organisation that recorded the failure within four quarters, which is faster than most incumbents record anything. Execution is the ledger above (default block to gateway to wallets to handles in thirteen months) and, more tellingly, what the company did to itself: since May every employee has worked inside Cloudflare OS, an agent workspace built on Workers in which agents receive scoped capability objects from a 'Gatekeeper Worker' and never a credential, and on 5 August the whole system was open-sourced under Apache 2.0; the internal proof points (a Next.js reimplementation built by one engineer in under a week for about $1,100 of tokens; 131,246 AI code reviews across 48,095 merge requests in thirty days at a median cost of 98 cents) are the specific, quantified disclosures the framework asks for and most management teams cannot produce.

Revealed behaviour, the tier we weight highest, is where the grade gets complicated, and every datapoint below carries a date so that none of it can be back-fitted to the chart. Founder control is intact: co-founders Matthew Prince and Michelle Zatlyn hold 50.3% of the vote (June 2026 proxy), and a June recapitalisation preserved that control while unlocking liquidity. Named executives receive no annual cash bonus, but the founder performance awards vest on share-price hurdles between $156 and $579, with no Workers, R2 or Cloudflare One volume anywhere in the design, so the founders are paid for the multiple and not for the pipeline, and shareholder support for the pay design fell to 76% this year from 89%. Insider flow runs one way: no open-market purchases against 75 sales by the chief executive over five years, roughly $55 million sold under a plan between 22 June and 6 July at $212-247, further planned sales on 4-5 August at $288-296 before the print, and the chief financial officer and chief legal officer selling on 17-18 August at $305-315, all under pre-arranged plans, all at prices far below the $497 at which the company's own creditors will convert. The datapoint that resists a clean reading is the May cut. Headcount rose from 3,682 at the end of 2023 to 5,156 at the end of 2025, crossing the level at which our framework expects a matrix organisation to ossify, and on 7 May, alongside a record quarter, the company removed about 1,100 roles, a fifth of its staff, in a memo describing the change as a move to an agentic-AI-first operating model and explicitly not an assessment of individual performance; headcount was 4,700 at the end of June with the programme completing in the third quarter, and the stock fell 24% the next day. A cut of that size at a record quarter reads to us as entropy arriving, with only the calibration capable of showing otherwise, and it has not been shown; what is observable instead is reliability, the operational variable that a fifth of the web's traffic makes systemic: a roughly six-hour global outage on 18 November 2025 from a corrupted bot-management feature file, further multi-hour global incidents in February and April, a fibre cut in June, and thirteen separate status-page incidents between 7 and 14 August (R2 write failures in the eastern United States, Workers KV, Workers AI, Durable Objects, Magic Transit, the MCP Server Portal, a Spamhaus listing that disrupted outbound email), none individually large and, as a cluster, the datapoint we read against an organisation a fifth smaller and shipping faster than it ever has. Glassdoor's rating sits at 3.2-3.3 with 46% chief-executive approval as of August, a workforce-exhaust signal we weight lightly and directionally.

On our own rubric the organisation grades as a strong converter of optionality with an open question on calibration, which would ordinarily raise conviction on duration. It does not here, because the second gate fails: capital markets psychosis, the market's habit of valuing a company's pipeline at zero or below, is absent in Cloudflare's case and runs the other way. Act IV is the investor-relations narrative, it is the reason the stock re-rated from $183 in April to $331 in August, and our own rule is that where the pipeline is explicit in the multiple the lens sizes and does not select. At fifteen times revenue the same organisation would be a core position; at thirty-five it is a medium one.

## Security: a credible fourth, priced like a first

Zero trust is the part of Cloudflare most often described as a share-gainer and least often measured. It is the fourth vendor in a four-vendor market, and the gap to the top three is structural, so no cycle closes it.

| | Zscaler | Palo Alto (Prisma SASE) | Netskope | Cloudflare One |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| SASE ARR (estimated) | ~$3.4B | ~$1.5B | ~$0.85B | $0.33-0.54B |
| Growth | ~25% | ~40% (platform bundling) | ~29% | ~40-50% |
| Enterprise SASE customers | ~4,000+ | ~6,300 platform | ~2,500 | ~400 |
| 2026 Gartner SSE Magic Quadrant | Leader | Leader | Leader | Niche Player |
| DLP / CASB depth | Deep (1,500+ classifiers) | Deep, CyberArk-augmented | Best in class | ~30-50 classifiers, text-only, no endpoint DLP; in-line-only CASB |
| Full-bundle list price per user per month | $35-50 | $50-80 | $15-22 | $20-35 (self-serve from $7) |

The pricing gap is real margin and not a promotion, because single-pass inspection on an anycast network costs roughly 40-50% less per protected user to serve than a regional-PoP architecture, and the performance claims (46% faster zero-trust access than Zscaler, 58% faster secure web gateway, both self-reported) are consistent with the physics. Neither fact clears the procurement filter: Gartner Leader status and depth in data-loss prevention and cloud-access security are gating criteria in roughly 60-70% of Fortune 500 SASE evaluations, where a missed data-loss incident is a chief information security officer's personal career risk and price is not the variable, and the 2026 Magic Quadrant moved Palo Alto into the Leaders' box while leaving Cloudflare a Niche Player. Zscaler's AI-security ARR alone, heading past $500 million this fiscal year, is roughly the size of Cloudflare's entire SASE business, and the flagship win of the first quarter, a $5.1 million five-year contract with a Fortune 500 insurer replacing six vendors, is real and an order of magnitude below the eight-figure ZFlex deals Zscaler now reports.

Our own sizing puts Cloudflare's addressable share at 10-13% of a $60-70 billion 2030 SASE market, $6-9 billion of ARR if it sustains 40-50% growth for four more years, which is a large business and a capped one. Two things could move the cap. FedRAMP High, achieved on 12 August with GovRAMP Moderate and a stated intent to pursue DoD IL4, is a regulatory fixed cost that opens civilian High-impact federal workloads to the whole platform, and it buys a different customer cohort with a different retention curve, not a feature catch-up. And the longer-run argument, which we have not yet promoted from hypothesis to view, is that in an era of autonomous attackers the durable layer of security is inline enforcement and the commoditising layer is classification and reporting, in which case the classifier gap that keeps Cloudflare out of the Leaders' box is the side of the stack that models erode and the anycast inline path is the side they cannot.

## What the price requires

Investor Day set the targets the market now discounts: $5 billion of annualised revenue before the end of 2028 (from $2.4 billion at the end of 2025, a 28% compound rate), operating margin of 30% or more against a prior 20%, a free cash flow margin of 30-35% against a prior 25%, GAAP profitability by 2028, and a 'Rule of 50' in 2027-28. Reverse-engineering $101 billion from this year's $2.87 billion guide over a seven-year horizon, and ignoring the near-zero net cash and the dilution in the convertible stack, produces the following:

| Discount rate | Terminal multiple of 2033 free cash flow | 2033 free cash flow required | 2033 revenue at a 32% FCF margin | Implied 2026-33 revenue CAGR |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 8% | 40x | ~$4.3B | ~$13.5B | ~25% |
| 9% | 35x | ~$5.3B | ~$16.5B | ~28% |
| 10% | 30x | ~$6.6B | ~$20.5B | ~32% |

Every row requires the Investor Day model to be delivered in full and then sustained for four further years, with the free cash flow margin roughly doubling from the trailing 15% (8% in the second quarter on $99 million of restructuring cash, about 22% without it) and the terminal multiple still at 30-40 times cash flow at the end of the period; management's own $5 billion target covers only the first three years of the path. Few $3 billion software companies have held 25-32% growth for seven years while doubling a cash margin, and the two operating variables the price is most sensitive to, net retention and Act IV disclosure, are exactly the two on which the evidence is youngest: retention has recovered to 120% from 114% a year ago, and the fourth act has no revenue line. Cloudflare's own capital structure shows where it struck the trade. On 10 August it borrowed $2.5 billion at a 0% coupon, convertible at $497 (a 60% premium to that day's close), with capped calls extending the protection to $854, taking the convertible stack to $5.6 billion against about $6.4 billion of pro forma cash, of which only $2 billion counts toward the leverage covenant; creditors are lending at zero for the fourth act, and the executives converting stock to cash are doing so at $288-315.

| What the price pays for | What exists on 27 August 2026 |
|---|---|
| Act IV as a multi-billion-dollar revenue line by the early 2030s | A private beta with two launch partners, a wallet without funding rails, no disclosed revenue; $41 million of lifetime settlement across the whole x402 protocol |
| A proprietary agent-payment layer | An open Linux Foundation standard that AWS ships free inside CloudFront; the proprietary residual is enforcement and neutrality |
| A 30%+ operating margin from an AI-first cost base | 13.8% non-GAAP in the second quarter; G&A at 11% of revenue against a 3-5% target; leverage visible in sales and R&D only |
| Gross margin stabilising at the 70% floor | 71.8% GAAP, down 590 basis points in six quarters, developer mix 'not yet optimised' |
| Enterprise SASE share gains | Structural fourth, Gartner Niche Player, ~400 enterprise SASE customers, a 10-13% share ceiling |
| Reliability befitting a fifth of the web | Three multi-hour global outages in five months and thirteen incidents in eight days this August |
| 25-32% growth for seven years | 36% today and reaccelerating, 120% net retention, RPO growing faster than revenue |

That last row carries the position; the six above it set its size.

## The layer and the price

We hold Cloudflare for the fact that does not need the fourth act: a network in the path of a fifth of the web that now carries more machine traffic than human, an architecture that suits the next workload better than the incumbents' and that they cannot copy without breaking their own economics, a developer funnel converting to enterprise revenue at an accelerating rate, and an organisation that ships and reorganises itself faster than the incumbents it competes with. We hold it at a medium weight for the fact that the price does need it: the market has priced the monetisation of machine demand as delivered, when the honest description on 27 August is a private beta with two partners, an open standard the largest cloud gives away, a wallet without funding rails, and a rule taking effect on 15 September that risks its own publishers' search traffic in order to make scarcity real. Our framework puts Cloudflare among the best optionality-capturing organisations we know, and the same framework refuses to pay for that optionality twice; conviction rises on a revenue line or on a price that reopens the gap, and until one of them arrives the second gate sets the size.
