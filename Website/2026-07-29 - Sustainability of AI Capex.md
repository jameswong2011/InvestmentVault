# Part One: AI Capex Appears Stretched Relative to Adoption Trends

**The 2026 AI build is spending roughly $850–900 billion a year on infrastructure against just $150–180 billion of annualized revenue from actual end buyers — a five-to-six-times gap between the capital going in and the money coming out. Measured today, the $1.0–1.2 trillion of AI-specific capital invested through end-2026 earns a pre-tax return of about 5–7%, below its 8–10% cost of capital — and the depreciation from the current spending wave has not even reached the income statement yet.** None of this means AI demand is false. It means that three things the market currently prices as one — workload growth, revenue capture, and returns for the asset owners — are in fact three different variables.

**That 5–7% deserves an immediate caveat: it is a snapshot built on a backward-looking method — today's profit divided by capital invested to date — and it is not the number anyone at the table is actually underwriting.** The true long-run return on this build is impossible to estimate today, and it will not land anywhere near its average. It will follow a power law: exceptionally high for the few winners who turn compute into durable revenue, deeply negative for the losers left holding stranded or refinanced capacity — with the capital-weighted average still sitting meaningfully above the cost of capital. That asymmetry is the engine of the whole cycle. Every company with a seat at the table is racing to capture AI-winner economics, and the race is rational even while the measured return on the aggregate is not. So treat the 5–7% as a clock rather than a verdict: it tells you how much monetization has to arrive before the depreciation wave lands, and what it costs to own the wrong assets.

**The base case is digestion, not crash: capex keeps growing into 2028, then runs flat to down through 2029 as depreciation and financing discipline expose the weak spenders, before growth resumes from 2030.** Long-run demand is healthy — the pool of corporate work that can be automated is enormous, token volume keeps compounding, and the physical constraints on supply are real. But between that healthy endgame and today sits a pullback. Its severity — set by how much of the build is funded with credit, how fast enterprises deepen their adoption, and whether the 2028 depreciation wave finds revenue to absorb it — is the single most important variable for AI-exposed equities over the next several years. And the digestion, when it arrives, is the entry point: the moment the market reprices every AI asset on the old cyclical template, including businesses protected by qualification gates — the years-long certification customers require before they can switch suppliers — whose trough earnings no longer behave cyclically at all.

The essay runs in three parts. Part One builds the case that the current investment pace is stretched relative to adoption: where the money goes, what it earns, how fast depreciation arrives, and how deep consumer and enterprise demand actually run. Part Two builds the opposite case — that winner-take-most economics push every player to spend to its financing limit, deferring any digestion until that limit is reached. Part Three prices both paths.

## One label, four cycles

"AI capex" is four different economic projects sharing one name, and they will not correct together.

Roughly 30–35% of the 2026 spending is the big cloud platforms — Microsoft, Alphabet, Amazon and Meta, the "Big Four" hyperscalers — defending their own franchises and running internal workloads: protecting search, advertising, cloud and engineering productivity. This slice is funded from operating cash flow and will be cut last. Another quarter is merchant enterprise cloud: capacity sold to a proven pool of business customers, mostly cash-funded, exposed to how deep adoption goes rather than to anyone's solvency. The third slice, 25–30%, is capacity built to serve the frontier labs. It is financed with debt, special-purpose vehicles (SPVs), customer prepayments and vendor credit, and it carries the real risk — counterparty, utilization and resale value. The final 10–15% is labs and sovereigns building for themselves, running on story-priced equity or state capital. That money freezes first when funding conditions turn.

Add it up and 55–60% of the build sits on cash flow from some of the best business franchises ever assembled, which is why a telecom-style systemic collapse is unlikely. The cyclically fragile share is roughly 30–35% — the lab-serving slice plus the equity-funded part of the self-build, since state-funded projects answer to politics rather than financing — and that is where the correction will concentrate.

The funding mix is already deteriorating at the margin. Alphabet spent $44.9 billion on capex in Q2 2026 against $39.1 billion of quarterly operating cash flow. Oracle spent 174% of its operating cash flow, leaving free cash flow near negative $24 billion. Meta paused buybacks in Q1 2026, making no repurchases while keeping its authorization in place. Amazon's build consumed 95% of its operating cash flow before leases. In aggregate, hyperscaler capex is on track to cross above operating cash flow in Q3 2026. History is specific about what that crossover means: the railway, electrification, telecom and shale booms all became far less tolerant of demand disappointment within roughly two years of the marginal dollar shifting from internal cash to borrowed money. Today's integrated hyperscalers are better businesses than the median historical builder — but that protection covers the cash-funded slices, not every contract they sponsor. The successful counter-example proves the same rule rather than breaking it: the 2015–19 cloud build also looked overcapitalized before delayed utilization paid off spectacularly — but that build never crossed above operating cash flow, and it migrated workloads enterprises had already budgeted for. The happy precedent covers the cash-funded slices, not the credit-funded ones.

The frontier-lab commitments sharpen the point. OpenAI has announced roughly $1.4 trillion of commitments across Broadcom, Oracle, Microsoft, Nvidia, AMD, AWS and CoreWeave — against $25–30 billion of annualized revenue and a projected 2027 cash burn near $63 billion. Parts of that $1.4 trillion are non-binding or overlapping, so it is not near-term capex. The real problem is the circularity: suppliers and infrastructure owners are financing the very customer whose commitments support their own revenue. Backlog protects volume only for as long as the counterparty can keep raising money.

## The return on the money already spent

Reported capex at Microsoft, Alphabet, Amazon, Meta and Oracle compounded at 32% a year from 2020 to 2025 — from $94.5 billion to $378.7 billion — and the 2026 plans roughly double it again to $785–830 billion, with Microsoft near $190 billion and Alphabet and Amazon around $200 billion each. Add CoreWeave's $31–35 billion, the other pure-play GPU rental clouds (the "neoclouds"), the Chinese platforms and the sovereign projects — and strip out contracts already financed on a hyperscaler's balance sheet — and you arrive at the $850–900 billion ecosystem total.

What does that capital earn? No hyperscaler reports a datacenter return on invested capital, and the omission is structural rather than evasive: the same facilities simultaneously run rented cloud capacity, first-party applications, ad-targeting improvements, internal cost savings and strategic reserve capacity. Segment profit is the wrong numerator, and reported plant and equipment is the wrong denominator. The honest alternative is a set of bounded proxies, and they agree on three things.

First, the installed base was good capital allocation. Microsoft's Intelligent Cloud generates $44.6 billion of operating income — a 21.8% pre-tax return signal against its property base — and AWS earns $45.6 billion on $128.7 billion of revenue. Second, the platforms hide both the best and the worst returns. Meta's ad machine runs at a 58% signal, which proves Meta can afford AI — not that AI is earning the return. Alphabet's direct cloud signal is only 5.6%, because the real payoff — defending search — sits outside the Cloud segment entirely. Third, and decisive: a profitable installed base does not validate the new spending. Each year's investment is its own cohort, and the 2026 cohort is three times larger, far heavier in accelerators, and entering a market with more suppliers and falling prices.

The aggregate is harsher than the segments. Attributable AI profit today is roughly $50–70 billion — a proxy built from gross margins, before allocated overhead and tax, so the true operating figure sits lower still. The frontier labs sit near breakeven as a group, while hyperscaler AI cloud and software revenue of $100–120 billion earns 50–60% gross margins. Set that against the $1.0–1.2 trillion of capital committed through end-2026 and you get the 5–7% pre-tax figure — below the cost of capital even before the proxy's flattery is stripped out. But the spread around that average matters more than the average itself. At the toll layer — the suppliers everyone must pay to build at all — qualification-protected silicon, memory and networking vendors earn returns of 35–60% and up. At the other end, merchant GPU rental earns mid-teens gross returns before debt, and those returns turn into negative equity cash flow once the debt is layered on: CoreWeave's interest expense reached 25.8% of its revenue. The lesson is simple. A qualified chokepoint can tax the whole stack. A financed building full of interchangeable accelerators is just capacity, not a monopoly.

The company-by-company picture compresses to this. Microsoft has the strongest direct bridge from spending to revenue — more than 20 million paid Copilot seats and Azure above $75 billion — but its $190 billion of 2026 capex is triple its FY2025 cash capex, so Copilot has to become a second real consumption engine rather than a bundled feature. Alphabet is the hardest to measure: defending search economics is rational, but defending a legacy profit pool is not the same thing as earning new AI profit. Amazon has proven cloud economics and the least cash cushion of the integrated three at today's pace. Meta has the strongest indirect economics and the weakest proof of causation — and its decision to extend server lives to 5.5 years added $2.59 billion to 2025 net income at exactly the moment hardware is turning over faster. Oracle carries the clearest risk of destroying equity value: revenue contracted on borrowed money still loses money if margins, customer credit or resale values disappoint.

## The depreciation clock

Depreciation is the forcing function for the whole thesis, because it converts a debate about capex into a dated earnings event. The Big Four's depreciation and amortization (D&A) is already running near $170 billion a year and growing 30–40%. The roughly $1.09 trillion spent across 2025–26 depreciates at a blended rate of about 13% — reflecting five-to-six-year lives on the IT equipment that makes up about 60% of it — which adds another $140 billion of annual charges as the equipment enters service. By 2028, Big Four D&A plausibly reaches $260–300 billion. At a 50% incremental margin before the new depreciation charges, offsetting that step-up — measured against the ~$110–120 billion D&A base of 2025, before the wave — requires roughly $300–360 billion of new AI revenue by 2028. That race, not user counts or token charts, is the one expectations variable the market is under-testing.

Accounting choices can defer perhaps $40–60 billion of annual depreciation by stretching useful lives, but they cannot outrun the wave. The economic question underneath is whether older chips keep earning once each new generation lands. The mid-2026 evidence cuts both ways: four-year-old H100s still rent for $2.29–3.12 per hour and resell for $12–22K, while Amazon shortened some AI-server lives to five years. The variable to watch is revenue per watt as fleets migrate down the price curve, not benchmark leadership. Impairment arrives when rental prices fall faster than operating costs and the hardware can no longer stay busy.

## The consumer engine: fast, shallow, capped

Consumer adoption is the fastest-growing engine in the stack and the shallowest pool of revenue. OpenAI reports more than 900 million weekly users but only 50 million paying subscribers — a 5–6% conversion rate. Free usage still has value: it builds habit, feeds advertising and keeps the infrastructure busy. But it is not subscription revenue, and it should not be counted as if it were.

The pricing evidence caps the mass market. The market has settled into an $8–20 monthly band: ChatGPT added an $8 tier and began testing ads, while Gemini cut its top consumer plan from $250 to $100. One survey of what users would need to be paid to give up AI for a month found a median of just $11.40 against a mean of $124.50 — and what people would actually pay runs below what they demand to give something up — so a thin tail of power users accounts for most of the value. AI apps keep roughly 21% of their users at twelve months, versus 31% for non-AI apps, and only a small minority of payers hold more than one subscription.

The prosumer story is a power law, not a wave. AI has collapsed the cost of producing software, websites and media; it has not collapsed the cost of attention, distribution, trust or customer acquisition. A realistic planning distribution for a thousand vibe-coded projects: 650 never get published, 230 get published and earn under $100, and five clear $100,000 — with those five funding most of the tool spending. Pricing power lives where the tool owns deployment, payments or a live production workflow. Pure generation has almost none.

Add it up — subscriptions from 2.5–3 billion weekly users converting at 6–8%, plus creation tools and advertising — and consumer plus prosumer revenue reaches $85–135 billion by 2031, growing 17–22% a year from today's $40–50 billion. That makes it the slowest of the major AI demand pools, because conversion, churn and price deflation all pull in the same direction. One asymmetry survives the cap: capped consumer *revenue* does not cap consumer *compute*. Free tiers, ads and always-on agents can multiply tokens per user by 10–100 times, with advertising economics footing the bill. That concentrates the sustainable consumer-serving capex with the companies that own distribution and ad monetization — and strands the merchant capacity built on the hope that token volume implies a revenue pool.

## The enterprise engine: deep, slow, binding

Corporate demand is the opposite shape: the ceiling is enormous and the clock is organizational. Today's corporate run-rate of roughly $90–120 billion is growing more than 100% year over year, and the official statistics describe adoption that is broad but shallow. The US Census finds 18% of firms using AI — 32% when weighted by employment — and 57% of adopters use it in three or fewer business functions. The EU reads 20%, the UK 16%, and only 7% of UK adopters run anything agentic — AI that acts on its own rather than just answering. "Any use" measures access. Capex returns need something stronger: paid, repeated, production use with enough autonomy to consume tokens at scale.

What stands between access and depth is not model capability — that is no longer the typical bottleneck. It is a funnel of organizational gates: documented processes instead of "ask Sarah" workflows; data estates where customer, permission and transaction records actually agree; explicit rules about what an agent may approve, spend or disclose; governance, audit and liability plumbing; and simple change capacity in firms already saturated with other programs. Sixty percent of enterprises say they cannot adapt their operations as fast as the technology arrives, and agents are scaled in fewer than one function in ten. The tooling gates — identity, audit, runtime governance — resolve over 2027–29 procurement cycles. Process redesign for the median firm resolves closer to 2032.

This is the central asymmetry of the whole cycle: **supply gets installed on financing clocks measured in quarters, while demand matures on organizational clocks measured in years — and no supplier can spend its way through a customer's unclear decision rights.** The productivity evidence follows the same funnel. Customer-support agents improved 14% in a randomized NBER study, with the biggest gains for novices. METR found experienced developers were 19% *slower* with AI on familiar codebases — while believing they were faster — though its later update points toward speedups, with heavy selection-effect caveats. AI raises the return on novice work and on bounded, measurable digital tasks; it does not yet substitute for expert judgment. And the graveyard of failed pilots coexists with exploding token volume for a simple reason: the successful deployments scale while the median pilot dies.

Government demand is real capacity but poor proof. Federal AI use cases nearly doubled in a year and generative deployments grew ninefold — but 94 separate government-wide AI requirements, plus accreditation and appropriations, put revenue years behind the announcements. Call it $20–40 billion of application revenue by 2031. Sovereign infrastructure buying adds to capex demand, but a buyer who may rationally accept sub-commercial returns proves nothing about private-sector economics.

The conservative 2031 ledger: $250–400 billion of corporate AI software, agents and APIs; $20–40 billion of government applications; $85–135 billion of consumer — **$355–575 billion of direct end demand, counted once.** A broader bridge — one that also counts enterprises buying cloud directly, advertising uplift and internal cost savings, without ever double-counting the same dollar as lab, cloud and chip revenue — reaches roughly $850 billion by 2030. Getting there demands growth of 80% decelerating to 35%, which is achievable. What it does not support is the consensus glide to $1.4 trillion of capex in 2028. Mature cloud businesses spend 35–45% of revenue on capex, so $850 billion of annual spending ultimately needs a $2.0–2.5 trillion revenue pool behind it. Something has to yield — and the first thing to yield is the growth rate of the build.

## Constraints to adoption that clear in the medium term, and hard gates that don't

Sorting the binding variables by how fast they move is what separates this analysis from a generic bubble call.

The short-term constraints are physical and financial, and they sit on the supply side. Power is the visible one: US datacenter capacity can grow from 76GW to 134GW by 2030, but turbine slots, transformers and grid-connection queues set the pace — CoreWeave has energized only about 29% of its 3.5GW of contracted power, and Nebius just 5–6%. Component inflation added $25 billion to Microsoft's capex alone. High-bandwidth memory, advanced packaging and networking all remain qualification-bound. These constraints resolve on supplier timescales of two to four years, and while they last they *protect* pricing for whoever owns the bottleneck. They cap the bull case — with global additions running roughly twice the US pace, at $45–55 billion per delivered gigawatt, worldwide physical delivery tops out near $1.5–2.0 trillion a year by 2028–29 — but they do not cap the base case. Financing is the fastest-moving constraint of all: it can shut down the fragile slices within quarters.

The hard gates sit on the demand side, and capital cannot clear them: organizational redesign, machine-readable context, decision rights, liability, measurement and change capacity in the enterprise; conversion, churn and free-substitute anchoring in the consumer market; procurement in government. These mature on five-to-ten-year clocks that do not care how many gigawatts get energized. Falling inference prices genuinely expand the pool of work worth automating — the classic Jevons effect, where cheaper computing enlarges its own market — but flat-rate subscriptions and free tiers hand those gains straight to users. Only consumption pricing, advertising and outcome fees capture them. Volume growth has to beat price deflation *on surfaces that can actually charge*.

The timing conclusion falls out directly. Supply constraints clear faster than demand gates open. The build's constraints ease into 2028 — exactly as the depreciation wave lands, and before organizational depth arrives. That scissors, not any failure of AI itself, is what produces the digestion.

## The tests that settle it

The thesis is falsifiable in both directions, on a calendar.

1. **The 2028 depreciation race.** Big Four D&A of $260–300 billion against the $300–360 billion of new AI revenue needed to offset it. The base case breaks downward if, by end-2028, new AI revenue and realized savings sit below $180 billion while fleet utilization falls under 65%. No backlog or token chart rescues that outcome.
2. **The January–February 2027 guidance window.** The first sub-20% capex guide means digestion is on schedule; uniform 30%-plus guides mean spending is being pushed to the financing limit — the scenario Part Two develops. Neocloud refinancings and frontier-lab fundraises through 2027 are the same branch point expressed in credit markets.
3. **Utilization and rental pricing.** Above 70% on revenue-ready fleets keeps the base case alive. Two quarters below 60–65%, or a break in H100/B200 rental prices, means the correction has arrived ahead of either schedule. Sustained utilization above 80% despite all the new supply falsifies the pause entirely.
4. **Enterprise depth, not enterprise use.** The median adopter moving beyond three functions — with audited agents writing into live production systems before capex slows — is what would falsify the digestion from the bull side. "Any use" rising while depth stalls confirms it.
5. **Token economics.** Volume above roughly 2x year over year, with provider gross profit compounding through falling prices, proves the Jevons elasticity is being captured. A quarter below 2x while unit costs keep falling breaks the demand floor.

# Part Two: The Alternative Universe is a Race to the Financing Ceiling

Every bearish claim above has one counterweight strong enough to equalise it, and it comes from the return distribution itself. If terminal returns follow a power law, the rational strategy for every player with a seat at the table is to spend to its financing limit. The participant that under-invests is close to guaranteed exclusion from the winner set; the one that over-invests risks bad-but-survivable returns on the marginal spending — for a franchise owner, at least, since the deeply negative outcomes concentrate in the leveraged vehicles. Nobody improves their expected outcome by unilaterally slowing down while rivals continue. The equilibrium is therefore capitulation by exhaustion, not capitulation by discipline: the race stops when a party cannot fund the next round, not when measured returns disappoint.

How far can the race run? Convert the question into a leverage ledger — every debt-like claim, on and off balance sheet: reported debt, finance and operating leases, SPV structures, vendor financing, customer prepayments — measured against the cash operating earnings (EBITDA) of the complex. Figures are order-of-magnitude estimates:

| Segment | Debt-like claims | Current leverage | Ceiling before credit stress | Capacity drawn |
|---|---|---|---|---:|
| Big Four platforms | $400–500B gross incl. leases; ~$100–200B net of cash | ~0.1–0.3x net (~0.6x gross) on ~$700–750B EBITDA | 2.0–2.5x net for A-rated franchise cash flows | **~5–15% net** |
| Oracle | ~$110–130B and rising | ~3.5x | ~4.5x (investment-grade cusp) | **~75–85%** |
| Neoclouds | ~$60–100B, asset-backed and prepay-secured | Interest already 25.8% of revenue at CoreWeave | Interest ~20–25% of revenue — where every historical marginal builder broke | **~90–100%** |
| Labs / sovereign | ~$1.4T of commitments vs $25–30B revenue (OpenAI) | Not meaningful — equity- and commitment-funded | No historical builder survived commitments at ~50x revenue | **Narrative-priced** |
| **Complex blended** | **~$0.8–1.1T** incl. ~$200–350B of complex-level SPV/vendor/prepay structures | **~1.0–1.3x on ~$800–850B EBITDA** | **~3–4x historical terminal tolerance** | **~30–35%** |

**Roughly a third of maximum leverage capacity is consumed — and two-thirds of the unused portion sits on the four platform balance sheets, the owners least likely to blink.** The fragile slices have already spent their rope; the strong ones have barely started drawing theirs. One caution on the blended row: it is a scale gauge against history, not a claim that capital moves freely between entities — the segmentation is the point, because nobody here cross-guarantees anyone else.

History says booms like this stop at tripwires, not at a particular debt number. The canal mania was funded with equity only: a third of projects never paid a dividend, and the bust was a repricing rather than a credit crisis. The railway mania put 5–7% of UK GDP into track at the 1847 peak and broke when capital calls came due. By 1893, America's bond-financed railroads carried fixed charges above earnings on a third of the network; 30% of the mileage went through receivership, and Morganization — J.P. Morgan's consolidation of the bankrupt roads — moved the assets onto strong balance sheets. The 1920s utility pyramids ran roughly 8–10x effective leverage hidden across holding-company layers, until the refinancing failed. Telecom raised about $1.6 trillion, grew to a quarter to a third of the entire high-yield bond market, spent above 100% of operating cash flow for roughly three years, and broke where the marginal builders' interest bills crossed 20–25% of revenue — capex halved even though traffic never stopped growing. Three tripwires generalize from that history: the marginal builder's interest bill crossing roughly 20–25% of revenue; the boom's paper saturating the credit market that funds it; and the whole complex spending above operating cash flow for two to three consecutive years. Where does AI sit against them? The first is already breached at the neoclouds. The second approaches as $200–300 billion of annual issuance heads toward 15–20%-plus of investment-grade supply by 2028. The third's clock starts at the projected Q3 2026 crossover — and two to three years from there lands on 2028–29, the same date the depreciation wave arrives. For scale: at $850–900 billion the build is about 0.8% of world output and — because the cash flows funding it are overwhelmingly American — about 3% of US GDP; a $2 trillion run-rate by 2030 approaches railway-mania intensity.

Run the drawdown to its maximum — suspend distributions, meaning buybacks and dividends, and draw every balance sheet to its ceiling — and the bull case prices itself:

| Funding source, cumulative 2027–2030 | Capacity |
|---|---:|
| Retained operating cash flow (distributions suspended, incl. Oracle; before non-AI maintenance capex) | ~$3.0–3.4T |
| Platform issuance to 2.0–2.5x on ~$1.1T of 2030 EBITDA | ~$1.8–2.3T |
| Oracle + neocloud secured, asset-backed and prepay structures (net of prepayments already inside operating cash flow) | ~$0.3–0.5T |
| Lab + sovereign equity | ~$0.5–0.8T |
| **Maximum-drawdown stack** | **~$5.6–7.0T** |

That stack funds **$1.2–1.3 trillion in 2027, $1.5–1.7 trillion in 2028 — above the $1.4 trillion consensus glide that the demand math alone cannot justify — $1.8–2.0 trillion in 2029 and $2.0–2.2 trillion in 2030**, at which point the power ceiling binds before the financial one and the stack runs out — the top of the path consumes the top of the stack, which is what maximum drawdown means. Financing therefore cannot falsify the bull case before 2029; only demand can, or a tripwire at the fragile slices. The platforms exit at 2.0–2.5x leverage with interest near 10–12% of EBITDA — they never hit the historical break lines. The break lines live at the neoclouds, the labs and the credit market's appetite.

Extend the clock to 2035 and the maximum-leverage point dates itself. The stack draws down through 2030, and the final borrowing completes in **2031**, funded by that year's cash flow plus the last of the leverage capacity — platforms at their 2.0–2.5x ceiling, fragile slices long since capped. Beyond that point the constraint inverts: with no net new borrowing available, capex falls back to whatever internal funding supports, and leverage deflates passively at roughly 0.2–0.3x per year as earnings compound — or painfully, through cuts, if demand has not arrived:

| Race path | AI capex | Platform net leverage vs 2.0–2.5x ceiling | Financing state |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| 2027 | $1.20–1.30T | ~0.4–0.6x (≈20% drawn) | Distributions suspended; issuance begins |
| 2028 | $1.50–1.70T | ~0.9–1.0x (≈40%) | Above the $1.4T consensus; AI paper 15–20%+ of investment-grade supply |
| 2029 | $1.80–2.00T | ~1.4–1.5x (≈60%) | Credit-market appetite becomes the swing variable |
| 2030 | $2.00–2.20T | ~1.9–2.0x (≈85%) | Power-bound; stack near exhaustion |
| **2031 — maximum leverage** | ~$2.0–2.2T | **~2.0–2.5x (fully drawn)** | Final draw completes; capex capped at internal funding |
| 2032–33 | $1.4–1.9T | ~1.8–2.1x, deflating | Plateau-to-cut: EBITDA growth deleverages if demand arrived; forced cuts if it has not |
| 2034–35 | $1.9–2.6T | ~1.5–1.7x | Headroom reopens; a second borrowing leg becomes possible |

The base-path comparison closes the loop. Digestion's organic re-acceleration compounds to roughly $1.8–2.2 trillion by 2035, so the two scenarios converge in the low-$2 trillion range mid-decade. The race pulls roughly $3 trillion of spending forward into 2027–2031 and pays for it with a harder 2031–33; the base defers that spending and pays with the 2028–29 pause. Both paths are built on the same terminal demand, so their convergence is by construction — the analytical content is the route: date, depth, and who gets carried out along the way.

Taken seriously, then, there is a strong possibility of no meaningful digestion on the 2028 schedule at all. The remaining stack supports continued expansion with no requirement that enterprise depth arrive first — and the tell is already legible: buybacks paused to fund capex, credit issuance rising into flat measured returns, commitments growing faster than the revenue behind them. Read January–February 2027 through this lens: uniform 30%-plus guides mean the race is being run to the limit, and the pullback moves out until financing capacity — not conviction — gives way. The caveat is symmetrical. A correction deferred to the 2030–31 ceiling arrives on a complex at 85–100% of its leverage capacity: telecom-shaped, an air pocket rather than a digestion, with stranded assets migrating at cents on the dollar to the strongest owners — which is where every prior boom's consolidation returns were made. The mechanism underwrites near-term capex volume. What it moves is the date and the sharpness of the eventual test — not the toll-layer positioning that survives either version.

# Part Three: Scenarios for AI Capex until 2030

Big Four operating cash flow of roughly $600 billion in 2026 grows to perhaps $780 billion by 2028. With distributions reduced, that funds $600–650 billion of internal capex; add a realistic $200–300 billion of annual debt, SPV and lab-equity capacity, and the no-stress financing ceiling sits near $900 billion–1.0 trillion. The race scenario deliberately breaks that ceiling — distributions suspended, balance sheets drawn — as Part Two quantifies above. For the market to tolerate more, revenue coverage must compress from 5–6x toward 2–2.5x. The scenarios:

| Scenario                           |        2027 |        2028 |        2029 |               2030 → 2031 | Logic                                                                                                                                                                                                     |
| ---------------------------------- | ----------: | ----------: | ----------: | ------------------------: | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Base — digestion**               | $1.00–1.10T | $1.10–1.25T | $1.05–1.20T | $1.20–1.35T → $1.30–1.50T | D&A and financing discipline stop the lab-serving and self-build tranches; cash-funded tranches continue; depth supports re-acceleration                                                                  |
| **Bull — the race to the ceiling** | $1.20–1.30T | $1.50–1.70T | $1.80–2.00T |  $2.00–2.20T → ~$2.0–2.2T | Every tranche races to the financing limit: distributions suspended, platforms levered to 2.0–2.5x, fragile tranches refinanced; power binds before financing and the $5.6–7.0T stack exhausts by 2030–31 |

The base path reaches $1.30–1.50 trillion by 2031, with $5.5–6.5 trillion spent cumulatively from 2027 — a growth pause in 2028–29, not a collapse. The race path spends more in four years than the base does in five, and exhausts the financing stack by 2030–31. Either way, the pause — when it comes — coexists with rising usage. That divergence between units and dollars is the signature of the whole cycle: accelerator-hours, tokens and agent actions keep compounding while the dollars stall, because chips improve, models get distilled, and competition passes the efficiency gains on to customers. AI hardware revenue can still grow from $450–500 billion in 2026 to $650–750 billion in 2028 before flattening.

The severity question is where equity outcomes get decided, and the highest-information window has a date: hyperscaler guidance for calendar 2027, arriving January–February 2027. The first sub-20% capex guide from Microsoft, Alphabet or Meta confirms digestion on schedule. Uniform 30%-plus guides mean the race is being run to the ceiling. A credit-spread shock pulls either path forward a year. In the scheduled digestion, the repricing concentrates where it belongs: leveraged merchant capacity, single-customer exposure, short-term funding against fast-obsolescing hardware, politically reversible sovereign projects. In the race path, the same correction arrives later, deeper and all at once — a pullback landing on a levered complex cuts estimates everywhere, including at businesses whose earnings barely flinch. The two paths differ in date and depth, not destination — and the depth is what sets the entry.

Digestion is when classification error peaks: the market has always priced every AI-exposed asset owner on the same cyclical template, at exactly the moment their economics diverge. The businesses to own through and out of the trough are the ones where qualification gates, service annuities and contracted volumes hold trough earnings far above prior peaks — leading-edge silicon and fabrication, HBM at the qualified leaders, networking and optics, equipment service streams, power and thermal bottlenecks. The framework for investing in a digestion trough is to set fair value at an industrial-monopoly multiple on slightly-above-trough earnings, and let the digestion set the entry at peak pessimism. A further objective is to capitalise on the second-order bull case that begins after the pause: stranded merchant and lab capacity becomes cheap substrate for workflow, governance and distribution businesses, as dark fiber did after 2000. The builder can lose while the platform it installed compounds.
