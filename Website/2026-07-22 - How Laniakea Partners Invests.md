# How Laniakea Partners Invests

**At Laniakea Partners we invest in long-duration changes that the market can see but is structurally ill-equipped to value.** The work begins before those changes appear cleanly in earnings: a product qualification that closes a supply chain, an architecture shift that moves a bottleneck, a culture that can absorb machine intelligence faster than its peers, or a capital allocator quietly building the next earnings engine. Financial statements eventually record these developments, but rarely explain them soon enough to create an edge.

We are thus a qualitative, evidence-led investor rather than a conventional forecast-led one; ‘qualitative’ means treating technology, product quality, organisational design, competitive structure, pricing power and investor incentives as causal variables, then demanding observable evidence and a falsifier for every claim, not impressionism. Numbers discipline the conclusion without substituting for understanding the mechanism.

## The edge is a different horizon

Most professional capital competes over the next quarter, earnings revision or six-month catalyst, a horizon that matches the industry’s business model: clients judge recent performance, capital follows recent winners, risk committees shorten patience after drawdowns, and portfolio managers defend their jobs with positions that can be explained through the same estimates as everyone else. More information and faster models have made this arena more efficient without making investors more farsighted.

We move the research question out by three to five years and sometimes longer, not to predict a distant earnings number to two decimal places but to determine what an industry is becoming, which economic layer will control it, and which company can reinvest into that position for longer than the price assumes.

At that horizon small facts carry disproportionate value. A supplier’s design win may reveal that a qualification gate has narrowed, a new system architecture may redirect spending from processors to memory, packaging, optics or power, and a change in documentation culture may show that one company can automate workflows that remain trapped in the heads of employees elsewhere. Each fact looks too soft, too early or too remote for a quarterly model; connected into a causal chain, it can describe the earnings structure several years before consensus estimates do.

| Conventional process | Our process |
|---|---|
| Forecast revenue and margins forward | Reverse-engineer the future already embedded in the price |
| Compare a company with its reported peers | Map the economic stack and identify who owns the binding layer |
| Treat culture as commentary | Treat decision architecture and incentives as productive assets or liabilities |
| Extrapolate the latest cycle | Decide first whether mean reversion or trend continuation applies |
| Describe a moat | Name its mechanism, realised pricing evidence and failure condition |
| Add an AI premium to the narrative | Look for governed context, workflow evidence and revenue-per-employee gains |
| Seek committee-safe diversification | Concentrate where durability, upside asymmetry and falsifiability overlap |

## Valuation is an expectations test

Our objection is to false precision, not valuation itself. A conventional discounted cash-flow model can make an idea appear rigorous while hiding the only assumptions that matter inside a long sequence of revenue growth, margin and terminal-value estimates; the model becomes most fragile when the business itself is changing fastest, the exact setting in which a spreadsheet receives the most authority.

We prefer the reverse DCF, which starts with the market price and asks what the business must look like in ten years for that price to be justified. The implied future breaks into operating variables: customer growth, price and mix, retention, margins, capital intensity and the return on each incremental dollar invested. Research concentrates on the one or two variables where evidence can differ from the market’s assumption.

This prevents ‘cheap’ and ‘expensive’ from becoming substitutes for analysis. A high multiple can understate value if the market discounts a moat for five years that can persist for fifteen; a low multiple can overstate value if current earnings are the product of inventory hoarding, temporary scarcity or underinvestment by competitors. The question is thus never whether a multiple looks high against history but which future the price already contains, why that future may be wrong, and what evidence will resolve the gap.

## Start with the system, not the ticker

A company is one component in a changing economic system, and its results emerge from customers, suppliers, technical standards, credit conditions, regulation, capital spending and competitors reacting to one another. As such we start with the industry map: where value enters, where it accumulates, which constraint is binding, how competitors are likely to respond, and what could move the constraint somewhere else.

Semiconductors make the method visible. What investors call ‘the semiconductor cycle’ is at least three clocks: the economic clock changes consumer and enterprise demand; the capital-and-inventory clock turns shortages into hoarding, factory construction and eventual oversupply; and the technology clock changes what useful capacity means and relocates scarcity across fabrication, memory, packaging, interconnect and power. A bullish demand forecast can still produce a poor investment if customers have double-ordered, capacity is arriving and the bottleneck is about to migrate; falling units can still mark an attractive entry if prices stop deteriorating, inventories clear and production cuts finally constrain supply.

The same reasoning travels across sectors. We look for the sequence between an optically minor datapoint and a macro-level change, and specify the transmission mechanism rather than a grand story around scattered facts: what changes first, who must react, which constraint tightens, where bargaining power moves, when the financial statements should show it, and which observation would prove the chain wrong.

## Own the toll road, not the traffic

Our preferred unit of analysis is often an economic layer rather than a legal company. Modern industries are stacks: one firm may own the standard, interface, network, manufacturing process, dataset or qualification gate that every participant above it must traverse; another may look innovative while renting the decisive layer from somebody else. The first can collect structural rent; the second’s margin remains a policy decision made by its supplier.

We call the strongest version a ‘value-layer monopoly’: a company controlling one critical function through some combination of switching costs, network effects, proprietary learning loops, capital scale, standard ownership, regulatory fixed costs or technical qualification. Market share alone does not establish the case; the evidence must appear in customer behaviour and realised economics: pricing, take rates, retention, gross margins or a rising profit floor across cycles.

The lens also supplies its own disqualifiers: a diversified operator with no decisive layer may be complexity masquerading as resilience; an application built on another company’s model, store or compute can be a layer-renter masquerading as an owner; open standards can collapse switching costs; a focused entrant can exploit an incumbent whose advantage depends on continuously out-innovating smaller teams; and political power can become the ceiling on economic power through antitrust, forced interoperability, taxation or geopolitical duplication.

A monopoly becomes an investment only when it is mispriced: if every investor already recognises the layer and discounts its durability, the company may be exceptional but the stock may offer no edge. We search for emerging layer owners, businesses whose structural position is becoming decisive while the price still treats them as one contestable vendor among many, and the research must name both the layer and the specific expectation the market has wrong.

## Organisations are productive systems

Consensus analysis often treats two companies with similar products and financial ratios as close substitutes. We treat the corporation as a cybernetic organism (a system that senses information, makes decisions, allocates resources and learns), and culture is the operating architecture of that system: incentives determine which information travels, hierarchy determines how much degrades, and capital allocation determines whether today’s advantage becomes tomorrow’s compounding engine.

This view changes how management quality is assessed: charisma carries little weight, and the relevant questions are whether decisions follow evidence rather than status, whether bad news can move upward intact, whether decision rights are clear, whether management can cannibalise an existing product, and whether capital goes to the highest incremental return rather than the most politically protected division. The core economic variable is forward return on incremental invested capital multiplied by the runway over which capital can be deployed; current return on capital describes the existing mines, while strategy and culture determine the pipeline of new mines.

Accounting can obscure this distinction: research, software, customer acquisition and brand building are expensed even when they create multi-year assets, and aggregate revenue can hide whether growth comes from durable cohorts or ever more expensive acquisition. We adjust the lens accordingly, capitalising investment-like intangible spending where warranted, separating retention from acquisition, and asking whether the next dollar invested strengthens or dilutes the franchise.

## AI readiness is an organisational question

The same organisational lens shapes our view of artificial intelligence. Access to capable models will diffuse; proprietary decision context will not. Enterprise systems record what happened (a claim was paid, a discount was approved, a supplier was selected) but often discard why it happened, which evidence mattered, which policy applied and who overrode the default. An agent grounded only in the final state must guess at the missing logic, and can automate generic practice, bad precedent or both.

The companies best placed to compound AI gains produce current, governed, machine-readable context as a by-product of normal work: they write decisions down, codify authority, maintain consistent definitions and capture human corrections. The evidence is operational: named workflows, shorter cycle times, rising revenue per employee, flat headcount alongside growth, or a product embedded in the execution path where proprietary decision traces accumulate.

The lens is a filter. Physical, bespoke, regulated and relationship-heavy work imposes real limits; fragmented data and tribal knowledge can turn automation into scaled error; and a software wrapper without proprietary context or distribution can disappear when the underlying model improves. ‘AI’ in a presentation counts for nothing without a mechanism and a result; as such the lens raises or lowers conviction and never carries a position alone.

## The hardest call is mean reversion versus continuation

Investors are trained to expect excess returns and margins to fade, and the base rate supports that instinct: few companies sustain exceptional growth or returns for long. Yet applying mean reversion mechanically to an increasing-returns network, a qualification-gated supplier or a superior capital allocator can destroy more value than paying a high starting multiple, and the opposite error, calling a commodity producer a compounder at the top of its cycle, is equally costly.

We make classification explicit before valuation. Is the business a structural compounder, a true cyclical, or a hybrid whose trough is rising? Does new supply restore competition, or do qualification, standards and switching costs prevent it? Is the current adoption curve pre-chasm, in broad deployment or near saturation? Is the observed growth a durable shift in the system or inventory borrowed from the future?

The outside view gets the first word: historical growth persistence, margin decay, competitive entry and prior capital cycles establish the burden of proof, and company-specific evidence must then earn the exception with a mechanism and confirming observables rather than a coherent narrative.

## Every thesis is an adversarial contract

Our research structure is designed to prevent a differentiated view from becoming an identity. Every non-consensus insight has four fields: the consensus embedded in the price, the variant view, the first observable that should confirm it, and the falsifier. A thesis also carries outstanding questions, a bull case, a bear case, catalysts, risks and pre-defined conditions for raising, lowering or closing conviction.

New information enters through a similarly strict filter: what changed for the thesis, what is the evidence, and which existing assumption does it support or contradict? This turns research from an archive of interesting facts into a versioned decision system in which a datapoint earns attention through its effect on a causal claim rather than novelty.

Mental models serve the same discipline: lenses and questions rather than answers, raising the analytical floor by testing the value layer, mapping the capital cycle, inspecting organisational readiness, and comparing the inside view with the reference class. When several models produce the same attractive conclusion, we treat agreement as a warning to search harder for the bear case and the single falsifying datapoint; a house style becomes dangerous the moment every company starts looking like the house’s favourite kind of company.

## Concentration follows asymmetry, not conviction theatre

Public-market returns follow a power law: a small number of companies create a disproportionate share of long-run wealth. Excessive diversification can guarantee ownership of the long tail while shrinking the few outcomes that matter; deep losses also require disproportionately large gains to repair, and portfolio construction must respect both facts.

We resolve the tension through a barbell logic: a resilient core of businesses with durable economics and long reinvestment runways, combined with smaller positions whose downside is bounded but whose upside is nonlinear. Concentration is earned by evidence quality, moat durability, valuation asymmetry and the clarity of the falsifier; uncertainty reduces size even when the potential return is large, and a compelling narrative without a monitorable failure condition does not deserve concentration.

## The research organisation is part of the investment edge

Traditional asset management passes primary evidence through analysts, sector heads, portfolio managers and a chief investment officer, and each handoff compresses information. Career incentives encourage the safe conclusion, quantitative templates become a common language, and the person closest to the evidence often has the least authority; capital then arrives after good performance and leaves after drawdowns, pushing the institution toward aggression when prices are high and caution when opportunity is greatest.

Our research architecture is built against those failure modes: source evidence, industry maps, thesis logic, counterarguments and falsifiers live in one structured, versioned context graph, and AI agents can retrieve, compare, challenge and update that context without forcing the idea through layers of managerial translation. The system increases research throughput, but its main advantage is preserving the chain from evidence to decision while making contradiction searchable.

Technology does not remove accountability: skin in the game, explicit conviction triggers and an audit trail of what changed perform the role that committees claim to perform. The aim is to give judgement more complete memory, faster contradiction and less incentive distortion, not to automate it.

## Non-consensus is a result, not a pose

We do not begin with the assumption that consensus is wrong: most variant views fail, high growth usually decays, attractive margins invite a response, and a famous monopoly can be fully priced. Disagreement creates no return by itself; the opportunity exists only when the market’s horizon, institutional incentives or analytical categories cause it to misread a specific variable.

That variable may be the durability of a bottleneck, the direction of a technology transition, the quality of a reinvestment runway, the hidden asset inside an expense line, the speed at which culture converts AI into operating leverage, or the point where a cyclical business becomes structurally less cyclical. The thesis must say which one, how the mechanism works, when evidence should arrive and what would make the view wrong.
