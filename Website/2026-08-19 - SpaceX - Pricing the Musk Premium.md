# SpaceX: Pricing the long horizon perspective

SpaceX listed on June 12 at roughly $135. Two months later the stock sits at $140 — about $1.9 trillion across both share classes — having traded $104.83 on its first capital-expenditure print and $225.64 at the peak of the 10-gigawatt narrative, a 115% range inside a single summer. Sell-side price targets run from $62 to $800, the widest dispersion on any large-cap.

When a $1.9 trillion company's analysts disagree by 13x, the disagreement is not about execution. It is about identity. To make sense of this dispersion, we present a two-part sum-of-the-parts framework. The **bull mark** is the promoter's case — each slice valued on the outcome its believers describe. The **base mark** is ours — each slice valued on what is printed, contracted, and cash-generating today, at multiples peers actually command. The gap between the columns is the number we are after: how much of $140 is business, and how much is faith.

SpaceX reports three segments; economically it is two capital pools with Launch and Connectivity sharing the same spine.

| Segment (Q2 2026) | Revenue | Operating income | Adj. EBITDA | Capex |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Connectivity (Starlink + Starshield) | $4,291M | $1,656M | $2,597M (60.5%) | $1,367M |
| AI (Grok, X, cloud compute) | $2,561M | $(1,257)M | $1,146M | $15,828M |
| Space (launch) | $962M | $(542)M | $(205)M | $1,174M |
| **Total** | **$7,814M** | **$(143)M** | **$3,538M** | **$18,369M** |

Falcon is an owned railroad; Starlink is the toll road built along its tracks; the AI campus is a landlord who erected the building but leases the rolling stock from Nvidia.

## xAI / Grok 

Compute is the scarce input in the model race, and SpaceX controls more energized gigawatts than any rival lab. Grok Bot, launched this month, is an early entry in digital-labor agents and has a high chance at becoming a 'Claude Code' moment for the AI space. Cursor brings an enterprise coding franchise; X supplies consumer distribution only Google and Meta can match.

The recent releases of the Grok model (4.6) already indicate capabilities just short of Anthropic and OpenAI at the frontier. With Google Gemini effectively dropping out and Meta still struggling. Grok has the best shot at catching up to OpenAI and Anthropic at the frontier of AI development. 

Grok 4.6 presently offers near parity performance with Fable-5 and GPT 5.6 Sol at a fraction of the price per token, which should support material migration of the user base from Claude Code to Grok Build.

## xAI / Neocloud

For SpaceXAI's neocloud business, identifiable power sources through 2027 sum to about 5.5–6 GW of nameplate — 40% short of Elon's 10 GW by YE2027 aspirational target. This is before a single additional GPU is racked. Hitting 10 GW of *energized compute* requires three gates to open at once: a build rate of ~667 MW a month against a realised 100–130 MW; something like four million GB300-class GPUs, 40–50% of Nvidia’s global Blackwell output, to one customer; and $320–440 billion of capital expenditure. No organisation has energised more than 3 GW of IT load in a year. Hyperscalers add 2–4 GW a year globally, with mature chains. SpaceX holds the record and still has to beat its own record by a factor of four, for twelve consecutive months.

SpaceX is perhaps the strongest neocloud today and the biggest contender to becoming the next AWS, GCP, or Azure. The execution record backs it: 100,000 GPUs energized in 122 days, the first gigawatt-scale campus built in six months, a turbine complex scaled from 495MW to 1.7GW in five months while peers queue eight years for grid interconnection, and preferential Nvidia allocation — "a very significant percent" of next year's GPUs — secured through a Rubin-generation exclusivity no other buyer has. No one else has built at this speed, and power-and-permitting is the bottleneck that stalls everyone else. The three variables consensus does not understand about SpaceX's neocloud business:

1) preferential access to Nvidia GPUs, owing to Musk's commitment to single-sourcing from Nvidia and to their plans to co-develop orbital compute;
2) preferential access to power, turbines, and generators, on vendor perception that SpaceX is a credible counterparty and one worth currying favour with to participate in future Musk industrial complex supply chains;
3) preferential access to sites and permitting, owing to the favourable political treatment the Musk industrial complex has with regulatory bodies. The most important variable for a property (datacenter) developer is regulatory relations (or capture), and on this front, SpaceX far exceeds all other competitors.

This combination of variables is what makes a datacenter developer valuable in the case of a shortage. SpaceX's capacity to navigate the structural shortage in delivering powered datacenter is effectively its competitive moat and right to win.

**Base — $150 billion.** This scenario assumes SpaceX is overearning today on short-term contracts, and that AI demand does not support the current paradigm of extreme shortage. ~2x valuation is conservative against smaller-scale, less-advantaged competitor Nebius's valuation at ~$70bn. Orbital compute is not priced in this scenario.

**Bull — $700 billion.** The bull case is that SpaceX has the highest demonstrated capacity to stand up campuses at speed and to route around permitting and land. Musk's target is 10 GW of terrestrial compute by December 2027. At $30–50 per watt that is $300–500 billion of revenue, and $700 billion of equity if the paper lasts longer than the chips and turbines depreciate — assuming the current AI shortage does not end any time soon, and against a ~$1.5–3T valuation range for the big three CSPs. $700bn NPV does not seem ludicrous to assign to SpaceX's compute business.

Orbital compute extends as a 2030s-and-onwards play, only in the instance of a structural compute shortage caused by terrestrial permitting bottlenecks — an opportunity that is uniquely SpaceX's to capture: launch, satellite, solar and laser backhaul, owned end to end. If cost parity ever arrives, nobody else can follow.

**75%** of the $700 billion bull case valuation is attributed to **traditional neocloud** and **25%** is attributable to **orbital compute.**
## Launch

Strip out the flights SpaceX performs for its own constellation — 28 of 38 in Q2, generating no revenue — and external launch is $962 million a quarter: $648 million of customer missions at a ~$65 million average price plus $314 million of development contracts. The economics are excellent (a reused Falcon flies at $15–28 million marginal cost against that price; roughly $2 billion of annual EBITDA before Starship R&D consumes it) but the entire third-party launch market is $15–20 billion a year and SpaceX already carries 80–90% of its mass. A dominant share of a small market, at 18–22x: $35–45 billion. Do not credit "shadow revenue" for the internal flights — that surplus already sits inside Starlink's 60.5% margin, and counting it twice inflates the sum. Launch is the railroad: monopolistic and small. Starship's expansion of the manifest belongs to the optionality buckets below, not here.

**Base & Bull — $40 billion.** Mature business for SpaceX and fully penetrated relative to TAM. Future non-satellite and interstellar launch businesses captured in future sections, this is the traditional launch business as visible today.

## Starlink

Starlink is three businesses wearing one dish: a printed broadband-and-government annuity, an under-penetrated mobility franchise in the air and at sea, and a mobile option. The first two are nearly identical in both columns — the entire base-versus-bull delta is mobile, so that is where the argument spends its time.

The annuity first, because it anchors everything. Twelve million subscribers, $66 blended ARPU, $4.29 billion of quarterly revenue, $10.4 billion of annualised adjusted EBITDA at a 60.5% margin. Consumer is $2.49 billion of the quarter; enterprise and government are $1.81 billion — 42% of the mix — with more than $6 billion of multi-year Starshield awards in a single print and a management claim of zero enterprise churn to date. Twenty-two times current EBITDA is $230 billion, a utility-compounder multiple earned by owned launch and software-like incremental cost, not a legacy-satellite one. The two live risks: ARPU has slid from $99 to $66 on international and lower-tier mix, and the June $5–10 price rise is the first real retention test just as Amazon Leo reaches commercial service with Verizon, AT&T and Vodafone already in its enterprise beta.

Marine and aviation are live, under-penetrated, and bounded. Gwynne Shotwell said on the August call that aviation is still less than 10% penetrated. The global commercial fleet is 25–28 thousand aircraft; 10% is about 2,500 tails. United is heading for 1,000 by year-end, American past 500, plus Hawaiian, JSX, Frontier and the business-jet book. The list just doubled: Global Unlimited is $20,000 a month, Regional Unlimited $12,500, Regional 25GB $4,000. In-flight connectivity is a $3–6 billion market. Starlink does not enlarge it by an order of magnitude. It takes it, at higher speed, and raises price. A 39% commercial share by 2034 — Valour’s number, about 7,000 airliners plus 3,000 business jets — is $1.5–2.0 billion of revenue. Ceiling, including business aviation: about $3–4 billion of revenue and $2.5 billion of EBITDA.

Maritime satellite communications is a $7–8 billion market. Starlink had more than 4,000 commercial vessels by early 2026 and was already a quarter of satellite-connected ships in 2024. Fifty to seventy percent of that TAM is $4–6 billion. Marine plus air at maturity: $7–9 billion of revenue, about $5 billion of EBITDA, of which only about $1 billion is in the current mix. Capitalise the increment at 16 times and the under-penetration is worth $70 billion. The terminals exist. The question is attach, not invention.

Mobile is the delta. The annuity, marine and air move the two columns by almost nothing; mobile is $80 billion in the base and $200–300 billion in the bull, and the spread turns on six variables — each with a put the bull is paying for and a take the base refuses to pay for:

| Variable | The put (what the bull pays for) | The take (what the base concedes) |
|---|---|---|
| Spectrum | EchoStar's 65 MHz, owned and contiguous — 13× the sliver the current service uses; the only owned-spectrum LEO position of scale | US licenses only — global direct-to-cell is country-by-country regulatory work; the deal itself closes November 2027 |
| Handsets | Apple ships 2 GHz satellite silicon in the iPhone 18 → hundreds of millions of capable devices at a stroke | Talks unclosed since January; Apple runs Emergency SOS on Globalstar, Amazon has bid $11.6 billion for Globalstar, and Apple gains leverage by keeping two suppliers alive |
| Network build | 12 million installed dishes double as femtocells on EchoStar's terrestrial rights — a tower grid at near-zero tower capex | The dedicated mobile satellite fleet is real capex that first flies in 2027; higher-power waivers are pending; "100× better" is an end-2027 service claim |
| Capacity physics | V3 satellites at 1 Tbps each, launched weekly, relieve the constraint every year | A satellite cell serves a county-sized area with the capacity a terrestrial carrier deploys per city block — a coverage product, never urban substitution |
| Distribution | Carriers pay per-SIM wholesale as churn insurance — T-Mobile already bundles the service free into premium plans; revenue arrives at near-pure margin | Going direct burns the carrier channel that built the book; Verizon and AT&T are already hedged through AST SpaceMobile partnerships |
| Demand | 150–300 million SIMs of dead-zone, maritime, IoT and government coverage at $3–6 wholesale is $8–14 billion of revenue | Dead zones shrink as carriers extend rural mid-band; not one direct-to-cell subscriber or revenue dollar has been disclosed in a filing — the whole line is pre-print |

Mobile in the base case is wholesale fill-in, not a fourth carrier; the bull is that plus the direct stack. The arithmetic behind both marks:

**$80 billion — the wholesale-only build.**

| Step | Value | Anchor |
|---|---|---|
| Partnered-carrier SIM base | ~1.5–2 billion | T-Mobile US plus the announced international carrier roster |
| Attach rate | 10–15% → **150–300M SIMs** | Attach is a carrier decision, not a consumer one — T-Mobile already bundles T-Satellite free into premium tiers as churn defense |
| Wholesale ARPU | **$3–6/month** | T-Satellite retails at $10 standalone; bundled wholesale clears at 30–60% of retail. Cross-check: Apple pays Globalstar ~$1–1.5B a year for an emergency-only tier across ~400M capable iPhones — under $0.30 per device-month; a voice-and-data tier is worth an order of magnitude more |
| Revenue | **$8–14B** | 150M × $4.50 × 12 at the low end |
| EBITDA | $5.5–10B at ~70% | The capacity already flies; the carrier owns the customer, the CAC, the billing and the support |
| Value | **$80B** | ~14× EBITDA at the low end of the $80–140B range, held down because the book matures 2028–30 and not one subscriber is yet printed |

**$250 billion — the full-stack build.**

| Layer | Build | Revenue |
|---|---|---:|
| Wholesale, matured | 250M+ SIMs at $4–5 | $10–14B |
| Direct retail on EchoStar bands | Apple ships the radio → 30–60M subs (rural primary, vehicles, redundancy) at $15–25/month | $7–18B |
| Femtocell / home-zone MVNO | 10–20M mobile lines bundled onto the dish base at ~$15 | $2–4B |
| Machines and government | 50–100M vehicles and devices at $1–2, plus Starshield mobile | $2–4B |
| **Total, 2030–32** | | **~$28B mid** |

At 60–65% margins that is $17–18 billion of EBITDA; $250 billion is 14–15× — a growth multiple against T-Mobile's ~8× on $33 billion of EBITDA, and the honest stretch in the number. The $200–300 billion band is this same stack with the direct layer at its floor (Apple slips a cycle) or its ceiling (Apple and Android both ship at the iPhone 18 launch).

One framing error to avoid: the TAM is not rural. Rural is where the traffic flows, not who pays. The largest slice is coverage insurance bought by urban premium subscribers who leave coverage twice a year — which is why attach can reach hundreds of millions of SIMs when the rural population alone never could. Machines are bought by fleet operators; resilience is bought by governments — networks fail in hurricanes where people are. What the TAM permanently excludes, by physics, is urban capacity: 80%-plus of the $1.1 trillion global mobile industry. Satellite direct-to-cell is a $20–35 billion-a-year coverage layer by the early 2030s, and $250 billion prices the leader's share of it at a growth multiple. The trillion-dollar fourth-carrier outcome requires the capacity TAM, and the physics forecloses it. 

**Base — $330 billion.** $230 billion of printed annuity, $70 billion of marine-and-air under-penetration, $80 billion of wholesale mobile, less a $50 billion haircut for Leo and the ARPU test. Nothing in the number requires Apple, owned-spectrum economics, or a single direct subscriber. 

**Bull — $500 billion.** The upgrade is mobile: the EchoStar bands close in November 2027 and thirteen-fold the usable spectrum; the dish-as-tower femtocell build converts a $100 billion carrier plant into a marginal-cost overlay; and an Apple attach puts the radio in hundreds of millions of pockets at launch. $230 billion of annuity, plus $70 billion of marine and air, plus $250 billion of mobile, less the $50 billion haircut, is $500 billion. A coverage-and-backhaul layer with owned spectrum, not Verizon. The full fourth-carrier outcome ($400 billion-plus for mobile alone) requires the plant management explicitly declined to build; it is excluded.

## Terafab

The bull case is that Terafab becomes a new memory producer and a second alternative manufacturer — an onshore, off-grid source of the HBM and logic the campus and the orbital fleet would otherwise rent. That is a successful-IDM outcome, not a site. It needs a node, an anchor, and tools. The increment versus base is $180 billion, and it is a semiconductor underwriting, not a SpaceX one. However, Grimes County is a $16.8 billion initial filing, up to $119 billion across four phases, with an off-grid claim that still reads as tax-abatement language rather than proven islanding at that scale. There is no named process node, no anchor customer, and no tool purchase order. 

Rapidus (Japan's sovereign semi initiative) has achieved feasibility on the basis of IBM's legacy 2nm process technology. China, without access to advanced western equipment, was able to develop a domestic manufacturing supply chain on the back of meaningful subsidies within 10 years. A formidable engineering organisation with the recruitment credibility supported by lenient capital markets will undoubtedly allow SpaceX to replicate and potentially surpass these achievements. 

Terafab will likely become the incremental second source supplier and solution to the semiconductor market to incumbent semiconductor vendors that refuse to scale up and prefer to 'farm the bottleneck' instead. SpaceX will likely partner, acquire, or license process IP from tier 2 fabrication houses like Intel, Samsung, GlobalFoundries in compute, and potentially companies like Nanya in DRAM. 

A structural semiconductor shortage and sky-high prices provide the breathing room for a start-up semiconductor manufacturing operation to scale. However, should demand falter, Terafab is likely to either be shelved or face structural mounting losses.

**Base — $20 billion.**  This scenario models a sub-scale and temporarily profitable swing producer that makes money in upcycles but is loss making or non productive in downcycles.

**Bull — $200 billion.** At 1/5th Micron and 1/10th TSMC, the optionality for SpaceX is fairly valued.

## Future optionality, realistic horizon

We book Mars architecture, point-to-point transport, robotics adjacency, lunar contracts beyond booked milestones — at precisely zero. These are two large numbers cancelling. Earth-adjacent option value on one side; on the other, a Starship program burning north of $4 billion a year to serve ambitions that generate no revenue this decade. Mark the line positive and you must first subtract the burn; mark it negative and you ignore the tangible revenue potential the burn is supporting.

**Base & Bull — $0.**

## Future optionality, multi-generational 

The claim: this equity is a ticket to the East India Company in 1700 — a fractional deed to whatever a spacefaring economy becomes. Infinity is not a number a sum of parts can carry, but it can be priced by inversion: value every finite component, subtract the total from the market price, and whatever remains is what the market charges for the infinite. 

Two notes from the reference class first. The East India Company's shareholders collected dividends for a century, then the enterprise became too strategically important to leave in private hands and was nationalized. Most colonial charters fared worse: the Virginia Company — the actual ticket-to-the-Americas trade — went bankrupt and lost its charter in 1624, and the continent's value accrued to people who never held its paper. A company operating the West's launch monopoly, its battlefield connectivity layer, and a frontier AI lab is the modern candidate for that arc — and under the Outer Space Treaty, the property rights the multi-generational dream assumes do not yet legally exist. Tickets like this should be acquired for free, or nearly so.

**Bull — $∞**

## The final table

The multi-generational component is priced as the residual the market is charging at $140:

| Component ($B) | Base | Bull |
|---|---:|---:|
| xAI / Grok | 300 | 1,000 |
| xAI / Neocloud | 150 | 700 |
| Launch (ex-internal Starlink flights) | 40 | 40 |
| Starlink (fixed + marine/air + mobile) | 330 | 500 |
| Terafab | 20 | 200 |
| Future optionality — realistic horizon | 0 | 0 |
| Net cash, less remaining spectrum payment | 46 | 46 |
| Governance / conglomerate discount | (80) | — |
| **Sum of the finite components** | **~$0.81T (~$61/sh)** | **~$2.49T (~$189/sh)** |
| Market value at $140 | ~$1.90T | ~$1.90T |
| **Multi-generational optionality, as priced by the market** | **+$1.09T — $83/sh, 58% of the price** | **−$0.59T — less than free** |

One of the last row's two readings must be true.

On base marks, the market is charging $1.09 trillion for the East India ticket — 58 cents of every dollar in the price, a bigger line than Starlink, launch, and the cash combined, for a component whose legal title does not exist and whose reference class ends in nationalization. 

On bull marks, the ticket comes essentially free — but only after paying every other belief at face value: the frontier catch-up, the ten gigawatts, the contract duration, the fab, the Apple deal — years before the filings can confirm any of it. 

**SpaceX thus will continue to trade as a sentiment stock where the closing figure to the valuation is the embedded optimism humanity has towards future space colonisation.** The proportion that the market is willing to pay for this future optionality thus becomes the object of speculation, and right now, on a bullish lens, this optionality comes at meaningfully below zero as a price point.
