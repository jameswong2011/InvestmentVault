#### English Script
Over the next few weeks, I'm breaking down one of the most consequential companies of our era — Nvidia. This is video one.

Here's the strangest fact in markets right now: the most valuable company in the world, the company at the center of the AI boom, trades at the same earnings multiple as an average American business. Forward P/E means the share price divided by next year's expected profit. Nvidia's is about twenty-one. Two years ago it was above forty-five.

A multiple doesn't fall like that because a business is failing. Nvidia's revenue grew sixty-five percent last fiscal year, and the latest quarter grew even faster. The multiple fell because profits grew faster than the share price — the stock rose modestly while earnings roughly doubled. Mathematically, that's the market refusing to pay up for each new dollar of profit.

Why refuse? Because a low multiple on a great business is a forecast. The market is pricing one of two futures: either Nvidia's biggest customers — who are all designing their own chips — take serious market share, or the AI spending boom itself breaks and today's profits turn out to be a peak, not a plateau. Cheapness is only an opportunity if both of those are wrong.

And here's the counterweight: chip companies often look cheapest right at the top of their cycle, because peak earnings flatter the ratio. That's the classic value trap.

So the real question isn't whether Nvidia is cheap. It's whether these earnings are durable — and that's what this series tests, front to back.

#### 中文脚本（普通话，简体）
接下来几周，我会拆解这个时代最重要的公司之一——英伟达。这是第一期。

当前市场最反常的事实：全球市值第一、站在AI浪潮中心的公司，估值倍数却只相当于一家普通的美国大企业。预期市盈率，就是股价除以未来一年的预期利润。英伟达现在约二十一倍，两年前超过四十五倍。

#### English Script
This is video ten in my series on Nvidia.

Nvidia does something almost no dominant supplier does: it invests billions into the companies that buy its chips. A hundred-million-dollar stake in CoreWeave in twenty-twenty-three has grown into thirteen percent of the company; add stakes in Nebius, Nscale, Lambda, and headline programs around OpenAI and Anthropic, and the equity book tops forty billion dollars.

Why? Game theory. In the great GPU shortage, Nvidia deliberately allocated scarce chips beyond the big three clouds — because if all AI compute lived inside Amazon, Microsoft, and Google, those three would own the customer relationship *and* they're the ones building replacement chips. Nvidia would be squeezed from both sides. The neoclouds — cloud providers that rent almost nothing but GPUs — keep the rental layer fragmented, keep pricing power upstream, and will never design their own silicon. An Nvidia investment doubles as an allocation credential: it decides who gets chips, which decides who exists.

The counter-moves came. Every hyperscaler accelerated its in-house chip program. And this month Meta started renting out its own surplus GPUs — a tech giant entering the neocloud business directly. CoreWeave's stock fell fourteen percent on that single announcement.

Now the red flag, stated plainly: this is vendor financing. Equity goes out, comes back as chip orders, books as revenue, inflates backlogs, and the stakes appreciate on the boom they're funding. Telecom ran the same loop in nineteen-ninety-nine, and it ended in write-downs. CoreWeave carries forty-six billion in liabilities on three billion of equity. The mitigant is real — contracts are almost entirely non-cancellable with mostly deep-pocketed counterparties — but it holds only while those counterparties do.

Funding your own demand is brilliant in a real boom and an accelerant in a false one. It makes the cycle's eventual verdict binary — which is exactly why this structure, more than any chip benchmark, deserves your attention.

#### English Script
Part twelve of the Nvidia breakdown.

This month Nvidia did two things that sound contradictory: it launched a new business model around software — and made its flagship software free.

The software is Omniverse: a platform for building physics-accurate digital twins — working virtual copies of factories, warehouses, and robots, simulated before anything is built in steel. It runs on OpenUSD, an open file standard for 3D worlds that became an independent industry standard last December. BMW runs it across thirty-one factories; General Motors just adopted it.

Now the honest tension. The segment where Omniverse revenue sits produced about three billion dollars last year — under two percent of Nvidia's total. And giving software away is the opposite of a software business model.

The resolution is that Nvidia monetizes software sideways. Free software makes the hardware indispensable and premium-priced — that's the existing engine. The new move, called DSX, goes further: Nvidia provides the blueprint, simulation, and operating software for entire AI data centers, and takes revenue-sharing and credit-support positions in the facilities themselves. Less a parts vendor, more a franchisor. Add licensing — it paid twenty billion dollars to license a rival's inference technology, proving the model runs both directions — and a software-like revenue layer becomes visible, targeting an industrial-software market estimated near six hundred billion dollars.

Today, the honest answer is: Nvidia is a hardware company whose free software prices the hardware. The software second act is a real option the market values at roughly nothing — and it pays off only if factories, not just chatbots, start paying rent on intelligence.
