#### English Script

Welcome back — this is video two in my series on SK hynix.

A top AI processor can cost as much as a car, and much of the time it sits idle — waiting for data to arrive. That waiting problem created one of the most valuable products in computing.

AI models work by moving enormous amounts of data — the model's parameters and everything it's processing — through the processor, over and over. Speed of movement, called bandwidth, becomes the ceiling. Past that ceiling, a faster processor adds nothing; it just waits faster.

High-bandwidth memory attacks the ceiling physically. Instead of placing memory chips across the circuit board, HBM stacks layers of DRAM — working memory — vertically, connects them with thousands of microscopic channels through the silicon, and sits directly beside the processor. Think of a warehouse: instead of one distant loading dock, you build a dozen docks against the factory wall.

The current generation doubles the data connections to 2,048 and improves power efficiency by more than 40 percent. June's next-generation samples reached 16 gigabits per second per pin — roughly four terabytes per second from a single stack. Each new AI platform specifies more memory bandwidth and capacity than the last, which is why demand compounds with every processor generation.

The counterpoint: software keeps getting more efficient, and smaller models need less memory per task. But cheaper computing has historically expanded total usage — when each AI answer costs less, people ask far more questions. Efficiency has grown the market before; it isn't guaranteed to next time.

The investment logic stands on physics: as long as AI performance is limited by data movement, the company that moves data fastest owns the bottleneck — and bottlenecks are where pricing power lives.

#### English Script

Hello again — part three of the SK hynix breakdown.

SK hynix earned a 72 percent operating margin last quarter. To judge whether that can last, you need to see that it isn't one business — it's three, sharing the same factories.

First, conventional DRAM: standard working memory for servers, phones, and PCs. Essential, but chips from different suppliers are largely interchangeable, so prices swing hard with supply and demand. This is the classically cyclical part.

Second, high-bandwidth memory. HBM starts as DRAM but adds vertical stacking, advanced assembly, and customer qualification — months of testing to prove the memory works flawlessly beside a specific processor. A qualified supplier can't be swapped out for a cheaper quote. That protection is why HBM carries the richest margins, and why roughly 70 percent of company revenue sits on the DRAM side where HBM lives.

Third, NAND storage — including Solidigm, the enterprise-storage business acquired from Intel, selling high-capacity drives to data centres.

The shared engine is the fab: a factory costing tens of billions that runs around the clock whether demand is strong or weak. Costs are mostly fixed, so when prices rise, each extra wafer is nearly pure profit. That's operating leverage, and it's how a memory maker prints a 72 percent margin in a shortage.

The counterpoint is that leverage cuts both ways — in a glut, the same fixed costs crush margins. And profit quality differs by bucket: contract-protected HBM earnings deserve a higher value than commodity earnings that the cycle giveth and taketh.

The bullish read: the profit mix is shifting toward the protected bucket — and mix, not luck, is what changes a company's earnings power.
